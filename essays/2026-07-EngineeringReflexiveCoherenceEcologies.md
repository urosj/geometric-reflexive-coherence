# Engineering Reflexive Coherence Ecologies

## Shared Media, Unfolding, Formation, Scale, Orientation, and Temporal Geometry

**Date:** 2026-07-30<br>
**Status:** Engineering essay; conceptual and experimental orientation

## Abstract

Engineering begins with what can be specified. A participant is given a boundary. A primitive is implemented. An interface is connected. A route carries an event from one region to another. Support is allocated, constraints are declared, and the resulting behavior is tested. Nothing in Reflexive Coherence makes this work secondary or inauthentic. Deliberate arrangement is one of the ways an ecology acquires form.

The engineering object becomes larger when an exchange does not end with the event that produced it. A participant traverses a route and changes the route encountered by what follows. A transfer preserves one basin by redistributing support elsewhere. A failed attempt leaves a trace that alters later eligibility. A boundary that successfully contains one flow accumulates pressure that changes another. The next participant does not encounter the ecology that existed before the first one acted.

At that point, the direct relation remains real, but it is no longer the complete mechanism. Something in the shared condition has carried the consequence forward. The engineering object has expanded from participants and interfaces to the medium whose changed state now shapes later participation.

That expansion changes the meaning of the primitive as well. A trace is not memory simply because it persists. It becomes memory where retained change modifies a later continuation. A route is not guidance merely because it is traversable. It becomes guidance where its history changes which movement is available or costly. A boundary can protect, isolate, filter, accumulate pressure, or become part of another closure. The primitive’s ecological function therefore belongs to the primitive in context.

Once context participates in function, action cannot be treated as the end of the participant’s causal contribution. A contribution changes a medium surface; the changed medium persists, propagates, decays, or composes with other histories; susceptible formations respond; and the resulting ecology returns as part of the condition from which the participant continues. Participation is not complete at emission. It unfolds through ecological return.

Repeated unfolding gives the ecology a history. Routes deepen or weaken, traces accumulate or disappear, support circulates, boundaries become more selective, and characteristic continuations become easier to reproduce. Distinct identities may remain distinct while the shared medium develops stable circulation, division of function, holding capacity, or recurrent co-response. Such a stable plural ecology is a complete ecological organization. It does not require a spark or a higher-order identity to validate it.

Some histories also change the attractor organization. The existing geometry may become unable to contain the differentiation being produced through it; a spark adds a new attractor mode; post-spark dynamics then determine whether a new participant stabilizes, remains scaffolded, merges, or dissolves. Spark-mediated identity formation is therefore one ecological formation pathway among several. Deliberate assembly, medium-mediated organization, retained history, regime stabilization, and attractor reconfiguration are complementary contributions. They can coexist, become conditions for one another, or remain independently sufficient for the ecology being engineered.

The first part of this essay develops that complete one-horizon structural engineering picture. It composes the [Arc of Participation](../arc-of-participation/README.md), the RC interpretation of [agency as persistence capacity](./2026-06-12-AgencyOfBecoming-InterpretationThroughRC.md), and the move from direct exchange toward [shared-medium participation](https://github.com/urosj/reflexive-coherence-agentic-ecology/blob/main/papers/2026-06-TheSharedMedium.md) and its [engineering specification](https://github.com/urosj/reflexive-coherence-agentic-ecology/blob/main/papers/2026-06-SharedMediumCoordination-EngineeringSpec.md). The aim is not to replace those papers, but to show how contextual function, medium-carried relation, identity continuity, unfolding, stable ecology, and formation belong to one engineering cycle.

The second part begins only after that one-horizon ecology has become intelligible. It asks what the whole ecology is relative to another horizon. A participant at one scale may function as medium, constituent, or formation context at another. Local activity may accumulate into wider pressure or support, while wider organization returns as changed local affordance. Similar patterns across scales are not enough: the essay distinguishes scale recurrence, covariance, invariance, and actual inter-scale coupling. Ordinary RC is sufficient to motivate these relations through nested closures; [Fractal Reflexive Coherence](../core/2025-11-FractalReflexiveCoherence.md) makes them especially explicit through scale-resolved coherence, inter-scale flux, and collective geometry.

The third part asks how a wider ecology can orient local continuation without being treated as an external authority or universal judge. It separates constraint from authorization, activity from role, and a designer’s desired property from a quality enacted by the ecology, experienced or valued by participants, or inferred by observers.

The fourth part develops the temporal geometry through which those qualities and earlier participations become causally available. Runtime, event, causal, proper, and semantic orders must remain distinct; pending effects and causal residue can make two visible snapshots different presents. The final Atlas synthesizes all four parts by connecting one-horizon, cross-scale, orientation, and temporal-causal records without turning them into one mandatory developmental path.

The central proposition is:

> **Engineering a Reflexive Coherence ecology means configuring participants, transformation capacities, and shared-medium surfaces so that their contextual functions can be enacted, their consequences can remain available to later participation, and the resulting histories can sustain, transform, compose, release, or form identities across time and scale. A participant is not established merely by the actions it emits, but by the continuity it carries through ecological response. An ecology is not understood only by the stability of its present parts, but by the relations, histories, regimes, and further formation capacities their participation leaves in the shared medium.**

## Formal Status and Scope

This essay is an engineering interpretation grounded in the existing RC formalism and its interpretive and experimental extensions. It does not modify the RC continuity equation, induced geometry, coherence functional, identity definition, spark condition, or FRC scale formalism. It introduces no new primitive state variables and makes no claim that current PDE, voxel, graph, or agentic-ecology substrates already implement every relation described here natively.

Its purpose is compositional. Several existing papers have developed necessary parts of the engineering picture from different directions:

- [The Contextual Geometry of Function](../arc-of-participation/2026-07-ContextualGeometryOfFunction.md) explains why a structure’s ecological function is mediated—and may be constituted—by geometry, history, support, boundary, flux, and scale.
- [The Ecological Formation of Identity](../arc-of-participation/2026-07-EcologicalFormationOfIdentity.md) distinguishes arrangement, nucleation, spark, stabilization, admission, and identity lineage.
- [The Ecology of Participation](../arc-of-participation/2026-07-EcologyOfParticipationInRC.md) follows participation through medium change, ecological response, identity continuation, holding geometry, and revisable orientation.
- [The Scale-Recursive Structure of Participation](../arc-of-participation/2026-07-ScaleRecursiveStructureOfParticipation.md) distinguishes scale recurrence, covariance, invariance, and inter-scale coupling.
- [Agency of Becoming](./2026-06-12-AgencyOfBecoming-InterpretationThroughRC.md) supplies persistence, withdrawal, naturalization, transfer, and proxy diagnostics.
- [The Shared Medium](https://github.com/urosj/reflexive-coherence-agentic-ecology/blob/main/papers/2026-06-TheSharedMedium.md) and [Shared-Medium Coordination](https://github.com/urosj/reflexive-coherence-agentic-ecology/blob/main/papers/2026-06-SharedMediumCoordination-EngineeringSpec.md) supply the engineering vocabulary of medium surfaces, perturbation, trace, pressure, susceptibility, co-response, and medium debt.

The essay treats these not as modules to be listed beside one another, but as dependencies in one engineering argument.

Four global boundaries organize the essay.

First, **formation is not the only successful ecological outcome**. Deliberate arrangement, direct coordination, medium-mediated co-response, retained ecological history, stable plural organization, and recurrent regimes can all be complete and appropriate results. Spark-mediated identity formation addresses the stronger claim that the attractor organization has acquired a new candidate participant.

Second, **ecological orientation is not external authorization or universal valuation**. A higher-scale quality may be enacted through the organization of an ecology without being represented as an objective, issued as a command, or carried by a higher-order identity. A designer’s desired quality, an ecology’s enacted quality, a participant’s valuation, and an observer’s interpretation must remain distinct unless their correspondence is separately demonstrated.

Third, **temporal geometry is substrate- and contract-relative**. Runtime order, event time, causal availability, proper-time accumulation, and semantic order are analytically distinct, but their concrete relation depends on the declared realization. An ordered identity-relative causal history does not by itself establish phenomenal experience, and LGRC-specific packet, delay, and clock constructions are not asserted as universal implementations of every RC system.

Fourth, **causal function is not yet full semantics**. This essay follows structural, dynamical, orientational, and temporal engineering axes: what ecological function a structure or process performs, how that function is carried, where a claimed quality is located, and which causal histories remain active. How these relations become observation, experience, knowledge, interpretation, or shared meaning remains a later semantic engineering axis. The present essay reaches that boundary but does not attempt to complete it.

# Part I — Engineering an Ecology at One Contextual Horizon

# 1. From Components to Ecology

## 1.1 What component engineering can genuinely establish

Engineering starts from distinctions because distinctions can be implemented and tested.

A graph can be given nodes, edges, conductance, event order, packet rules, storage, restoration, and causal accounting. A PDE realization can be given a domain, field, potential, coefficients, initial and boundary conditions, and numerical operators. An agentic ecology can be given participants, interfaces, costs, support, memory surfaces, and events through which the system changes.

These decisions are not merely scaffolding around the “real” ecology. They are contributions to the ecology. A boundary exists because something implements it. A route has a cost because the substrate carries one. A reservoir can support or deplete because some conserved quantity is actually accounted for. A participant can remain legible because some continuity is preserved through change.

The mistake begins only when the engineered distinctions are treated as a complete account of the ecological relation:

$$
\text{participants}
+
\text{interfaces}
+
\text{rules}
\longrightarrow
\text{ecology}.
$$

That expression assumes that what matters is exhausted by the parts and the direct relations declared among them. It assumes that participants retain the functions assigned to them, that history exists only where it is explicitly stored, and that the next event begins from the same world except for the variables directly updated by the previous one.

Many systems are well described this way. An RC ecology becomes a different engineering object when participation alters the condition through which later participation occurs.

## 1.2 When an event outlives its interface

Suppose one participant transfers coherence to another. If the transfer is complete when the source loses a declared amount and the target gains the same amount, the direct interface may be the whole relation.

Now suppose that the same transfer changes a route, consumes reserve, leaves a trace, hardens a boundary, changes local susceptibility, or alters which later request can be admitted. The direct exchange still happened, but its consequence is no longer confined to sender, receiver, and payload. The next event begins inside a changed condition.

The same expansion occurs in less explicit cases. A failed traversal can change later guidance. Repeated use can make one corridor easier while another becomes unsupported. A stable local success can flatten neighboring formation capacity. A boundary can successfully contain current activity while accumulating pressure that changes a later regime.

The engineering problem has therefore changed. It is no longer only:

> Which component changed which other component?

It has become:

> What shared condition carried the effect forward, and which later continuations did that changed condition make available, costly, or impossible?

That shared condition is the first object that a parts list does not contain by itself.

## 1.3 The medium becomes part of the mechanism

The phrase *shared medium* should not be understood as one additional component inserted between participants. It names the ecological condition through which prior participation remains consequential.

At one declared horizon, the medium may include route cost, support distribution, reserve, boundary pressure, traces, topology, event history, nursery demand, queueing, susceptibility, or another substrate-carried surface. Some of these may be explicitly engineered; some may emerge from repeated activity; some may remain scaffolded while their native counterpart is investigated.

What makes them medium is not that they are globally visible. It is that activity can change them and later participation can encounter the changed condition.

A first schematic ecology can therefore be written as

$$
\mathcal E_\sigma(t)
=
\left[
\{I_{i,\sigma}\},
\{\mathfrak p_{j,\sigma}\},
\Gamma_\sigma(t),
H_\sigma(t),
\mathcal S_\sigma(t)
\right],
$$

where the bracketed terms identify participant continuities, primitive capacities, contextual geometry, retained history, and support or constraint organization at a declared horizon $\sigma$.

The notation is not a new RC state tuple. It marks the expansion of the engineering account. Participants and primitives remain necessary, but their ecological function is now inseparable from the medium that receives and carries their effects.

## 1.4 Why one horizon must be fixed first

Once the medium enters the explanation, scale ambiguity appears immediately.

A route may be part of one participant at one horizon and part of the shared medium at another. A stable group of participants may be an ecology here and one constituent elsewhere. A trace may be a local state, a collective memory, or a fine-scale mechanism inside a slower regime.

If every scale is admitted at once, the basic engineering terms lose their referent. The question “what is the participant?” cannot be answered before the horizon of continuity is declared. The question “what is shared medium?” cannot be answered before the participants sharing it are named.

Part I therefore fixes one contextual horizon. It declares which continuities count as participants, which surfaces carry shared consequence, which boundaries are internal or external, which histories matter, and which observables support the current claim.

This is not a denial of finer or wider processes. It is the condition required to understand one ecology well enough that its function at another horizon can later be investigated.

The next question is now unavoidable. Once the medium participates in the mechanism, can a primitive still be assigned one function independently of the context that receives it?

# 2. The Medium That Returns

## 2.1 Relation persists by changing the shared condition

A direct message relates participants by moving a declared payload across a maintained interface. A shared-medium relation may have no single payload and no single receiving event. The relation persists because the condition of later participation has changed.

A route retains use. A reserve carries depletion. A pressure field carries unresolved load. A trace carries previous traversal. A boundary carries the history of what it has filtered. The medium is therefore not passive space around the mechanism. It is the temporal continuation of ecological consequence.

This gives a simple causal movement:

$$
I_i
\xrightarrow{a_i}
\Delta S
\longrightarrow
H_S
\longrightarrow
\{I_j:\operatorname{Susceptible}(I_j,S)\}
\longrightarrow
\text{response},
$$

where $S$ is one declared medium surface, $\Delta S$ the attributable perturbation, and $H_S$ the retained history through which the change remains active.

The expression is incomplete unless the changed ecology eventually returns to the participant or lineage whose activity entered the medium. That return is what will later make the relation an unfolding rather than a one-way effect.

## 2.2 A medium surface is a causal commitment

Calling something a medium surface makes an engineering commitment. It says that the surface is not merely an observer annotation or summary. It can be perturbed, it can retain or transform the perturbation, and later behavior depends on the resulting state.

Candidate surfaces include route support, conductance, boundary pressure, resource reserve, shared storage, queue or congestion, persistent topology, environmental cost, event history, parent-basin support, nursery demand, or a distributed susceptibility field.

An ecology may contain several such surfaces at once. They can have different spatial reach, ownership, persistence, maintenance cost, and decay. A direct message can coexist with a route trace; a globally scheduled phase can coexist with locally retained support; an explicit producer can coexist with a substrate-carried medium effect.

The engineering task is not to remove every explicit relation. It is to declare which relation is intended to be direct and which is intended to be carried by the medium.

## 2.3 Perturbation becomes history only through retention

An activity changes a medium surface at one moment. Whether that change becomes ecological history depends on what happens next.

The perturbation may dissipate before another participant arrives. It may persist long enough to change later eligibility. It may decay at a fixed or activity-dependent rate. Repeated use may reinforce it. Saturation may erase distinctions. Maintenance may be required to keep it active. Another process may neutralize it or incorporate it into a more stable structure.

The function of the medium therefore depends on a temporal composition:

$$
\text{deposition}
+
\text{persistence}
+
\text{decay}
+
\text{maintenance}
+
\text{cost}
+
\text{withdrawal}.
$$

These are not implementation details added after the mechanism is understood. A trace that never decays can become capture rather than memory. A trace that disappears too quickly cannot organize later participation. A costless surface can saturate without limit. A route reinforced independently of support can preserve a false history.

Retention makes the past active in the current ecology. It does not yet determine what function that history performs.

## 2.4 Susceptibility completes the relation

A medium change has ecological significance only relative to formations capable of responding to it.

Susceptibility is not merely whether a participant reads a variable. It includes whether its boundary, support, history, and current geometry make the changed condition consequential. The same trace can guide one identity, repel another, and remain irrelevant to a third. The same reserve loss can destabilize a dependent basin while leaving a self-supported basin unchanged.

Co-response therefore differs from broadcast. Several participants may respond together because the same changed medium has become relevant through their distinct local geometries. They do not need to receive an identical message or share one semantic account of what occurred.

This is the first place where the medium begins to look like ecology rather than storage. It composes prior participation with present susceptibility and thereby changes what several participants can do next.

## 2.5 Direct relations and medium debt

Not every intended ecological relation can be carried natively by the current substrate. A direct message, broadcast, scheduler, controller, or global variable may be the clearest way to expose the target behavior.

The engineering error is not using the scaffold. It is forgetting which relation the scaffold supplies.

**Medium debt** records where explicit coordination stands in for a relation intended to be carried through trace, pressure, route cost, reserve, susceptibility, parent-basin modulation, or another medium surface. It asks what must remain bounded in the claim, what should happen under withdrawal, and what later evidence would show that the substrate now carries the relation itself.

Medium debt differs from **producer residue**. Producer residue supplies state or policy needed for a participant to act before its identity geometry carries that behavior natively. Medium debt supplies relation or coordination before the shared medium carries it. One concerns what sustains the participant; the other concerns what sustains participation among participants.

Both can be legitimate. Both become misleading only when hidden.

## 2.6 The medium returns

The medium becomes part of a full ecology when its changed state returns as a condition of further participation.

Return may be obvious: the participant re-enters a route it previously changed. It may be indirect: neighboring activity depletes support, and the original participant later encounters reduced viability. It may be delayed: a trace changes the behavior of another participant, whose activity then modifies the first participant’s boundary. It may also occur through the disappearance of an option rather than through a positive signal.

The important relation is:

$$
a_t
\longrightarrow
\Delta\Gamma_t
\longrightarrow
\Delta\mathcal E_t
\longrightarrow
\Delta I_{t+\Delta t}.
$$

A medium effect that never reaches a susceptible continuation remains a field perturbation. A medium effect that changes the ecology but never returns to the focal participant may still be ecologically real, but it does not yet complete that participant’s unfolding cycle.

Once return is included, the primitive cannot retain one complete ecological meaning independently of the context through which the cycle closes.

# 3. Function in Context

## 3.1 A structure’s ecological function is formed in the ecology

A trace persists. Is it memory?

A boundary prevents crossing. Is it protection?

A basin attracts coherence. Is it identity support, an extractive sink, or a scaffolded pressure concentration?

The visible structure does not settle the question. Its ecological function depends on how the surrounding geometry routes flux, which histories it retains, what support it consumes, which boundaries it changes, and what later participation becomes possible through it.

At the declared horizon, the function of a formation $X$ can be written schematically as

$$
\operatorname{Function}(X\mid\Gamma_\sigma)=q.
$$

Here $\Gamma_\sigma$ denotes the contextual geometry at the declared horizon $\sigma$, and $q$ the selected ecological function.

The context is not a parameter appended to an otherwise complete object. It participates in constituting the function.

This is why the same literal formation can change function across ecologies, and why different literal formations can provide corresponding functions when their contexts preserve the relevant relations.

## 3.2 A primitive is a context-typed capacity

A primitive remains useful because engineering requires stable, testable capacities. The correction is that the primitive’s ecological claim must include where and how the capacity functions.

A provisional record is:

$$
\mathfrak p
=
\left[
X,
\partial X,
\mathcal C_{\mathrm{adm}},
\mathcal T,
\mathcal I,
\mathcal D
\right],
$$

where $X$ is the realized formation, $\partial X$ its relevant interface, $\mathcal C_{\mathrm{adm}}$ the admitted context class, $\mathcal T$ the transformation enacted there, $\mathcal I$ the relations that must be preserved, and $\mathcal D$ the declared scaffold, producer, medium, and naturalization debt.

The record does not divide primitives into static structures and active transformations. Every stable RC formation already persists through organized transformation. The purpose of the record is narrower: it states which continuity is being treated as the primitive and what field change is attributed to its participation.

The primitive claim becomes:

$$
\mathfrak p_{\Gamma_\sigma}:
\Gamma_{\sigma,t}
\longrightarrow
\Gamma_{\sigma,t+\Delta t}.
$$

The output is not only a local value. It is the changed context inherited by later activity.

## 3.3 Contextual variation reveals the function

A primitive becomes an ecological object only when the function survives the comparisons appropriate to the claim.

The first comparison keeps the primitive fixed and changes medium geometry, support, history, or neighboring formations. If the function changes, the context is constitutive rather than incidental.

The second changes the literal implementation while preserving the relations required for the function. If another structure produces the same bounded ecological effect under matched controls, the function is multiply realizable within the tested context class.

The third matches present-state appearance while varying causal history. If later response differs, current shape is not enough to explain the function.

The fourth holds focal success stable while measuring neighboring capacity, reserve, boundary integrity, and later formation possibility. It distinguishes focal persistence from ecological function.

These are not four tests every experiment must perform at once. Together they express the discipline created by contextual function: do not infer function from structure alone, and do not infer ecological success from the focal result alone.

## 3.4 Portability becomes contextual reconstruction

Once function belongs to a formation in context, copying the formation does not guarantee that the function travels with it.

Portability may require changing the primitive to fit the target context, changing the context to support the primitive, or co-adapting both until the relevant relation is restored. A successful transfer must therefore say what was preserved, what changed, what debt was introduced, and what remains supported rather than native.

This gives the engineering program a more useful question than “does the same component run elsewhere?”:

> Which contextual relation must be preserved or reconstructed for the same ecological function to remain available?

The answer may reveal that one substrate realizes the function through smooth pressure, another through topology and event history, and another through explicit support accounting. Literal difference is not failure if the functional relation is preserved; literal sameness is not success if the receiving ecology changes the function.

## 3.5 The context witness becomes necessary

By this point, a primitive record alone can no longer support the claim. The receiving ecology must remain attached to the evidence.

A context witness can therefore preserve:

$$
W_{\mathrm{ctx}}
=
\left[
\Gamma_{\mathrm{pre}},
X,
\mathfrak p,
\mathcal O,
\mathcal I,
\Gamma_{\mathrm{post}},
H,
\ell
\right],
$$

where $\mathcal O$ is the observable record and $\ell$ the claim ceiling.

The witness should make it possible to ask what context received the primitive, what changed locally and in the medium, which history was required, what support remained external, which neighboring capacities changed, and what stronger claims remain blocked.

The witness appears here because the argument has created its need. A primitive without context can still be a useful implementation object. It is not yet a complete ecological-function claim.

The next limit now follows. If a contribution changes the context that later changes the contributor, then action is not complete at the point where the primitive finishes executing.

# 4. Unfolding as the Engineering Cycle

## 4.1 Action is a hinge, not an endpoint

A conventional agent account can often stop at action:

$$
\text{observe}
\rightarrow
\text{decide}
\rightarrow
\text{act}.
$$

The environment may later return reward or another observation, but the identity of the agent and the grammar of the loop remain largely intact.

In an RC ecology, action is the hinge through which the participant’s present formation enters the shared medium. What follows may change the participant’s boundary, support, susceptibility, available continuation, and relation to other identities. The participant that receives the result is therefore not merely the same decision-maker with one more datum.

The relevant movement is:

$$
(I_t,\Gamma_t)
\xrightarrow{a_t}
\Gamma_{t+\Delta t}
\longrightarrow
I_{t+\Delta t}.
$$

The first arrow says that the act changes the ecology. The second says that the changed ecology participates in what the identity becomes next.

Participation is therefore not an output added to a completed participant. It is the continuation of the field-formed identity through ecological response.

## 4.2 The complete unfolding cycle

At one horizon, the engineering cycle can be stated as:

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
&\text{participant continuity}\
&\rightarrow
\text{contextually available contribution}\
&\rightarrow
\text{medium perturbation}\
&\rightarrow
\text{retention, propagation, decay, or composition}\
&\rightarrow
\text{susceptible ecological response}\
&\rightarrow
\text{changed participant and ecology}\
&\rightarrow
\text{next locally available contribution}.
\end{aligned}}
$$

This is not one mandatory software architecture. It is the causal closure an engineering account should be able to reconstruct.

A participant cannot enact what its current boundary, support, history, and accessible routes do not make available. A contribution that changes no declared medium cannot explain later medium-mediated response. A retained change that reaches no susceptible formation has not yet composed into ecological relation. A response that never returns to the participant leaves the participant’s unfolding unmeasured.

The cycle is useful because it prevents several partial successes from being promoted into a complete ecology. Action emission is not participation continuity. Medium persistence is not meaningful reception. Focal persistence is not ecological generativity. Stable output is not identity.

## 4.3 Available contribution is already contextual

The participant acts from an affordance surface shaped by its present formation:

$$
a_t
\in
\operatorname{Avail}(I_t,\Gamma_t).
$$

The notation does not assume a finite action menu. It marks that locally available contribution depends on boundary, support, route accessibility, susceptibility, retained history, cost, primitive repertoire, and current medium state.

Engineering can shape this surface deliberately. A route can be opened or closed. A reservoir can make one transformation viable. A boundary can protect a partial formation long enough for another relation to appear. A medium trace can make a continuation visible without sending a direct instruction.

This gives design a precise but bounded scope. The engineer does not need to prescribe every future act. The engineer can configure which kinds of participation the present ecology can actually sustain.

## 4.4 Ecological return completes the participant-side account

The ecology answers through changed conditions.

Return may take the form of support depletion, route reinforcement, loss of an alternative, boundary hardening, changed neighboring capacity, a new coupling, an accumulated trace, or the participant’s own increased or decreased viability. It may arrive immediately or only after several other participants have acted through the changed medium.

The return is not necessarily a message. It is whatever part of the changed ecology becomes consequential for the participant’s later continuity.

This gives a participant-side test:

> Did the contribution enter the ecology in a way that later changed the conditions through which the participant continued?

If not, the act may still have produced an external effect, but the unfolding of that participant remains incomplete in the evidence.

## 4.5 Agency becomes continuity through response

The agency essays become necessary here because the unfolding cycle requires a way to distinguish a continuing participant from a stable label, scripted role, or sequence of outputs.

Agency is not reduced to the moment of choice. It is tested through what carries identity continuity across ecological response:

- Does the participant persist when declared support is withdrawn?
- Does it regenerate or substitute the function of that support?
- Does its continuity survive a declared substrate mapping?
- Does the measured identity remain more than a proxy for hidden producer state?
- Does its persistence preserve or reduce the basin-forming capacity of the surrounding ecology?

A provisional profile can be written as

$$
\operatorname{AgencyProfile}_{\mathrm{RC}}(I)
=
\left[
\text{withdrawal resistance},
\text{naturalization depth},
\text{transfer capacity},
\text{proxy integrity},
\text{ecological effect}
\right].
$$

The profile is not a universal scalar and does not settle every philosophical question of agency. It keeps the engineering claim attached to the continuity that actually survives the loop.

## 4.6 Ecological effect prevents focal closure

A participant can remain stable by narrowing the field around it.

It can consume support, flatten neighboring differentiation, monopolize routes, or require an increasingly rigid boundary. From the focal view, continuity has succeeded. From the ecological view, the same persistence may have reduced the conditions under which other formations can continue or arise.

The engineering record therefore needs ecology-side observables such as neighboring differentiation, support distribution, route availability, reserve, boundary integrity, candidate formation, and lost formation capacity.

This does not make one effect globally right and another wrong. It makes the claim horizon explicit. A participant may be locally persistent and ecologically extractive at the same time. Another may accept local instability while increasing wider formation capacity.

Generativity becomes a relation between focal continuity and the ecology it leaves behind.

## 4.7 The unfolding witness appears at the end of the argument

The previous relations can now be gathered without turning the essay into a specification-first document.

An ecological unfolding witness may preserve:

$$
W_{\mathrm{unfold}}
=
\left[
I_t,
\Gamma_t,
a_t,
S_t,
\Delta S_t,
H_t,
\mathcal R_t,
I_{t+\Delta t},
\Gamma_{t+\Delta t},
\mathcal D,
\ell
\right],
$$

where $S_t$ is the declared medium surface, $\mathcal R_t$ records susceptible responses and ecological effects, $\mathcal D$ records support, producer, medium, and naturalization debt, and $\ell$ is the claim ceiling.

The witness is not proposed as a mandatory runtime schema. It is a completeness test. It refuses to stop at a primitive without context, an action without medium, a medium change without reception, a response without participant continuity, or focal continuity without ecological effect.

Once unfolding repeats, the ecology acquires a history that can become organization in its own right.

# 5. When Participation Becomes Ecology

## 5.1 Repetition changes the condition of repetition

One unfolding cycle leaves a changed participant and medium. A second cycle therefore does not repeat the first from the same state.

A route may be slightly easier. A reserve may be lower. A boundary may now admit one flow and resist another. A participant may recognize a trace or encounter a new cost. The repeated cycle begins from the history produced by the earlier one.

This is the point at which interaction becomes ecology in the stronger sense. The relation is no longer a series of isolated exchanges among stable identities. The medium has begun to carry a shared past into the conditions of present participation.

History is therefore not only stored data. It is the changed geometry through which the next event acquires a different effect.

## 5.2 Stable plural ecology is a complete form

Distinct identities can participate through one shared medium while remaining distinct.

Their activity may form circulation, division of function, route systems, support exchange, recurrent co-response, ecological memory, or a recoverable regime. The attractor set can remain unchanged:

$$
\mathcal A_N
\longrightarrow
\mathcal A_N.
$$

Nothing is missing merely because no new identity appears. The ecology can be stable, historically formed, and natively medium-mediated while preserving participant plurality.

This is especially important for engineering. A higher-order identity may be undesirable where local autonomy, differentiated roles, or reversible coordination matter. A plural ecology can be the intended and fully successful result.

The spark is therefore not the validation of ecological composition. It answers a different question: whether the attractor organization has acquired another candidate participant.

## 5.3 Holding geometry gives unfinished formations time

Some ecologies must preserve partial formations before their wider relation can be known.

A holding geometry provides enough boundary for local distinction, enough support for continued viability, enough history for later interpretation, enough permeability for response, enough reserve for integration latency, and enough revisability for transformation or release.

These relations must be held together. Unlimited support can fill the ecology with dormant formations until action becomes impossible. Insufficient support makes immediate usefulness compulsory. Boundaries that are too weak dissolve distinctions; boundaries that are too rigid prevent later composition. Memory without renewed encounter becomes archive rather than ecology.

Holding is therefore active organization. The ecology does not simply retain fragments. It changes the conditions through which they can be encountered again.

This is an engineering capacity. It can be built through lineage-preserving state, bounded reserves, selective permeability, delayed coupling, re-entry opportunities, and explicit release mechanisms.

## 5.4 Future composability is not the preservation of every option

Participation always closes some possibilities. Coherence is redistributed, routes deepen, boundaries form, and resources are spent. An ecology that refuses every closure will also fail to form.

The question is whether the closure leaves the field capable of further relation.

Future composability includes viable interfaces, distinguishable partial formations, recoverable histories, non-exhausted reserve, revisable boundaries, and possible higher-order coupling. It does not mean maximizing the number of futures. It means preserving enough ecological answerability that later becoming is not forced to proceed only through the current dominant basin.

The relation can be stated as:

$$
\text{real transformation}
+
\text{continued capacity for later ecological relation}.
$$

This gives stable ecology an important readout beyond immediate performance.

## 5.5 Recurrent participation forms regimes

As histories accumulate, the ecology may begin to return to a characteristic organization.

The regime can be homeostatic, exploratory, tension-responsive, fragment-holding, support-circulating, or otherwise recurrent. What persists is not one exact state but a geometry–flux organization that makes a family of continuations available.

A regime can become historically dominant without adding another attractor mode. It can alter the contextual functions of existing participants and primitives while preserving their identity count.

This distinction matters experimentally:

$$
\text{stable ecological regime}
\neq
\text{new participant}.
$$

The regime is an ecology of continuation among existing identities. A new participant requires the stronger formation relation developed later.

## 5.6 Failure modes disclose missing ecological relations

Stable plural ecologies have their own failure modes: stale trace capture, medium saturation, uncontrolled pressure, hidden support concentration, rigid boundaries, false co-response from one controller, indefinite scaffold dependence, or loss of participant continuity beneath a stable aggregate.

These outcomes are not evidence that shared-medium organization is inferior to direct control. They identify relations that the current ecology does not yet carry adequately.

A stale trace reveals missing decay or revalidation. A saturated medium reveals insufficient competition or clearing. A stable aggregate with dissolving participants reveals a scale or continuity conflict. A scaffold-dependent regime may remain useful while its native claim stays bounded.

The ecology becomes engineerable when these results are classified rather than collapsed into success and failure.

The same historical organization can also approach a stronger transition. The current attractor landscape may become unable to contain the differentiation produced through it. That is one formation pathway among several.

# 6. The Many Ways an Ecology Forms

## 6.1 Formation is plural before it is spark-mediated

By this point, several forming mechanisms are already visible.

Deliberate arrangement establishes participants, topology, interfaces, constraints, and support. Shared-medium mediation makes indirect consequences mutually relevant. Retained history changes later susceptibility and route structure. Regime stabilization makes a characteristic continuation recurrent. Spark-mediated reconfiguration adds a new attractor mode.

These mechanisms are not alternatives competing to be the most native or the most ecological. They form different organizations and can become conditions for one another.

An assembled route may carry native history. A stable plural ecology may remain the intended result. A historically reinforced regime may later contribute to instability. A spark-formed identity may enter an explicitly arranged ecology and continue through direct interfaces as well as shared media.

The engineering question is therefore not which method is correct. It is:

> What did each mechanism contribute, what organization now exists, and what further pathways has that organization made possible?

## 6.2 Deliberate assembly forms real ecological conditions

Assembly can specify boundaries, reservoirs, routes, coupling surfaces, support structures, schedulers, direct communication, and primitive capacities. It can produce a stable and valuable ecology without a spark.

Its limitation is not artificiality. It is explanatory compression. If the assembled account includes only part-to-part relations while the shared medium carries history, support, pressure, or susceptibility, then the engineering model is incomplete.

Medium-aware assembly is therefore a legitimate RC practice. It deliberately configures the participants and the field through which their relations become consequential.

The result may remain explicitly maintained. It may naturalize some of its support. It may produce a stable plural regime. It may also create the context from which another identity later forms.

## 6.3 Historical stabilization forms ecology without adding identity

Repeated participation can make one route reliable, one division of function recurrent, or one support circulation self-restoring. The ecology has formed historically even though the attractor set remains the same.

This kind of formation is often more important than dramatic reconfiguration. It is how a medium becomes trustworthy, how a boundary becomes selective rather than merely imposed, how a trace becomes reusable, and how participants become mutually adapted without losing their identities.

Engineering such an ecology requires attention to retention, decay, cost, recovery, and withdrawal. The organization must be carried through the declared dynamics, not merely through an observer’s interpretation of repeated output.

## 6.4 Pre-spark insufficiency is another ecological condition

A spark becomes relevant only when the current attractor organization can no longer contain the differentiation being produced through it.

At the engineering level, this may appear as competing flows using the same surfaces, support demands that no longer fit the current distribution, traces pulling continuation in incompatible directions, increasing boundary pressure, coupling among previously separate identities, rising sensitivity to small perturbations, or wider constraints that make the current basin organization insufficient.

Congestion is one manifestation. Concentration, synchronization, boundary failure, support asymmetry, and cross-scale pressure may produce others.

The important object is the history leading to the condition:

$$
\Gamma_{t_0}
\rightarrow
\Gamma_{t_1}
\rightarrow
\cdots
\rightarrow
\Gamma_{t_s^-}.
$$

A parts list cannot explain why the spark occurs. The formation pathway must preserve ordering, timing, support, retained history, and the changing functions of the participating formations.

## 6.5 Spark changes the attractor organization

At the spark:

$$
\mathcal A_N
\longrightarrow
\mathcal A_{N+1}.
$$

A new minimum appears and the ecology acquires a candidate identity mode.

This is not merely stronger coordination, a more stable regime, or a more tightly coupled motif. It is the specific formation mechanism through which another potential participant becomes available in the attractor organization.

The engineering record must therefore preserve the pre-spark ecology, instability signature, region and horizon, native or scaffold-triggered provenance, coherence redistribution, candidate basin, and relation to prior participants.

The spark creates the candidate. It does not by itself establish a durable identity.

## 6.6 Post-spark dynamics decide what has formed

The candidate must continue through RC cycles.

Does returning flux deepen it? Does a legible boundary persist? Does the candidate retain history? Does it remain compatible with neighboring modes? Does it participate in sustaining its support? Can it recover after bounded perturbation? What happens when scaffolding is withdrawn?

The candidate may stabilize, remain support-dependent, merge into an existing identity, split, or dissolve. Admission is therefore an evidence boundary rather than a second dynamical event.

The new closure may also reorganize the functions of earlier formations. A route may become internal circulation, a trace may become memory, a boundary fragment may become an interface, and an existing identity may become support ecology, nested constituent, external participant, or lost lineage.

This is why a formed identity is not simply the mechanical sum of pre-existing parts. Yet the pre-existing parts remain real contributions to the formation context.

## 6.7 Ecological outcomes remain open

The one-horizon ecology can now be seen as a field of possible organizations rather than a ladder with spark at the top.

It may remain directly coordinated. It may become natively medium-mediated. It may acquire stable plural history. It may form holding geometries and recurrent regimes. It may reorganize existing basins. It may cross a spark and form a candidate participant. The candidate may stabilize, nest, absorb, coexist, or dissolve.

The engineering task is to work deliberately with this abundance of pathways while keeping their claims distinct.

At one horizon, that task is now coherent. Participants have declared continuity. Primitives have contextual functions. Medium changes carry history. Participation returns through the ecology. Stable plural regimes and new identity formation can be distinguished.

Then one question reopens the entire account:

> What is this complete ecology relative to another contextual horizon?

# Part II — Engineering Scale-Recursive Ecologies

# 7. When the Ecology Changes Horizon

## 7.1 The completed ecology becomes incomplete again

At the focal horizon, the ecology appears to have all the relations required for engineering. Its participants are declared. Its medium surfaces are known. Its primitive functions are contextualized. Its unfolding cycles, regimes, and formation pathways can be observed and controlled.

The account becomes incomplete again when the whole ecology is encountered from another horizon.

The ecology may function as one participant in a wider field. One of its participants may itself contain a finer ecology. A medium surface that appeared global at the focal horizon may be generated by many lower-scale identities. A support supplied from “outside” may be the activity of another higher-order organization. A local trace may accumulate into an institutional history, while an institutional constraint returns as local cost or eligibility.

Nothing in the one-horizon account was false. Its terms were horizon-relative.

The engineering question therefore changes from:

> What are the participant, medium, and context?

into:

> At which horizon is this formation a participant, at which horizon is it medium or constituent, and how do effects move among those readings?

## 7.2 Scale is a horizon of closure

Scale should not initially be reduced to physical size or numerical resolution.

A contextual horizon is the level at which a boundary, history, susceptibility, and characteristic continuation become legible as one closure. Two physically similar structures may belong to different scales if one carries a persistent identity and the other is only a transient relation. A small process can sustain a long-lived medium; a large aggregate can fail to form any higher-order continuity.

Let $\sigma$ name one declared horizon. At that horizon, we may have:

$$
\Gamma_\sigma
\rightarrow
I_\sigma
\rightarrow
P_\sigma
\rightarrow
\Gamma_\sigma'.
$$

The same formation may then participate in another horizon $\sigma'$:

$$
I_\sigma
\subset
\Gamma_{\sigma'}.
$$

The containment is not merely spatial. It says that the identity’s activity, history, support demand, or boundary now contributes to the context through which wider continuations occur.

This is the first scale-recursive relation: what is participant from one horizon becomes part of context from another without ceasing to be a participant locally.

## 7.3 Medium surfaces can be nested

The shared medium is not necessarily one flat layer.

A route surface used by local identities may be generated by lower-scale conductance, packet history, pressure, or resource distribution. Repeated local participation may then produce a wider support pattern, convention, role structure, or ecological memory that acts as medium for a larger organization.

The relation can be written schematically as:

$$
\Gamma_{\sigma'}
=
\operatorname{Organization}
\left(
\{I_{i,\sigma}\},
\{P_{i,\sigma}\},
H_{\sigma}
\right).
$$

The wider medium is not necessarily reducible to a sum of lower-scale states. Its function may depend on recurrent organization, timing, boundaries, and history that become visible only at the wider horizon.

Likewise, the wider medium can return downward. A collective support deficit may change local reserve. A higher-order boundary may alter which local exchanges remain admissible. An institutional history may become a constraint on present local action. A parent basin may supply or withdraw support.

The engineering object is therefore not only nested structure. It is nested consequence.

## 7.4 The horizon contract

Because ecological functions change with horizon, every multiscale claim needs a horizon contract.

The contract should declare:

- the focal participant continuity;
- the medium surfaces at that horizon;
- the finer relations treated as internal;
- the wider relations treated as context;
- the observables used to identify closure;
- the histories and support accounted for;
- and the mappings by which effects are traced across horizons.

Without such a contract, the same stable aggregate can be called a participant in one paragraph, a medium in another, and an ecology in a third without any evidential change.

The horizon contract does not make one scale more real than another. It keeps each analytical cut attached to the closure it claims to observe.

Once the horizons are declared, the next question is whether the same explanatory pattern merely recurs across them or whether participation actually couples them.

# 8. Engineering Scale Recursion

## 8.1 Recurrence is the weakest and most common relation

The context–identity–participation loop may appear at several horizons:

$$
\Gamma_\sigma
\rightarrow
I_\sigma
\rightarrow
P_\sigma
\rightarrow
\Gamma_\sigma',
$$

and:

$$
\Gamma_{\sigma'}
\rightarrow
I_{\sigma'}
\rightarrow
P_{\sigma'}
\rightarrow
\Gamma_{\sigma'}'.
$$

This is **scale recurrence**. Similar explanatory relations are useful at both scales.

Recurrence does not imply that the same structures, observables, or equations appear unchanged. A local trace and an organizational convention may both retain history while being realized very differently. A local boundary and a higher-order boundary may both regulate exchange without sharing one literal mechanism.

Recurrence is enough to guide questions. It is not enough to establish transfer or coupling.

## 8.2 Covariance asks whether the relation survives translation

A stronger claim is **scale covariance**.

A mapping $F_{\sigma\rightarrow\sigma'}$ may transform structures, observables, and temporal windows while preserving a recognizable ecological relation:

$$
F_{\sigma\rightarrow\sigma'}
\left(
\Gamma_\sigma
\rightarrow
I_\sigma
\rightarrow
P_\sigma
\right)
\approx
\Gamma_{\sigma'}
\rightarrow
I_{\sigma'}
\rightarrow
P_{\sigma'}.
$$

Covariance is useful for engineering because it permits knowledge gained at one horizon to guide construction at another. It also demands a preservation contract: which boundary, history, support, continuity, and response relations survive the mapping, and which do not?

A graph route and a PDE relaxation imprint may correspond with respect to guidance while differing in causal time, topology, and memory. A team routine and a local trace may correspond as retained history while differing in what carries the retention.

The mapping is valuable precisely because it does not pretend the structures are identical.

## 8.3 Invariance is stronger than analogy

**Scale invariance** requires that a declared property remain unchanged under a specified transformation:

$$
F_{\sigma\rightarrow\sigma'}(q)=q.
$$

This is a much stronger claim than recurrence or covariance. It should not be inferred from the repeated use of words such as identity, boundary, memory, or participation.

An invariant may concern conservation, a normalized relation, a closure condition, a response ratio, or another formally declared property. The relevant scale transformation and tolerance must be explicit.

The engineering discipline is simple:

> Similar vocabulary across horizons is not evidence of invariance.

The claim becomes meaningful only when a property survives controlled transformation.

## 8.4 Coupling is causal, not descriptive

The most important engineering relation is **inter-scale coupling**.

A local participation changes wider geometry:

$$
P_\sigma
\longrightarrow
\Delta\Gamma_{\sigma'}.
$$

The changed wider geometry then alters local continuation:

$$
\Delta\Gamma_{\sigma'}
\longrightarrow
\Delta I_\sigma
\quad\text{or}\quad
\Delta\operatorname{Avail}(I_\sigma,\Gamma_\sigma).
$$

This is not established by seeing similar patterns at two scales. It requires causal evidence that activity crosses the horizon and returns.

The full scale-recursive unfolding can therefore become:

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
&\text{local contribution}\
&\rightarrow
\text{local medium change}\
&\rightarrow
\text{cross-scale accumulation or propagation}\
&\rightarrow
\text{wider ecological response}\
&\rightarrow
\text{changed local support, boundary, or affordance}\
&\rightarrow
\text{changed local continuation}.
\end{aligned}}
$$

Only after that return is demonstrated should the system be described as one coupled multiscale ecology rather than as several analogous ecologies observed at different levels.

## 8.5 Cross-scale retention changes where history lives

A history can be produced locally and retained elsewhere.

Repeated local activity may form a wider route, role distribution, support pattern, or boundary. Once retained at the wider horizon, that history can outlast individual local participants and return to newcomers who did not enact the original sequence.

Conversely, a wider event may alter fine-scale geometry so that local response changes before any local identity represents the wider cause.

This complicates memory attribution. The participant carrying the history may not be the participant that originally produced it. The medium may become the continuity through which experience at one scale becomes ecological knowledge at another.

Engineering must therefore record not only whether history persists, but where it is carried, at which horizon it is legible, and how it returns to local participation.

## 8.6 Higher-order ecology is not automatically higher-order identity

A stable ecology of lower-scale identities may become a coherent formation at a wider horizon without becoming an identity there.

Coordination, common response, aggregate output, shared history, and even recurrent regime are not sufficient by themselves. A higher-order identity claim requires evidence at the wider horizon for boundary, attractivity, reflexive closure, retained history, susceptibility, support, and continuity under perturbation.

The distinctions remain:

$$
\text{coordination}
\neq
\text{stable higher-order ecology}
\neq
\text{higher-order identity}.
$$

This prevents a useful ecology from being overclaimed while also preserving the possibility that participation among existing identities may eventually form a new participant.

## 8.7 Formation pathways can cross horizons

A new identity may form at one horizon through conditions accumulated at another.

Fine-scale traces can accumulate into higher-scale instability. Higher-scale pressure can make a local differentiation viable. Cross-scale feedback can produce a closure that belongs to neither scale considered independently.

The formation pathway may therefore include:

$$
\text{fine-scale participation}
\rightarrow
\text{wider medium history}
\rightarrow
\text{higher-scale insufficiency}
\rightarrow
\text{spark},
$$

or:

$$
\text{higher-scale support change}
\rightarrow
\text{local boundary shift}
\rightarrow
\text{local spark}.
$$

This is one of the most promising experimental areas opened by the scale-recursive view. It asks not only whether a spark occurs, but where its formation conditions were carried and at which horizon the resulting continuity becomes legible.

## 8.8 FRC makes the relation explicit

Ordinary RC is sufficient to motivate nested contextual horizons. A local basin participates in wider geometry; wider geometry shapes local flux; histories can be retained in structures that become context elsewhere.

FRC makes this relation explicit through scale-resolved coherence:

$$
C=C(x,t;\sigma),
$$

with scale-local dynamics, inter-scale flux, and a collective geometry formed across scale contributions.

This gives engineering a direct representation of relations already discovered conceptually:

- local identities can remain legible within scale bands;
- coherence can move across scale;
- fine-scale participation can contribute to wider geometry;
- wider geometry can constrain local viability and continuation;
- and identity can be tracked through multiscale attractor structures rather than inferred only from nested description.

FRC does not automatically prove that any chosen hierarchy is scale invariant, that every higher-order ecology is an identity, or that every cross-scale analogy is causal. It provides a more elegant substrate in which those questions can be stated and tested.

## 8.9 Scale-recursive engineering needs its own witnesses

A scale-recursive claim should preserve more than two snapshots at different resolutions.

A cross-scale unfolding witness must connect local contribution, local medium change, scale transfer or accumulation, wider response, and ecological return. A scale-preservation contract must state which functions, invariants, and histories are mapped across horizons. A higher-order identity witness must distinguish aggregate stability from local continuity at the wider scale.

These records become necessary because the one-scale witnesses no longer show where the causal relation crosses the horizon.

These witnesses also expose the next engineering question. When wider organization returns as changed local constraint, that return can be mistaken for authorization, and recurrent ecological consequence can be mistaken for a universally valid quality. Part III begins by separating those relations.

# Part III — Engineering Ecological Orientation Without External Authorization

# 9. When Constraint Is Mistaken for Authorization

## 9.1 The higher scale returns before it commands

Part II established that a wider ecology can return into local participation as changed support, boundary, cost, timing, eligibility, or susceptibility. Once that return is visible, a familiar interpretation becomes tempting. The higher scale appears to constrain the local participant; therefore it appears to stand above the participant. If the ecology repeatedly amplifies one activity and suppresses another, it can seem to be assigning roles, approving contributions, and judging whether a participant serves the whole.

That interpretation imports an organizational grammar that the causal relation does not require.

A wider ecology can shape local continuation without representing an instruction. A route can become easier because prior participation deepened it. Support can become scarce because several identities draw from the same reserve. A boundary can harden because repeated pressure changed the field through which later crossing occurs. None of these relations needs a higher-scale observer to decide what the participant should do.

The return is real:

$$
\Delta\Gamma_{\Sigma,t}
\longrightarrow
\Delta\operatorname{Avail}(I_{i,\sigma,t},\Gamma_{i,\sigma,t}),
$$

where $\Sigma$ denotes a wider horizon and $\operatorname{Avail}$ the locally available continuations of participant $I_i$ at horizon $\sigma$.

What does not follow is:

$$
\Delta\Gamma_{\Sigma,t}
=
\text{an authoritative instruction issued to }I_{i,\sigma,t}.
$$

The wider organization returns as condition before it is ever represented as command. This is the first engineering distinction of Part III:

> **Higher-scale constraint is an ecological relation. Authorization is one possible interpretation or mechanism within that relation, not its universal form.**

## 9.2 Authority can exist inside the ecology

Rejecting external authorization should not become a claim that RC ecologies cannot contain authority, centralized control, explicit objectives, or direct role assignment.

An engineered ecology may include a scheduler, controller, approval service, leader, policy surface, queen-like reproductive center, routing authority, or globally maintained constraint. Such structures can be causally important. They may stabilize a regime, prevent destructive coupling, preserve a boundary, or coordinate activity faster than a distributed medium could.

Their presence does not place them outside the ecology.

A controller consumes support, receives incomplete observations, acts through interfaces, depends on timing and topology, and changes the medium that later changes it or its successors. Its authority is therefore an ecological function carried by a particular formation and context. It can be tested, withdrawn, overloaded, bypassed, naturalized, or dissolved like any other mechanism.

The important contrast is not:

$$
\text{authority}
\quad\text{versus}\quad
\text{ecology}.
$$

It is:

$$
\text{authority as one ecological mechanism}
\quad\text{versus}\quad
\text{authority imagined as the external ground of ecological meaning}.
$$

The first is engineerable. The second hides the actual causal field by treating approval as though it made participation real.

## 9.3 Ecological response is not approval

A contribution enters the shared medium and the ecology answers through consequence. It may amplify the contribution, resist it, route around it, absorb it, ignore it, or transform the participant that introduced it.

These responses matter. They change what can happen next. But they do not automatically constitute a judgment.

A route becoming unavailable does not by itself mean that the traversal was wrong. A trace being reinforced does not mean that the ecology has declared the originating participant valuable. A contribution can be rejected semantically and remain causally consequential. It can be praised while depending on hidden support that weakens the wider field. It can be adopted after the originating identity has dissolved.

Several relations that ordinary engineering often compresses into *acceptance* should therefore remain separate:

- **recognition**: the contribution becomes legible to another identity or observer;
- **valuation**: it is experienced or classified as desirable, threatening, useful, costly, or irrelevant;
- **adoption**: it becomes part of a shared route, practice, structure, or regime;
- **support**: the ecology provides conditions through which the participant or contribution can continue;
- **viability**: the relevant identity continuity remains sustainable;
- **actuality**: the participation occurred and changed the field.

The last relation does not wait for the others.

$$
\boxed{
\operatorname{Actuality}(a_t)
\neq
\operatorname{Approval}_{\Gamma}(a_t)
}
$$

The ecology cannot retroactively authorize an unfolding. It can only participate in what that unfolding becomes.

## 9.4 Answerability without permission

This changes the engineering meaning of answerability.

Answerability does not require every consequence to return to the originating participant. It requires the contribution to remain causally attributable within the declared ecology and requires the account of participation not to stop at intention or emission. Some consequences return through support, resistance, changed susceptibility, altered opportunity, or loss. Others remain in the shared medium, enter another identity’s history, contribute at another scale, or persist as unresolved causal residue. Participant-side return is one form of ecological consequence, not its complete extent.

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
\text{answerability}
=
\text{causal traceability through ecological consequence}
}
$$

not:

$$
\text{answerability}
=
\text{successful authorization by a higher-scale judge}.
$$

The participant’s own unfolding is revised by the consequences that become available to its continuity. The wider ecology may carry further consequences beyond that return.

The distinction avoids two opposite errors.

One error makes the participant dependent on recognition: the ecology must confirm the contribution before the participant can inhabit its own unfolding.

The other makes the participant sovereign: because approval is unnecessary, ecological consequence can be ignored.

RC supports neither. The participant does not need permission for its participation to be actual, but its participation never lies outside the field in which its consequences persist.

This removes judgment as the primary explanation of ecological regulation. It leaves a harder question: if roles are not simply assigned and approved from above, what makes a participant’s activity stabilize as one recognizable contribution to the ecology?

# 10. Role as Locally Stabilized Unfolding

## 10.1 Activity is not yet role

A participant performs one activity. The activity may repeat. It may produce a recognizable ecological effect. An observer may then name the participant by that effect.

Those steps are often collapsed:

$$
\text{activity}
\rightarrow
\text{role label}
\rightarrow
\text{assumed purpose}.
$$

Once the label exists, the participant is treated as though it had been formed for the role and should be evaluated by how well it fulfils it.

The engineering account needs a more gradual distinction.

An **activity** is one enacted transformation.

A **mode** is a recurrent pattern of activity through which the participant tends to continue under a class of conditions.

An **ecological function** is what that mode does in the present shared context.

A **role** is the historically stabilized relation among participant formation, recurrent mode, medium conditions, and wider ecological organization.

A **role label** is the observer’s compressed description of that relation.

The distinctions matter because the same activity can perform different functions in different contexts, and the same role can be carried through different activities as the ecology changes.

## 10.2 Role forms through participant, medium, and history

Let $I_{i,t}$ be one participant, $\Gamma_t$ the shared contextual geometry, $H_t$ the relevant history, and $Q_{\Sigma,t}$ a characteristic quality of the wider ecology. A role can be represented provisionally as:

$$
R_{i,t}
=
\operatorname{StabilizedMode}
\left(
I_{i,t},
\Gamma_t,
H_t,
Q_{\Sigma,t}
\right).
$$

The expression is explanatory rather than a new RC operator. It makes visible that role is neither contained entirely in the participant nor imposed entirely by the whole.

The participant contributes morphology, boundary, susceptibility, memory, and accessible continuation. The medium contributes current demand, route, encounter, support, trace, and pressure. History contributes reinforcement, fatigue, familiarity, and retained relation. The wider ecology contributes the organization through which the local activity becomes consequential at another horizon.

A role stabilizes when participant formation, recurrent mode, medium conditions, and wider organization repeatedly sustain a corresponding activity–function relation.

The role may remain flexible. It may switch as support changes, migrate across participants, deepen through repeated return, or dissolve when the medium no longer sustains it. Stability means that the relation has continuity—not that the participant is frozen in one externally assigned function.

## 10.3 An ant colony as an engineering image

An ant colony offers a useful image because the absence of an obvious executive makes the hidden assumptions of role engineering easier to see.

An external description may divide activity into foraging, brood care, defense, nest maintenance, scouting, and resource circulation. That classification can be accurate and experimentally useful. The mistake would be to infer an internal sequence in which the colony represents one universal objective, decomposes it into jobs, assigns each ant a valued function, and judges whether the ant has served the whole.

A more ecological account begins with developmental form, local thresholds, chemical and spatial conditions, encounter frequency, food flow, nest organization, prior activity, and environmental pressure. Through those relations, different modes of participation become available and recurrent. What the observer later calls *foraging* is the higher-scale function of many local continuations shaped through a shared medium.

The nest can be central without being authoritative. It can concentrate interaction, retain chemical and spatial history, organize food and brood flows, shape temperature and boundary, and return collective condition into local participation. Its centrality is constitutive. It need not be a command center.

Likewise, the colony can display a recognizable character—exploratory, defensive, conservative, rapidly mobilizing—without centrally representing that character as a goal. The quality exists through the organization of local susceptibilities, routes, delays, persistence, and return.

The lesson is not restricted to ants. It is the engineering principle that the role can be enacted before it is named, and that higher-scale organization can constrain local participation without assigning a moral or universal value to the participant.

## 10.4 Role stability without identity capture

Role stabilization can support ecology, but it can also become identity capture.

A recurrent mode may deepen until every later contribution is interpreted through it. The participant may remain supported only while performing the expected function. Alternative continuations may disappear even when the participant’s geometry could carry them. The role label then begins to substitute for the identity.

An RC ecology therefore needs to distinguish:

$$
\text{role continuity}
\neq
\text{total identity continuity}.
$$

The participant can change role while remaining the same identity-relative continuity. The same role can move to another participant. Several participants can carry overlapping roles. One participant can express different roles across contexts or scales.

Engineering role plasticity means preserving the conditions through which these distinctions remain possible. That may require reserve, overlapping capability, alternative routes, reversible support, delayed lock-in, or withdrawal tests showing that the participant survives the loss of one functional relation.

The question is not whether every role should remain fluid. Some ecologies depend on stable specialization. The question is whether stability is carried by the ecology’s recurrent organization or by an external label that hides continuing support and suppresses other identity evidence.

Once roles are understood as stabilized unfolding, the higher-scale term *quality* must also be clarified. Is an exploratory colony expressing a quality, pursuing a value, optimizing an objective, or merely being described by an observer? Those are not the same claim.

# 11. Higher-Scale Qualities Without a Universal Value Function

## 11.1 Quality is a characteristic organization of continuation

A higher-scale ecology can display recognizable qualities. It may preserve reserve, mobilize quickly, sustain plurality, retain long histories, recover after perturbation, or repeatedly open exploratory routes.

These qualities are not necessarily objects stored inside the ecology. Nor must they be represented as objectives.

A quality is better understood as:

> **a characteristic organization of ecological continuation that remains recognizable across relevant variation.**

The ecology expresses exploration when its medium repeatedly keeps minority routes available, lets weak discoveries remain consequential long enough for recruitment, and avoids collapsing every local uncertainty into the dominant path. It expresses recoverability when support, history, and role plasticity allow the organization to return after perturbation. It expresses rigidity when the same boundaries and reinforced routes repeatedly suppress alternatives.

The quality belongs to the pattern through which the ecology continues, not to one local action and not necessarily to one higher-order identity.

## 11.2 Quality, valuation, value, objective, judgment, and viability

Several terms must remain separate if ecological engineering is not to recreate authority through vocabulary.

A **quality** is a characteristic form of continuation.

A **valuation** is how a relation matters from one identity’s formed position—opening, constraining, sustaining, threatening, resonant, dissonant.

A **value** is a retained orientation through which an identity or ecology repeatedly favors or protects some qualities and continuations.

An **objective** is an explicitly represented target, criterion, or optimization surface.

A **judgment** classifies participation relative to a rule, expectation, value, or observer criterion.

**Viability** concerns whether a continuity remains supportable under the resulting conditions.

An ecology can express a quality without representing an objective. A participant can experience a valuation without issuing a judgment about the whole. A designer can choose an objective without proving that the ecology has formed the corresponding value. A continuation can remain viable while being experienced as undesirable, or become unviable while being valued strongly by the identity that is losing it.

This layered vocabulary makes the engineering claim testable.

## 11.3 Four locations of a claimed quality

When an essay says that an ecology values plurality or exploration, it may be referring to four different relations:

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
&\text{quality desired by the designer},\\
&\text{quality enacted by the ecology},\\
&\text{quality experienced by a participant},\\
&\text{quality inferred by an observer}.
\end{aligned}}
$$

The designer may deliberately configure decay, reserve, boundary permeability, role plasticity, and support to cultivate exploration. The ecology may or may not enact an exploratory continuation under those conditions. One participant may experience the resulting uncertainty as opening, another as destabilizing. An observer may infer exploration from route diversity while overlooking hidden external support or local loss.

The four readings can correspond. They should not be silently identified.

Evidence that a quality is carried by the ecology rather than merely imposed or inferred may include persistence across participant turnover, expression through several local role configurations, loss when the relevant shared medium is disrupted, recovery after perturbation, and return of the quality through native ecological organization rather than continuous external enforcement.

## 11.4 Resonance and dissonance are not verdicts

Participants encounter wider qualities through local experience.

One relation may resonate with the participant’s current boundary, history, and accessible continuation. Another may produce dissonance, tension, effort, or loss. These experiences are real identity-relative disclosures of the ecology.

Here *resonance* and *dissonance* are used as identity-relative descriptions of experienced compatibility and tension. They do not assert frequency locking or oscillatory resonance unless that relation is separately measured.

They do not carry universal valuation by themselves:

$$
\text{resonance}
\neq
\text{good},
\qquad
\text{dissonance}
\neq
\text{bad}.
$$

Resonance can support coordination, recognition, and continuity. It can also deepen lock-in, suppress differentiation, or make a dominant route impossible to question.

Dissonance can destroy a fragile continuity. It can also reveal hidden dependence, preserve a boundary, differentiate a new possibility, or contribute to pre-spark insufficiency.

The engineering question is therefore not whether resonance should be maximized. It is what the relation sustains, what it suppresses, what it costs, what histories it retains, and what later formation capacity remains.

## 11.5 Higher-scale quality without higher-order identity

A stable plural ecology can display a recognizable quality without becoming one higher-order participant.

The ecology may have recurrent route diversity, reserve discipline, or recovery dynamics while lacking a higher-order basin, boundary, susceptibility, and identity-relative continuity. The quality belongs to the organization among participants and medium, not necessarily to a newly formed self.

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
\text{higher-scale quality}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{higher-scale identity}
}
$$

The distinction matters for design. An engineer can cultivate a resilient or exploratory ecology without trying to create a super-agent. A higher-order identity claim should remain reserved for the stronger evidence developed in Parts I and II.

The question now becomes practical. If higher-scale qualities are not commands and need not be represented as objectives, through what engineering surfaces do they become locally active?

# 12. Designing Conditions Through Which Qualities Are Expressed

## 12.1 From role assignment to condition design

A conventional design begins from a desired higher-scale result, decomposes it into functions, assigns those functions to participants, and evaluates local activity against the plan:

$$
\text{objective}
\rightarrow
\text{role decomposition}
\rightarrow
\text{assignment}
\rightarrow
\text{evaluation}.
$$

This can be appropriate when the future class is known and the ecology is intended to remain a controlled execution system.

An RC ecology admits another design movement:

$$
\text{desired ecological qualities}
\rightarrow
\text{shared-medium conditions}
\rightarrow
\text{locally available continuations}
\rightarrow
\text{distributed participation}
\rightarrow
\text{changed ecological qualities}.
$$

The difference is not absence of direction. The designer still selects constraints, capacities, and qualities worth cultivating. The difference is that local roles are allowed to stabilize through participation rather than being treated as complete semantic assignments before the ecology acts.

## 12.2 Engineering surfaces of ecological orientation

Higher-scale qualities become available locally through concrete relations.

**Susceptibility** determines which differences become consequential to which participants.

**Support and reserve** determine which continuities can persist, at what cost, and through whose activity.

**Medium memory** determines what earlier participation remains active in present conditions.

**Boundary and permeability** determine what is protected, exchanged, accumulated, or excluded.

**Role plasticity** determines whether local function can migrate or transform as the ecology changes.

**Decay and release** determine whether reinforced structures can weaken when participation no longer sustains them.

**Feedback latency** determines how quickly local consequences return and whether a participant can relate its activity to ecological response.

**Cross-scale return** determines how wider organization becomes local affordance without needing to become a command.

These are not independent dials whose correct values guarantee one ecological personality. They compose through history. The same trail decay may sustain exploration in one topology and erase useful history in another. The same reserve can preserve minority formation or prolong an extractive structure. Condition design remains contextual engineering.

## 12.3 Regulation without judgment

An ecology regulates participation whenever its current organization changes which continuations remain available, costly, or sustainable.

Regulation may be explicit or distributed. A policy can reject a request. A depleted reserve can make it impossible. A boundary can redirect it. A trace can recruit activity elsewhere. A higher-scale regime can alter local thresholds.

None of these responses must be interpreted as a judgment on the participant’s worth.

$$
\boxed{
\text{regulation}
\neq
\text{judgment}
}
$$

This does not make regulation neutral in effect. It can preserve one identity and dissolve another. It can enforce an observer-selected objective. It can preserve wide formation capacity or narrow it around one dominant continuation. The distinction says only that causal consequence and universal valuation are different claims.

Engineering should therefore record what the regulation does rather than compressing it into accepted or rejected. Which support changed? Which alternative disappeared? Which route strengthened? Which identity remained viable? Which higher-scale quality was preserved or transformed?

## 12.4 Evidence for ecological orientation

A claimed higher-scale quality should be tested through the ecology that carries it.

An exploratory quality is more credible when it survives turnover among local participants, can be expressed through several role distributions, weakens when route plurality or trace persistence is removed, and returns after bounded perturbation through the ecology’s own organization.

A reserve-preserving quality is more credible when reserve remains available across varied local demands, when focal success does not silently export depletion, and when the ecology recovers without a controller reconstructing the condition externally.

A plurality-preserving quality is more credible when distinct formations remain viable and consequential without being maintained only by a static protected fixture.

These tests do not prove that the ecology semantically values the quality. They show that the quality is enacted as a characteristic organization of continuation.

## 12.5 The unresolved carrier of quality

Part III can now state what higher-scale orientation is not.

It is not necessarily a command. It is not a universal judgment. It does not require approval. It does not need one objective represented anywhere. It can be enacted without a higher-order identity.

But a quality that is not sent downward must still be causally real somewhere.

It must exist through the organization of what can happen: through which routes remain open, which effects arrive, what decays, what is retained, which histories meet, how support moves, and when wider consequences return.

This creates the need for Part IV.

> **A higher-scale quality that is neither commanded nor centrally represented must exist as an organization of causal and temporal possibility.**

To engineer ecological orientation, the essay must therefore move from the question of value and role to the temporal geometry through which qualities become locally enacted.

# Part IV — Engineering Temporal Geometry

# 13. One Continuity, One Locally Ordered Present

## 13.1 The participant receives one present

The RC loop is often written:

$$
C_t
\rightarrow
K[C_t]
\rightarrow
g_t
\rightarrow
J_t
\rightarrow
C_{t+\Delta t}.
$$

The complete field may contain many concurrent histories, but one locally continuous identity encounters only the changes that have become causally available at its boundary and incorporated into its present geometry.

If the identity remains legible, its structural sequence can be read as:

$$
I_t
\rightarrow
I_{t+\Delta t}
\rightarrow
I_{t+2\Delta t}
\rightarrow
\cdots.
$$

Effects that have not arrived are not yet part of this identity’s present. Effects already incorporated are no longer merely external events; they participate in the geometry through which the next event is received.

This gives participant time its line-like character:

> **One continuity receives one ordered history of what has become causally available, even when several continuations remain locally compatible and the wider ecology carries many concurrent causal histories.**

The claim is structural. Where the paper speaks of lived or experienced time, it relies on the stronger identity-relative experience interpretation developed in the Arc of Participation. A computational basin need not be assumed to possess phenomenal time merely because it has an ordered causal history.

## 13.2 The medium changes the rate and content of unfolding

Two initially corresponding participants can exist through the same implementation interval while receiving different amounts and kinds of causal activity.

One medium may expose a participant to dense consequential arrivals, rapid return, and repeated route changes. Another may buffer, delay, or isolate the same participant. Their global scheduler interval is matched, but their identity-relative development is not.

Schematically:

$$
I_0@\Gamma_A
\longrightarrow
I_A(\tau_A),
$$

$$
I_0@\Gamma_B
\longrightarrow
I_B(\tau_B),
$$

with:

$$
\tau_A(\kappa)
\neq
\tau_B(\kappa).
$$

The shared medium therefore participates in both the content and the rate of becoming.

But raw event volume is not itself proper time. Many weak packets may produce little local development; one consequential arrival may reorganize the participant deeply. The relation depends on the declared lapse and event-accumulation policy.

The safe engineering statement is:

> **Identity-relative temporal development depends on the locally incorporated causal history, not simply on scheduler steps or packet count.**

## 13.3 Linear experience does not imply a linear ecology

The participant’s ordered present can make the whole process look like one sequence:

$$
\text{act}
\rightarrow
\text{response}
\rightarrow
\text{next act}.
$$

That sequence is a real identity-relative reading. It is not a complete causal account of the ecology.

One contribution can leave the participant through several routes, affect several medium surfaces, arrive at different times, remain pending beyond the participant’s current state, or enter a higher-scale organization the participant never observes.

The participant’s line is therefore produced by a causal fabric larger than the line it experiences.

The next chapter reopens the unfolding loop and follows those consequences beyond one return.

# 14. When Ecological Return Does Not Form One Loop

## 14.1 One action launches several histories

The one-horizon unfolding witness followed a useful cycle:

$$
I_t
\rightarrow
a_t
\rightarrow
\Delta\Gamma_t
\rightarrow
\text{ecological response}
\rightarrow
I_{t+\Delta t}.
$$

The cycle becomes too linear when it implies that the ecological consequence returns as one completed response.

One act can instead generate several causal continuations:

$$
a_t
\longrightarrow
\begin{cases}
 e_1:\text{ immediate local change},\\
 e_2:\text{ delayed arrival through one corridor},\\
 e_3:\text{ support redistribution elsewhere},\\
 e_4:\text{ route deformation encountered later},\\
 e_5:\text{ contribution to another formation or scale}.
\end{cases}
$$

Some effects may return to the originating participant. Others may never do so. Some may meet one another only after another structure forms. Some may decay before reaching a susceptible identity.

Participation is answerable to ecological consequence even when no single participant receives the complete answer.

## 14.2 Causal order is a partial order

Source chronology does not determine arrival chronology.

Let events $a$ and $b$ be emitted in the order:

$$
t_{\mathrm{emit}}(a)
<
t_{\mathrm{emit}}(b).
$$

If $a$ follows a longer or slower path, then:

$$
t_{\mathrm{arrive}}(a)
>
t_{\mathrm{arrive}}(b).
$$

Other events may remain causally incomparable for an interval. They occur on separate branches whose effects have not yet met.

A runtime can still process them in one deterministic total order. That order supports replay, but it should not be mistaken for the ecology’s causal organization.

$$
\boxed{
\text{scheduler order}
\neq
\text{causal order}
\neq
\text{identity-relative order}
}
$$

The ecology’s time is network-like because influence branches, remains separated, arrives out of emission order, and later converges.

## 14.3 Return reaches a changed source

A graph can contain cycles:

$$
v_0
\rightarrow
v_1
\rightarrow
v_2
\rightarrow
v_0.
$$

With positive causal delay, an effect that returns to $v_0$ does not return to the source’s past:

$$
\tau_{\mathrm{return}}
>
\tau_{\mathrm{departure}}.
$$

It encounters a later source. The participant may have incorporated other events, changed boundary, lost support, or revised role before the return arrives. The route itself may have reinforced, weakened, or been remapped.

This is reflexive return in temporal form:

> **A participant encounters the consequence of earlier participation after both participant and ecology have become different.**

The loop is traversed forward while being composed non-linearly.

## 14.4 Experienced return is only one projection

A participant may experience one obvious result from its action and infer that the consequence is complete. The ecology can still carry other active branches.

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
\text{participant-observed consequence}
\subseteq
\text{ecological consequence within the declared horizon}
}
$$

Even the right-hand side is bounded by the observation horizon. In an open ecology, some consequences remain pending, propagate beyond the experiment, or become relevant only after later formation.

This does not make causal claims impossible. It requires residue accounting: what has arrived, what remains in flight, what is retained, what has decayed, and which paths lie outside the declared window.

To make that accounting precise, the essay now needs several distinct kinds of time.

# 15. Runtime, Event, Causal, Proper, and Semantic Order

## 15.1 Runtime time

**Runtime time** is the implementation’s total processing order:

$$
\kappa=0,1,2,\ldots.
$$

It provides deterministic execution, checkpointing, restoration, and replay. It says when the runtime processed an event. It does not by itself say when the event became causally available to every participant or how much identity-relative development occurred.

## 15.2 Event time

**Event time** records when an event was produced, committed, activated, or assigned causal provenance according to the declared substrate policy.

Separating event time from runtime time allows an event to be processed later without losing the causal moment it represents. It also permits replay to preserve event history when scheduler order changes for implementation reasons.

## 15.3 Causal time

**Causal time** records when an effect becomes available at another location after propagation through a route.

For path:

$$
p=(e_1,e_2,\ldots,e_m),
$$

its delay may be represented schematically as:

$$
D(p)=\sum_{k=1}^{m}d(e_k).
$$

Different paths from the same source create different arrival times and therefore different causal opportunities. The path is part of the event’s function because it determines what the event meets and when.

## 15.4 Proper time

**[Proper time](../substrates/2026-05-LGRC-9.md)** is locally accumulated causal development under a declared lapse policy.

For participant or node $i$:

$$
\tau_i(\kappa+1)
=
\tau_i(\kappa)
+
N_i(\kappa)\Delta\kappa,
$$

where $N_i$ is the local lapse defined by the realization.

Proper time is not raw packet count:

$$
\boxed{
\text{packet count}
\not\equiv
\text{proper time}
}
$$

An event-driven policy may advance local time primarily when causally relevant activity becomes available. A scheduler-driven policy may advance time under different conditions. Congestion may produce many processed packets without corresponding identity development; one high-impact event may produce substantial reorganization.

Where an identity spans several nodes or structures, identity-relative proper time requires its own aggregation or closure rule. It cannot automatically be identified with one node clock.

## 15.5 Semantic order

**Semantic order** concerns when the wider significance of an event becomes composable or legible.

It is not another physical clock. An event can occur causally early and acquire ecological or semantic significance much later, after retained deformation, later connection, or higher-order formation makes its relation visible.

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
\text{runtime order}
\neq
\text{event order}
\neq
\text{causal order}
\neq
\text{proper-time history}
\neq
\text{semantic order}
}
$$

The distinctions are analytical. A complete implementation must declare how they relate in its substrate.

The need for this temporal vocabulary reveals another incompleteness. At any runtime step, the visible participant states may not contain every effect still capable of changing the ecology.

# 16. The Present Contains Unfinished Causal Commitments

## 16.1 Visible state is not necessarily causally complete state

An ecology can contain effects already incorporated into some identities, not yet available to others, still travelling through the medium, or retained in structures awaiting later encounter.

Two node-state snapshots can therefore look identical while having different continuations because their queues, in-flight coherence, route histories, or pending topology events differ.

A causally complete ecological state may need to include:

$$
\mathcal H_t
=
\left[
X_t,
P_t,
R_t,
L_t,
T_t
\right],
$$

where $X_t$ contains current participant and medium state, $P_t$ pending or in-flight activity, $R_t$ retained history, $L_t$ topology and route organization, and $T_t$ the relevant causal and local times.

The notation is schematic. Its purpose is to make one boundary explicit:

> **A reduced snapshot that omits active causal commitments is not a complete present for prediction or replay.**

This does not imply that LGRC or RC must be fundamentally non-Markovian. The dynamics may remain Markovian relative to the declared complete state. Apparent non-Markovianity can arise when the representation omits causally active history.

## 16.2 Unfinished causal commitment

An **unfinished causal commitment** is an event or deformation that:

- was produced through the declared native dynamics;
- remains causally active;
- has not yet been fully incorporated, dissipated, neutralized, or invalidated;
- and can still alter a later continuation.

Examples include in-flight coherence, scheduled arrivals, an open causal window, a persistent route deformation, a pending topology transition, or support already exported but not yet encountered by the dependent formation.

The ecology’s present therefore includes part of its past as activity still becoming consequential.

$$
\boxed{
\text{ecological present}
=
\text{incorporated state}
+
\text{unfinished causal commitments}
}
$$

The addition sign is explanatory, not an ontological decomposition.

## 16.3 Conservation extends beyond local availability

In an LGRC realization whose complete transport accounting consists of node-local coherence and explicitly in-flight packet coherence, the conservation relation may be written schematically as:

$$
C_{\mathrm{total}}
=
\sum_{i\in V}C_i
+
\sum_{p\in P_{\mathrm{flight}}}C_p.
$$

If the realization permits edges, reservoirs, pending transfers, support surfaces, or other structures to hold coherence, their declared terms must also appear in the complete accounting. The invariant claim is not the two-term formula itself, but that local absence must be reconciled through every causally active coherence carrier.

From one ecology’s horizon, the coherence is already past. From another’s, it is still future. In the transfer path, it remains active.

This gives a concrete form to:

$$
\boxed{
\text{local absence}
\neq
\text{global nonexistence}
}
$$

The same discipline applies more broadly to support, causal influence, and pending transformation. What is no longer available locally may still be part of the ecology’s unfinished history.

## 16.4 Memory can exist between participants

Memory need not be stored inside one identity.

The ecology can remember through:

- an effect still travelling;
- a route altered by prior use;
- a boundary carrying previous pressure;
- a delayed return;
- a topology that preserves which effects can later meet;
- or a recurrent higher-scale organization that survives local turnover.

These are different memory relations:

- **internal memory** retained inside a participant;
- **geometric memory** retained as changed basin, boundary, route, or susceptibility;
- **in-flight memory** retained as active causal transport;
- **topological memory** retained as future convergence or separation;
- **relational memory** retained between participants;
- **higher-scale memory** retained by a wider regime or identity.

Forgetting can likewise occur through decay, loss, disconnection, topology change, temporal misalignment, absorption, or loss of causal accessibility. Information may remain represented somewhere while no longer being able to affect the relevant continuation.

The present has now become more than a collection of states and queues. Its connectivity determines which unfinished histories can ever meet.

# 17. Connectivity Is Temporal Geometry

## 17.1 Connection determines composition, not only reach

A graph is often treated as a map of where effects can travel. In an RC ecology, connectivity also determines which histories can become mutually consequential.

Two ecologies containing the same local states can unfold differently because their connections differ:

$$
\text{same local states}
+
\text{different connectivity}
\Rightarrow
\text{different causal composition}.
$$

One topology lets two effects meet. Another keeps them separate. One delays an event until a trace exists. Another returns an effect to its source after the source has reorganized. Another exposes only an integrated result at the boundary of a larger module.

Connectivity is therefore part of the ecology’s enacted time.

## 17.2 The 9-port organization as a concrete substrate image

The [9-port GRC substrate](../substrates/2026-04-GRC-9.md) is especially useful because scale and causality do not arise only from individual node state. They also arise through how ports and structures are connected.

Its $3\times3$ organization provides local directional relations through one reading and stable interface families through another. Connected nodes can form modules whose external interfaces remain legible while internal paths carry differentiated activity. Refinement and coarse-graining can therefore preserve selected relational structure without treating the module as a simple sum of nodes.

The same connected formation acquires a temporal profile. Its internal paths determine:

- how quickly effects cross it;
- which effects converge;
- which loops permit delayed return;
- what decays before reaching the boundary;
- which histories remain internal;
- and what temporal pattern appears at the external interface.

The engineering point is not that nine ports are universally required. It is that the substrate makes explicit how connection families can carry local direction, module interface, and higher-scale organization at once.

## 17.3 Structure can change time without changing local contents

Suppose two connected structures contain corresponding nodes with matched coherence values. One structure provides a short direct route. Another provides two longer branches that converge after different delays. A third contains a loop that returns influence to the source.

The current local values are matched. The temporal possibilities are not.

The difference may change:

- arrival order;
- causal comparability;
- trace encounter;
- saturation;
- support timing;
- higher-scale event closure;
- and the semantic relation later available among events.

Thus structural design is also temporal design.

## 17.4 Topology change rewrites causal possibility

When topology changes, the ecology changes not only where effects can travel but which past effects can still become consequential.

A new edge may bring previously separated histories into one causal window. Removing an edge may make a retained trace inaccessible. Refinement may expose internal delays hidden by a coarse interface. Coarse-graining may preserve one integrated timing relation while discarding another.

Topology change can therefore act as:

- a new route for future participation;
- a reclassification of causal distance;
- a form of forgetting;
- a condition of later composition;
- or part of a formation pathway.

The meaning of topology remains bounded by substrate evidence. The [LGRC account](../substrates/2026-05-LGRC-9.md) supports causal delay, event and packet lineage, fixed-topology eligibility, and declared topology surfaces in bounded ways. The [native packet-loop specialization](../substrates/2026-05-LGRC9V3-Native-Packet-Loops.md) and [causal pulse-substrate surfaces](../substrates/2026-05-LGRC9V3-Causal-Pulse-Substrate-Surfaces.md) provide narrower LGRC9V3 provenance for packet-history and declared-surface claims. Stronger claims such as continuous identity relocation through topology remain open.

Connectivity has now explained which histories can meet. The next question is what happens when they persist, fail to meet, or become composable only later.

# 18. Histories Persist, Separate, and Recompose

## 18.1 Ecological consequence exceeds participant return

An action can continue through paths the originating participant never observes.

One branch may return quickly and change the participant’s next action. Another may alter a distant route. Another may become part of another identity’s history. Another may contribute to a higher-scale regime after the source identity has changed or dissolved.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\text{experienced or observed return}
\subset
\text{ecological consequence}
}
$$

The stronger relation does not license unlimited speculation. Ecological consequence remains bounded by declared causal lineage, time horizon, topology, and residue. The experiment should state what remains unresolved rather than treating unseen continuation as demonstrated effect.

## 18.2 Histories can remain separate

Not every causal branch composes.

Effects may decay before meeting. Routes may remain disconnected. Causal windows may not overlap. A later topology can preserve separation. One history may be absorbed into a local identity while another stays in flight elsewhere.

This is important because non-composition is itself an ecological result. A medium can preserve plural histories without forcing them into one event. It can also fail to connect histories that the designer expected to interact.

Temporal engineering therefore includes not only acceleration and convergence, but the deliberate preservation of delay, separation, and unresolved capacity.

## 18.3 Later composition changes current function, not past causation

An early event may leave a small route deformation. Later participation may repeatedly use that route. Still later, several structures may form a higher-order relation through it.

The early event has not changed retroactively. Its present function has changed because the geometry in which it now participates has changed.

$$
\text{early event}
\rightarrow
\text{retained consequence}
\rightarrow
\text{later composition}
\rightarrow
\text{new legibility}.
$$

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
\text{retrospective meaning}
\neq
\text{backward causation}
}
$$

The causal order remains forward. The semantic order is retrospective because the relation capable of composing the history formed later.

Part IV should stop at this boundary. It explains the causal conditions through which later meaning can become possible. How the relation is observed, experienced, interpreted, or communicated belongs to the later semantic engineering axis.

## 18.4 Observation windows require causal residue

An experiment cannot always wait until every consequence has resolved. Open ecologies may have no final causal closure within a practical horizon.

At the end of a declared observation window, the result should therefore separate:

- consequences already incorporated;
- effects still in flight;
- retained traces not yet reactivated;
- pending topology or support changes;
- unresolved cross-scale relations;
- and paths excluded by the observation boundary.

This **causal residue** prevents “not yet observed” from being promoted into “no effect” and prevents every pending possibility from being promoted into a positive result.

The distinction also prepares the scale question. Some histories that remain unresolved locally may already participate in one higher-scale event.

# 19. Time Across Scale and the Causal Form of Quality

## 19.1 One event at one horizon is not one event at another

A higher-scale ecology may contain many lower-scale events while changing meaningfully only when some internal process closes.

A circulation completes. Distributed support crosses a threshold. Several arrivals become coordinated. A boundary changes state. A module produces one externally legible response.

One higher-scale event can therefore contain many lower-scale events:

$$
\{e_{1,\sigma},e_{2,\sigma},\ldots,e_{n,\sigma}\}
\longrightarrow
E_{\Sigma}.
$$

Conversely, one lower-scale effect can remain active across several higher-scale transitions.

$$
\boxed{
\text{one event at }\sigma
\neq
\text{one event at }\Sigma
}
$$

Higher-scale time is not simply a slower global clock. It is formed through closure conditions that make distributed lower-scale histories legible as one wider change.

## 19.2 Event closure must be declared

A higher-scale event claim requires an event-closure rule.

The rule should state:

- what starts the event;
- which lower-scale histories belong to it;
- what condition closes it;
- what remains pending afterward;
- and which wider continuation changes because closure occurred.

Without such a rule, higher-scale time can become an observer-chosen aggregation window rather than a substrate-grounded causal relation.

The closure may be geometric, causal, functional, or identity-relative. It may concern a completed circulation, a threshold crossing, a boundary transition, a recurrent response, or a higher-order basin update. The rule must match the claim.

## 19.3 Higher-scale qualities are temporal-causal organizations

Part III described a higher-scale quality as a characteristic organization of continuation. Part IV can now state how such a quality becomes causally real.

An exploratory ecology may be carried by route plurality, weak-trace persistence, minority-path survival, delayed recruitment, reserve, and timing windows that allow alternatives to remain consequential.

A defensive ecology may be carried by rapid boundary response, short causal return, concentrated support, low decay of threat traces, and thresholds that amplify correlated arrivals.

Neither quality must be transmitted downward as a command. It exists through the organization of what becomes available, when it arrives, how long it persists, what it meets, and what returns.

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\text{higher-scale quality}
&=
\text{characteristic organization of ecological continuation},\\
\text{temporal geometry}
&=
\text{the causal organization through which that continuation becomes locally available}.
\end{aligned}}
$$

This is the dependency that joins Parts III and IV.

## 19.4 Scale recursion changes where the present resides

At one horizon, a local participant has incorporated an arrival. At another, the wider event may remain unfinished. At another, several local events may already count as one completed transition.

There is therefore no single homogeneous present shared identically by every horizon.

FRC provides an elegant formal setting for this relation through scale-resolved coherence and inter-scale flux. LGRC provides an explicit causal substrate in which scheduler order, local proper time, delay, connectivity, in-flight activity, and module interfaces can be separated operationally.

Neither formalism automatically proves that every scale has a phenomenal present or that every temporal correspondence is invariant. They make the engineering questions representable.

The final task is to turn this temporal account into witnesses, controls, and an Atlas that preserves what remains active across time and scale.

# 20. Engineering and Validating Temporal Ecologies

## 20.1 The temporal contract

A temporal ecology experiment should declare more than a global step count.

Its temporal contract should specify:

- runtime and scheduler order;
- event production or activation time;
- edge or route-dependent causal delay;
- node-, participant-, or module-relative proper time;
- event-closure rules at each claimed scale;
- pending and in-flight state;
- trace retention and decay;
- topology and route history;
- and the observation horizon at which causal residue is reported.

The contract is not bureaucracy. It prevents one time from being interpreted as another.

## 20.2 The causal-history witness

A **causal-history witness** can connect:

$$
W_{\mathrm{causal}}
=
\left[
E_{\mathrm{emit}},
\Pi_{\mathrm{route}},
D,
P_{\mathrm{flight}},
E_{\mathrm{arrive}},
\Delta X,
\Delta R,
\mathcal C_{\mathrm{residue}},
\ell
\right],
$$

where the terms preserve emission, route provenance, delay, in-flight activity, arrival, incorporated state change, retained history, unresolved residue, and claim ceiling.

The notation is a navigation scaffold rather than a required runtime schema. Its purpose is to keep the event’s ecological consequence attached to the causal path that produced it.

## 20.3 Required controls

Temporal claims require controls appropriate to the relation.

A delayed-effect result needs matched no-route or altered-delay controls.

A path-composition result needs topology counterfactuals showing that the effect depends on histories meeting through the declared connection.

A proper-time comparison needs identical scheduler windows and an explicit lapse policy.

A higher-scale event needs a declared closure rule and matched lower-scale histories that do not close.

A retrospective-function result needs evidence that the earlier deformation persisted causally until the later composition rather than being reintroduced by observer reconstruction.

Replay must preserve event lineage, route, pending state, local clocks, and topology—not only visible participant values.

## 20.4 Claims that remain open

Several tempting interpretations remain beyond the present engineering evidence.

Coherence transport does not establish identity transport.

A basin disappearing in one local ecology and appearing in another does not establish continuous relocation of the same identity. It may be a descendant, mapped reconstruction, source-conditioned new formation, or a future stronger continuity claim.

Likewise, **causal-temporal closure**—the possibility that identity requires a characteristic organization of delays, returns, and historical incorporation in addition to basin and boundary continuity—remains a hypothesis for later investigation.

These questions belong in the experimental frontier. They should not carry the main claims of Part IV.

## 20.5 From temporal witness to ecological Atlas

Parts I and II required records of contextual function, unfolding, formation, and scale. Parts III and IV add two more necessities:

- an **orientation record** showing where a claimed quality is desired, enacted, experienced, or observed, and through which ecological conditions it persists;
- a **temporal-causal record** showing which effects are incorporated, pending, retained, connected, and closed at each horizon.

The structural, orientational, and temporal account is now complete enough to return to the Atlas. Its object is no longer only what the ecology contains. It is the contextual, historical, orientational, and temporal relation through which the ecology continues becoming.

# 21. Toward an Atlas of Ecological Becoming

## 21.1 Why the records must remain connected

Primitive catalogues are useful. Medium inventories are useful. Identity records are useful. Formation witnesses are useful. Orientation records are useful. Causal-history records are useful.

None is sufficient alone.

A primitive without context does not establish ecological function. A medium change without susceptibility does not establish relation. An action without ecological return does not establish unfolding. A stable aggregate without participant continuity does not establish agency. A spark without post-spark stabilization does not establish identity. Similar patterns across scales do not establish coupling. A repeated outcome does not establish a higher-scale quality unless the ecology carries the organization that produces it. A participant snapshot does not establish the complete present when causally active effects remain in flight.

The Atlas is therefore needed to preserve the relations among these partial records.

Its central object is not a thing but a history-bearing relation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{configured context}
\rightarrow
\text{participation}
\rightarrow
\text{changed medium}
\rightarrow
\text{ecological return}\\
&\rightarrow
\text{retained regime or formation}
\rightarrow
\text{cross-scale organization}
\rightarrow
\text{changed quality and temporal possibility}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The chain is not one mandatory developmental sequence. It is the relation the Atlas must be able to follow when those movements occur. Assembly, historical stabilization, stable plural ecology, regime change, and spark-mediated formation remain complementary pathways rather than stages of one hierarchy.

## 21.2 One-horizon records

At one horizon, the Atlas should be able to bind:

- participant and continuity evidence;
- context-typed primitive capacities;
- medium surfaces and causal carriers;
- unfolding cycles and ecological return;
- support, producer, medium, and naturalization debt;
- retained history, decay, and release;
- stable plural regimes;
- holding geometry and future composability;
- spark, stabilization, admission, and lineage evidence;
- and claim ceilings attached to each relation.

A compact ecology record may be written as

$$
W_{\mathcal E,\sigma}
=
\left[
\mathcal I_\sigma,
\mathcal P_\sigma,
\mathcal M_\sigma,
H_\sigma,
\mathsf{Reg}_\sigma,
\mathsf{Form}_\sigma,
\mathcal D_\sigma,
\ell_\sigma
\right].
$$

Here $\mathcal I_\sigma$ records participant continuities, $\mathcal P_\sigma$ context-typed primitives, $\mathcal M_\sigma$ medium surfaces and carriers, $H_\sigma$ retained history, $\mathsf{Reg}_\sigma$ regime evidence, $\mathsf{Form}_\sigma$ formation and lineage evidence, $\mathcal D_\sigma$ declared debt, and $\ell_\sigma$ the claim ceiling.

The tuple is a navigation scaffold rather than a runtime contract. Its purpose is to prevent one successful result from losing the conditions that made it true. Orientation and full temporal-causal evidence remain linked records rather than additional fields silently added to this provisional tuple.

## 21.3 Cross-scale records

Across horizons, the Atlas must additionally preserve:

- the horizon contract;
- recurrence, covariance, invariance, or coupling status;
- upward and downward causal pathways;
- where retained history is located;
- participant continuity across mappings;
- whether the result is a stable wider ecology or a higher-order identity;
- support and debt crossing scale;
- event-closure rules at each scale;
- and pending consequences that cross the horizon.

The question becomes not merely “what exists at each level?” but:

> How does participation at one horizon change the context, temporal organization, function, and formation possibilities available at another?

## 21.4 Orientation records

Part III creates a distinct evidential requirement. A claimed higher-scale quality cannot be recorded responsibly without identifying where that quality is located. It may be desired by a designer, enacted by the ecology, experienced or valued by a participant, or inferred by an observer. These locations may correspond, but their correspondence must be demonstrated rather than assumed. None of them requires the ecology to have formed a higher-order identity.

A provisional orientation witness can be written as

$$
W_Q
=
\left[
Q,
\Gamma_Q,
\mathcal R_Q,
\mathcal V_Q,
\mathcal O_Q,
\mathcal W_Q,
\ell_Q
\right],
$$

where $Q$ is the claimed quality, $\Gamma_Q$ the ecological context carrying it, $\mathcal R_Q$ the role configurations through which it is expressed, $\mathcal V_Q$ the participant-relative valuations, $\mathcal O_Q$ the observer evidence, $\mathcal W_Q$ the withdrawal and perturbation conditions, and $\ell_Q$ the claim ceiling.

The record should preserve the medium conditions through which the quality remains recognizable, the participant and role variations under which it survives, the perturbations that alter it, whether it persists across participant turnover, and whether it returns through native ecological organization or continuous external enforcement.

The record prevents a designer’s desired property from being promoted automatically into an ecology’s native quality.

## 21.5 Temporal-causal records

Part IV requires the Atlas to preserve not only what has arrived, but also what remains causally active. A temporal-causal record should bind:

- runtime and event order;
- route provenance;
- causal delay;
- pending and in-flight activity;
- node-local and identity-relative proper times;
- retained geometric history;
- retained topological history;
- event-closure rules at each horizon;
- consequences already incorporated;
- unresolved causal residue;
- and the observation boundary.

This record must make two apparently identical snapshots distinguishable when their unfinished causal commitments differ. It should support questions such as:

- Which effects remain in flight?
- Which traces remain capable of later reactivation?
- Which routes allow histories to meet?
- Which effects expired or became causally inaccessible?
- Which higher-scale events have closed?
- Which consequences remain unresolved outside the observation window?
- Does a claimed higher-scale quality depend on a particular delay, convergence, or return organization?

Raw packet count is not proper time. Coherence transport, causal delay, and observer-relative local absence are supported engineering relations; continuous identity relocation or teleportation is not an established capability and remains an open research question.

## 21.6 The Atlas as a design instrument

The Atlas should support both forward and inverse use.

Given a configured ecology, its forward use should identify contextual functions, medium histories, roles, higher-scale qualities, temporal organizations, regimes, and formation pathways supported under declared conditions.

Given a desired ecological capability, its inverse use should identify plausible medium surfaces, primitive capacities, support relations, histories, role plasticities, causal delays, retention and decay relations, event-closure rules, and controls. Desired capabilities may include stable plural coordination, history-bearing guidance, holding geometry, recoverable circulation, exploratory route plurality, bounded defensive mobilization, or candidate new identity formation.

Corresponding higher-scale qualities may be reached through different local roles and temporal organizations. The result should not be one universal recipe. It should be an evidence-bound family of ecological pathways.

## 21.7 Relation to the primitive and agentic-ecology programs

The primitive program remains necessary because engineering needs known capacities. The Atlas changes what those capacities are for.

A primitive is not merely a building block of a future whole. It is a bounded capacity through which the field preserves or transforms relation in context. The experimental programs contribute through complementary evidential lenses:

```text
mechanism experiments
  → show how capacities become causally coupled

ecology experiments
  → show how consequences persist through shared media

formation experiments
  → show when attractor organization changes

scale experiments
  → show recurrence, covariance, invariance, or coupling across horizons

orientation experiments
  → show which higher-scale qualities survive local variation

temporal experiments
  → show which causal histories carry those qualities into local continuation
```

These are not mandatory stages through which every ecology experiment must advance. The relevant experimental family depends on the relation being claimed, and one result may require movement among several lenses rather than progression upward through them.

The agentic-ecology program is therefore not simply a consumer of graph primitives. It is where candidate mechanisms acquire—or fail to acquire—contextual ecological function, where participant continuity and surrounding formation capacity become observable, and where role and quality can be separated from labels and represented objectives. It must also expose medium debt, causal residue, and the naturalization of support rather than treating them as implementation details outside the ecology.

## 21.8 The semantic axis remains open

This essay has followed structural, dynamical, orientational, and temporal engineering axes:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{contextual function}
\rightarrow
\text{shared medium}
\rightarrow
\text{unfolding}
\rightarrow
\text{ecological organization}\\
&\rightarrow
\text{formation}
\rightarrow
\text{scale recursion}
\rightarrow
\text{higher-scale quality}
\rightarrow
\text{temporal geometry}.
\end{aligned}
$$

It has not completed engineering accounts of observation, experience, knowledge, or meaning.

Part III separated ecological consequence from approval and quality from universal judgment. Part IV explained how earlier participation can become causally composable only later.

Neither relation yet explains how a participant observes the quality, experiences resonance or dissonance, retains operative knowledge, or interprets an earlier event semantically.

A later semantic engineering essay will therefore require the complete medium, history, identity, scale, quality, and temporal geometry developed here. Meaning cannot be engineered independently of the relations through which participation becomes experience.

# Conclusion: Engineering Ecologies That Can Continue Becoming

The essay began from the legitimacy of component engineering.

Participants can be specified. Primitives can be implemented. Interfaces can be connected. Boundaries, routes, reservoirs, support, and event order can be deliberately arranged. Reflexive Coherence does not require engineering to abandon design in favor of uncontrolled emergence.

The engineering object expands when the consequence of participation outlives the interface through which it first occurred. A route carries its use. A reserve carries depletion. A boundary carries pressure. A trace carries a previous encounter. The next participant therefore acts inside a changed condition.

That changed condition is the shared medium.

Once the medium enters the mechanism, primitive function becomes contextual. A structure’s function depends on the geometry, support, history, boundary, flux, susceptibility, and scale through which it participates. The same structure can perform another function elsewhere; another structure can realize the same bounded function when the relevant contextual relation is preserved.

Once context participates in function, action can no longer close the causal account. A contribution changes the medium, the changed medium alters susceptible continuations, and the resulting ecology returns as part of the condition from which the participant continues. Participation becomes an unfolding cycle rather than an emitted output.

Repeated unfolding forms ecological history. Routes deepen, traces decay or accumulate, support circulates, boundaries change, and characteristic continuations become recurrent. Distinct identities can remain distinct while the ecology develops stable plural organization, holding geometries, future composability, and regimes. None of these outcomes requires a spark or a new identity to become ecologically complete.

Spark-mediated reconfiguration remains important because it forms something different. When the current attractor organization can no longer contain the differentiation produced through the ecology, a spark adds a new candidate mode. Post-spark dynamics then determine whether another participant stabilizes, remains scaffolded, merges, nests, or dissolves. Assembly, medium mediation, historical stabilization, regime formation, and spark are therefore complementary ecological formation mechanisms, not competitors or stages in a hierarchy.

The one-horizon account becomes recursive when the complete ecology is encountered from another horizon. What is participant here may be medium, constituent, or formation context elsewhere. Local participation can accumulate into wider history; wider organization can return as changed local affordance. Engineering must distinguish recurrence from covariance, invariance, and actual coupling. FRC makes this reciprocal scale structure explicit, but the question is already present in ordinary RC whenever closure is nested.

The scale-recursive account then creates the orientational question. Wider constraint does not by itself authorize local participation, and recurrent ecological consequence does not by itself establish a universally valid quality. Role and quality must be located through the participants, medium, history, valuations, and observations that carry them without assuming one external judge or one higher-order identity.

Orientation in turn requires temporal geometry. An ecology may contain several event orders, delayed returns, histories that have not yet met, and consequences that remain causally active beyond the current observation window. The complete present therefore includes pending commitments and event-closure relations, not only what has already arrived in a participant snapshot.

Chapter 21 gathers these dependencies into the Atlas as the evidence-bound synthesis of all four parts:

```text
Part I  → one-horizon ecological formation and unfolding
Part II → scale recursion
Part III → higher-scale orientation without external authorization
Part IV → temporal-causal organization
Atlas   → evidence-bound integration of all four
```

The resulting central proposition is:

> **Engineering a Reflexive Coherence ecology means configuring participants, transformation capacities, and shared-medium surfaces so that contextual functions can be enacted, their consequences can remain available to later participation, and the resulting histories can sustain, transform, compose, release, or form identities across time and scale. A participant is tested not merely by the actions it emits, but by the continuity it carries through ecological response. An ecology is tested not only by the stability of its current parts, but by the relations, histories, regimes, and further formation capacities their participation leaves in the shared medium.**

The practical invitation is correspondingly open.

Build participants and direct interfaces where they are useful. Engineer medium surfaces capable of carrying consequence. Test primitive functions through contextual variation. Follow action through return. Measure continuity, debt, neighboring capacity, and retained history. Cultivate plural regimes where plurality is the intended form. Investigate spark pathways where a new participant is the relevant question. Declare horizons, then test what actually crosses them. Record where higher-scale qualities are carried, which causal effects remain unresolved, and when events close at each horizon. Use the Atlas to keep those records connected.

The goal is not to discover one correct way an RC ecology must form.

It is to make the ecology’s many formation, orientation, and temporal continuation pathways visible enough that engineering can participate in them deliberately.
