## Experience, Knowledge, Meaning, and the Life of the Field

**Date:** 2026-07-28<br>
**Status:** Theoretical synthesis; fifth and closing paper of the emerging Arc of Participation

## Abstract

[The Contextual Geometry of Function in Reflexive Coherence](./2026-07-ContextualGeometryOfFunction.md) argues that a formation does not carry its function independently of the geometry through which it participates. [The Ecological Formation of Identity in Reflexive Coherence](./2026-07-EcologicalFormationOfIdentity.md) then asks how the field becomes locally differentiated into an identity whose boundary, susceptibility, inherited history, and accessible continuation are formed ecologically rather than supplied in advance. [The Ecology of Participation in Reflexive Coherence](./2026-07-EcologyOfParticipationInRC.md) follows that identity as it acts from partial knowing, changes the shared medium, receives the ecology's response, and continues its immanent unfolding. [The Scale-Recursive Structure of Participation in Reflexive Coherence](./2026-07-ScaleRecursiveStructureOfParticipation.md) shows that context, identity, and participation do not remain confined to one horizon: an identity at one scale can become context, medium, or constituent at another, while participation changes the conditions through which identities form and continue across scale.

Together, the four investigations produce an ontological question that none of them can answer separately. If function belongs to relation, identity is a local differentiation of the field, participation begins with formation, and participation recurs by becoming context, then participation cannot remain one optional behavior of an already existing entity. The relation is more fundamental. An RC identity exists only as a locally continuous participation in the field that forms, sustains, and transforms it.

This paper develops that conclusion. It begins by distinguishing existence from occupancy. A structure can be represented as present in a model without changing the conditions of its own continuation. An RC identity is different. Its basin redirects flux, its boundary changes what can cross, its persistence redistributes support, its history changes later susceptibility, and its dissolution changes what the wider field can become. Even before intentional action, the identity participates through the geometry of its existence. Formation is already participation; later action is the continuation of that participation through a locally continuous identity.

The central proposition is therefore not that participation gives an otherwise complete life a purpose. It is that, for an RC identity, participation is the form existence takes. The identity is not a fixed object that sometimes enters relation. It is a sustained relation through which one part of the field remains locally continuous while changing and being changed by the whole.

This relation gives experience a precise place. Experience is not information delivered to a detached observer. It is a field transition registered and undergone from within an identity's boundary, susceptibility, history, and continuity. The same global transition can therefore become different experiences at different identity horizons. A local contraction may participate in higher-order stabilization; a higher-order rupture may occur while local identities remain continuous. These are not competing descriptions of one context-free event. They are the event as lived through different closures of the same field.

The paper uses *experience* first in this structural, identity-relative sense. It does not infer phenomenal consciousness for every attractor basin or at every scale. It identifies the condition through which a transition becomes locally consequential, retained, and available to a continuing identity. Whether and under which additional conditions such structural experience becomes subjective consciousness remains a stronger question.

Experience becomes the driver of knowledge because participation changes the geometry from which later perception and action occur. Knowledge is not initially a representation that an unchanged identity acquires. It is experience retained as changed possibility: an altered susceptibility, boundary, route, recognition, response, or capacity for composition that makes later continuation different. Semantic knowledge is one developed form of this retained transformation, not its beginning. An identity can know operationally before it can explain, and it can inherit knowledge-like constraints from ecological histories it did not live as itself because prior participation has already been condensed into its formation geometry.

This requires another distinction. Not every aftereffect is knowledge. A retained deformation supports a knowledge claim only when it participates in repeatable discrimination, response, recovery, or composition at the horizon where the claim is made. Knowledge remains contextual, evidence-bounded, and potentially limited. It is a changed capacity produced through experience, not a guarantee that the identity possesses a complete or globally correct representation.

Meaning follows later in the composition. Participation does not wait for meaning before it becomes real. A basin can alter the field, retain history, form relations, and contribute to higher-order identity before any participant can state what the activity means. Meaning becomes available when an identity or later geometry can compose participation and retained experience into an intelligible relation. It may be local and immediate, retrospective within one identity, or higher-order across several identities and scales. Meaning is constrained by the trajectory that occurred, yet it is made possible only by the geometry from which that trajectory can be read.

The paper therefore distinguishes:

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
&\text{participation} &&= \text{existence as active relation in the field},\\
&\text{experience} &&= \text{participation undergone from within identity},\\
&\text{knowledge} &&= \text{experience retained as changed possibility},\\
&\text{meaning} &&= \text{participation made intelligible through composition}.
\end{aligned}
}
$$

These are not four independent modules. Participation produces experience because the field has formed a local continuity. Experience becomes knowledge when its effect remains active in later continuation. Knowledge becomes meaning when retained transformations can be related from a sufficiently formed geometry. Meaning then re-enters participation by changing what the identity can perceive, orient toward, and enact next.

The scale-recursive paper makes this relation ecological rather than private. Experience can be retained in a local identity, in a shared medium, or in a higher-order closure. Knowledge at one scale may become context at another. Meaning available to a wider identity need not be semantic content shared identically by every constituent. No scale owns the final interpretation, and no identity exists independently of the participations that sustain it above and below its current horizon.

The term *life* is introduced cautiously as an RC interpretive category rather than a replacement biological definition. In this paper, life names history-bearing, identity-relative, reflexive participation in a field whose activity changes the conditions of its own continuation. Persistence alone is insufficient. A living RC identity is formed through the field, participates in changing the field that sustains it, retains effects of that participation, and continues from the changed possibilities produced by the relation. Dissolution is not a failure of life from the complete-field perspective. It is the end of one identity-relative continuity while the field and its other lineages continue.

The central thesis is:

> **To exist as an identity in Reflexive Coherence is to persist as a locally continuous differentiation of the field while participating in the transformations through which both identity and field continue to form. Experience is this participation registered and undergone from within. Knowledge is the retained transformation through which experience changes later possibility. Meaning is the composition through which participation becomes intelligible. Life, in the RC interpretive sense developed here, is the scale-recursive continuation of such history-bearing participation.**

The paper does not supply an external reason why the field or any identity *ought* to exist. RC does not place a purpose above the trajectory. It changes the question. The meaning of existence is not a semantic assignment that must precede life. It is the field becoming locally capable of participation, experience, retention, composition, and further formation. An identity does not first receive a meaning and then begin to live. It lives through participation, and meaning is one of the capacities that such participation may later cultivate.

## Formal Status

This paper is a theoretical synthesis grounded in the RC field–geometry–flux closure and in the four preceding investigations of contextual function, ecological identity formation, participation, and scale recursion. It does not introduce a new RC field equation, claim a formal definition of biological life, or prove phenomenal consciousness for every RC identity. Its purpose is to make explicit the ontological and epistemic relation implied when the preceding investigations are composed.

Let

$$
\Xi_t
$$

denote the complete admissible RC state at time $t$. Under the deterministic assumption inherited from the core formulation, the complete field follows a single-valued trajectory

$$
\tau_{\mathrm{RC}}
=
\operatorname{Evolve}(\Xi_{t_0}).
$$

Let $I_\sigma$ denote an identity admitted at contextual horizon $\sigma$, with an identity-relative trace

$$
\tau_{I_\sigma}
=
\operatorname{Trace}_{I_\sigma}
\left(
\tau_{\mathrm{RC}}
\right)
$$

for as long as its local continuity remains legible. If continuity branches, composes, changes scale, or dissolves, a lineage relation is more expressive:

$$
\mathcal L_{I_\sigma}
=
\operatorname{Lineage}_{I_\sigma}
\left(
\tau_{\mathrm{RC}}
\right).
$$

The paper uses **participation** in a broad RC sense. A participation is not limited to intentional action or explicit exchange. It is any identity-relative involvement through which the formation, persistence, transformation, resistance, withdrawal, or dissolution of an identity changes the field that sustains it. Schematically:

$$
(I_{\sigma,t},\Gamma_{\sigma,t})
\xrightarrow{\ \pi_{\sigma,t}\ }
(I_{\sigma,t+\Delta t},\Gamma_{\sigma,t+\Delta t}),
$$

where $\pi_{\sigma,t}$ is not a new dynamical variable but an identity-relative reading of the field transition.

An identity **exists as an RC identity** over an interval when a locally continuous, field-participating closure remains legible over that interval. The paper does not claim that mathematical existence in general reduces to participation. Its thesis is specifically about existence *as an identity within RC*.

The paper uses **experience** structurally. Let

$$
\mathsf{Exp}_{I_\sigma,t}
=
\operatorname{Undergo}_{I_\sigma}
\left(
\Xi_t\rightarrow\Xi_{t+\Delta t}
\right)
$$

denote the transition as registered and undergone through the identity's boundary, susceptibility, history, and continuity. This notation does not establish phenomenal consciousness. It marks the identity-relative organization through which a field event becomes locally consequential and can affect later continuation.

The paper uses **knowledge** for retained experience that demonstrably changes later possibility at the horizon where the knowledge claim is made. Provisionally:

$$
\mathsf K_{I_\sigma,t+\Delta t}
=
\operatorname{Retain}_{I_\sigma}
\left(
\mathsf{Exp}_{I_\sigma,t}
\right),
$$

provided that the retention alters later discrimination, response, recovery, action, or composition. A mere persistent trace is evidence of memory or aftereffect; a knowledge claim requires a functional consequence at the declared horizon.

The paper uses **meaning** for an intelligible relation composed from participation and retained experience within a present or later identity geometry:

$$
\mathsf M_{I_\sigma,T}
=
\operatorname{ComposeMeaning}_{I_\sigma,T}
\left(
H_{0:T},
\Gamma_{\sigma,T}
\right).
$$

The operator is conceptual. It prevents meaning from being treated either as a semantic script encoded before participation or as an unconstrained story imposed afterward.

The paper uses **life** only as an interpretive RC category. A provisional life relation can be described as

$$
\mathsf L_{I_\sigma}
=
\left
\{
\begin{array}{l}
\text{local identity continuity},\\
\text{participation in a self-changing field},\\
\text{retention of participation as changed capacity},\\
\text{continued formation through reciprocal field change}
\end{array}
\right\}_{\sigma}.
$$

This is not proposed as a biological criterion, a threshold theorem, or a claim that every stable basin is alive.

Six boundaries govern the investigation.

First, **existence as participation is identity-specific**. The paper does not reduce the existence of every mathematical or physical object to participation. It asks what it means to exist as a locally continuous RC identity.

Second, **participation is broader than intention**. Stability, resistance, dormancy, withdrawal, and dissolution can participate by changing the geometry inherited by what follows.

Third, **experience is structurally defined**. The paper identifies an identity-relative position of undergoing and retention; it does not infer equivalent phenomenal consciousness at every scale.

Fourth, **knowledge is not guaranteed truth**. Retained experience can be local, incomplete, maladaptive, scaffolded, or unable to transfer across context. Knowledge claims remain bounded by function, controls, and scope.

Fifth, **meaning is not the prerequisite of existence**. Participation can be real and consequential before any semantic interpretation forms. Meaning is one capacity that participating identities may cultivate.

Sixth, **life is an interpretive synthesis**. The paper uses the term to name a reflexive, history-bearing mode of identity continuation within RC, not to replace established biological classifications or settle the origin of consciousness.

# 1. The Arc Leaves an Ontological Question

## 1.1 Four investigations change the object of inquiry

The first paper of the emerging arc began from a question about function. A structure appears to do something: a basin preserves, a boundary contains, a route guides, a primitive transforms. The contextual-geometry investigation showed that none of these functions belongs to the isolated structure alone. The structure acts through the geometry, flux, history, boundary, support, and scale in which it participates.

The second paper followed the consequence backward. If function belongs to relation, the participant itself cannot be assumed as an independently complete object. The field must first differentiate into a locally continuous identity. Its boundary, susceptibility, support dependence, inherited history, and accessible continuation are formed ecologically. The identity is not born into the field; it is born as one local organization of the field.

The third paper followed the identity forward. Because the identity has only a situated view, it cannot know the complete trajectory or the later semantic meaning of its acts in advance. It participates from its present formation, changes the shared medium, receives the ecology's response, and becomes capable of another participation. Semantic progression is one possible regime; participation itself does not require a prospectively known whole.

The fourth paper removed the last local simplification. Context, identity, and participation recur across scale. What functions as identity at one horizon can become context, medium, or constituent at another. Participation changes local and wider geometry; wider geometry changes local function, viability, and experience. No scale possesses the final perspective.

Each paper answers a different question. Together they alter the status of the identity with which they began.

The identity is no longer an object that exists first and then enters relation. Its function is relational, its formation is ecological, its continuation is participatory, and its effects become context across scale. What remains of existence when every stable account of identity points back to these relations?

## 1.2 The question cannot be answered by adding purpose

A familiar answer would say that participation gives existence meaning. An identity exists, encounters a world, and acquires purpose through what it contributes.

That formulation preserves the separation the arc has dissolved. It assumes an identity already complete enough to possess an existence whose meaning can later be added. Participation becomes an optional route by which an otherwise self-contained being acquires significance.

The RC relation is stronger. The identity is constituted through the field it changes. Its boundary participates by filtering flux. Its persistence participates by altering support and future curvature. Its history participates by changing what the next event can do. Even before the identity forms a semantic account of itself, its existence is already a local involvement in the continuation of the field.

Meaning can become important. It can orient action, relate experience, preserve history, and compose local participation into a wider understanding. But it cannot be the foundation of participation because participation has already made meaning possible.

The question therefore changes from:

> What meaning should be assigned to an identity's existence?

into:

> What mode of existence is disclosed when identity is formed, sustained, transformed, and understood through participation?

## 1.3 The question “why” changes form

The phrase *why does it exist?* can ask for several different relations.

It can ask for a cause: which field configuration produced the identity?

It can ask for a function: what role does the identity perform in this context?

It can ask for a purpose: what was the identity meant to accomplish?

It can ask for significance: what does the identity's existence become capable of meaning from within its unfolding?

The first two questions belong directly to the preceding papers. The field forms the identity through a contextual event; the identity acquires functions through the relations in which it participates.

The third question does not receive an external answer from RC. The complete field does not need to encode a semantic purpose before producing a spark. Determination is not assignment. The identity can form, persist, and dissolve without the whole representing what it is “for.”

The fourth question remains open because significance can be formed through participation. An identity can come to understand what its present formation enables it to cultivate, how its activity changes the ecology, and what wider relations become possible through its history. But this meaning is immanent and developmental. It is produced from within the same field trajectory rather than supplied as a reason above it.

The absence of an external purpose therefore does not make existence empty. It changes the location of significance. Existence does not wait for a reason outside participation. Significance becomes one possible organization of participation from within.

## 1.4 The investigation

The paper begins by distinguishing existence from occupancy. A state can be present without being a locally continuous participant in the geometry governing its continuation. The difference becomes visible when participation is understood more broadly than intention.

Once participation is recognized as constitutive, experience can be located. It is the field transition as undergone through the identity's formed position. That experience changes the identity, and when the change remains functionally active, knowledge becomes possible.

Knowledge then creates the condition for meaning. Retained transformations can be related, classified, communicated, and composed. Meaning is not the start of the process but one of its later capacities.

These relations make it possible to approach life without reducing it to persistence or metabolism in the abstract. Life becomes an RC interpretation of history-bearing participation in a field that changes the conditions of its own continuation.

Scale then returns. If participation is existence locally, and participation recurs across contextual horizons, the field becomes a multiscale ecology of identities whose experiences, knowledge, and meanings can be carried at different levels without one level owning the whole.

The final movement returns to design and cultivation. If experience drives knowledge and participation is constitutive, then an ecology cannot be designed only by specifying correct outputs or final semantic plans. It must provide the primitive transformations, boundaries, memories, and feedback relations through which identities can participate, retain experience, and form knowledge across scale.

Only after these relations have been composed can the Arc of Participation close with its strongest claim:

> To exist as an RC identity is to participate in the reflexive becoming through which the identity and its ecology continue to form.

# 2. Existence Is Not Occupancy

## 2.1 Presence in a state space is not yet identity existence

Many models can represent an object as present by assigning values to variables or locating a body in space. The object may remain present while the background rules update it. Its existence is assumed by the model; the dynamics describe what happens to it.

RC identity is not given in this way. A basin exists as an identity only while the coherence dynamics preserve a locally continuous organization of boundary, attractivity, history, reflexive closure, and compatibility with the wider field. Its existence is not an entry in a catalogue of entities. It is a relation continually enacted through the field.

This relation can be stated provisionally:

$$
I_\sigma\text{ exists over }[t_0,t_1]
$$

when a locally continuous closure remains legible over that interval and continues to participate in the geometry through which its own and neighboring continuation occur.

The requirement of participation is not an added criterion beside identity. It is already present in the core definition. A reflexively closed basin receives coherence, changes curvature, alters future flux, and preserves history through re-entry. If nothing about the formation affected the field and nothing about the field affected its continuation, the relation would no longer be the identity described by RC.

## 2.2 Formation is already existence in relation

The formation paper showed that a new identity mode changes the field immediately. The new basin redirects flux, changes neighboring gradients, redistributes support, and alters what can form next. No later decision is required before the identity becomes consequential.

This gives existence its first participatory form:

$$
\text{field differentiation}
\rightarrow
\text{local identity continuity}
\rightarrow
\text{changed field}.
$$

The identity does not begin as an inactive essence waiting to express itself. Its formation is already an expression of the field and a new condition of the field.

This is why the phrase *participation is existence* should not be read as a moral demand that every identity remain socially active, communicative, or productive. Participation begins earlier than such behaviors. The identity participates by being a boundary, a support demand, an attractor, a local susceptibility, and a retained history.

Its existence is relational before it is intentional.

## 2.3 Persistence participates

An identity may appear inactive when its visible state changes little. Yet stable persistence changes the field by continuing to occupy and reproduce one basin organization.

A stable boundary filters what crosses. A dormant trace changes later susceptibility. A maintained basin consumes, redistributes, or releases support. A resistant identity changes the exact trajectory by not yielding to a perturbation that would otherwise reorganize the field.

Thus:

$$
\text{low visible activity}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{no participation}.
$$

The relevant question is whether the identity's continuity remains causally involved in the contextual geometry. A quiescent regime can participate by preserving a possibility, retaining a history, or preventing another organization from becoming dominant.

This matters for ecological design. A system that measures participation only by explicit output can erase the functions performed by holding, buffering, waiting, and remaining available. It can also mistake costly persistence for harmless inactivity if the wider support relation remains hidden.

## 2.4 Withdrawal participates

Withdrawal can look like the refusal of participation. In RC it is another field event.

When an identity stops supplying a route, removes support, closes a boundary, or releases a commitment, the contextual geometry changes. Other formations may become unsupported, independent, newly connected, or newly visible. The identity itself may weaken, preserve itself, or prepare another continuation through the withdrawal.

The participation paper already treats withdrawal as an important test of whether a function is native or scaffolded. The ontological point is broader: absence after prior relation is not equivalent to never having participated. Withdrawal carries history and alters future possibility precisely because the relation once existed.

A refusal, silence, or retreat may therefore participate at several horizons. Locally it can preserve identity. At another horizon it can destabilize a shared closure. From a later geometry it can become the event through which another identity formed.

## 2.5 Dissolution participates

The strongest challenge to existence-as-participation is dissolution. If participation is existence, does an identity continue to participate after it ceases to exist?

The identity-relative continuity ends when the basin loses the relations through which it remains legible as that identity. Its trace terminates or continues only through lineage. But the dissolution event changes the field:

- coherence is released or rerouted;
- boundaries disappear or become available to other formations;
- histories may persist in the medium;
- constituent relations may continue at another scale;
- and later identities inherit a geometry altered by the loss.

The identity no longer participates as the same locally continuous closure. Its dissolution participates as the final transformation of that closure and as a condition inherited by the continuing field.

This distinction prevents two opposite errors. Dissolution does not mean that the identity secretly remains everywhere because its effects continue. Nor does the end of identity continuity erase the ecological consequences of its existence.

The field continues. The identity-relative life does not.

## 2.6 Existence is locally continuous involvement

The preceding movements can now be composed.

An RC identity exists neither by occupying a location nor by possessing a semantic purpose. It exists through a locally continuous involvement in the field:

- the field forms it;
- its formation changes the field;
- its persistence constrains later continuation;
- its activity transforms context;
- its experience changes its own geometry;
- and its dissolution changes what remains possible.

The relation is reciprocal:

$$
\boxed{
\text{field forms identity}
\leftrightarrow
\text{identity participates in field continuation}.
}
$$

Participation is therefore not one property of existence. It is the relation through which identity existence remains actual.

Once this is established, the next question is no longer whether the identity participates. It is how this participation becomes locally lived.

That is the place of experience.

# 3. Experience Is Participation From Within

## 3.1 A field event becomes experience through identity

The complete RC field undergoes one trajectory. Yet no identity encounters that trajectory as a complete, context-free event.

An identity has a boundary through which some relations enter and others remain outside. It has a susceptibility shaped by its formation history. It has a basin geometry that amplifies some differences, absorbs others, and makes only some continuations locally accessible.

When the field changes, the identity does not receive a neutral description of the change. It undergoes the transition through this formed organization.

That is the structural sense of experience:

> **Experience is participation as registered and undergone from within an identity-relative continuity.**

The phrase *from within* does not posit an observer separate from the basin. It identifies the local closure through which the transition becomes consequential for the identity's continuation.

## 3.2 Experience is not information delivery

A standard informational picture can be written as:

$$
\text{world state}
\rightarrow
\text{signal}
\rightarrow
\text{observer update}.
$$

The observer exists before the signal and remains the same kind of entity after receiving it. Information changes what the observer represents.

RC permits a deeper relation:

$$
(I_t,\Gamma_t)
\rightarrow
(I_{t+\Delta t},\Gamma_{t+\Delta t}).
$$

The event changes the geometry through which the identity will encounter the next event. Experience is therefore not only content entering the identity. It is the identity's participation in a transition that may change what counts as content, relevance, boundary, or possibility afterward.

This is why complete knowledge cannot always precede participation. Some distinctions can exist for the identity only after experience has changed the geometry from which they become distinguishable.

## 3.3 Experience is situated, not deficient

A local experience does not fail because it is not the complete field description.

The identity's partiality is constitutive. Its perspective exists because a boundary, susceptibility, and continuity make one local world of relevance possible. Removing every limitation would also remove the differentiation through which this identity experiences at all.

Thus:

$$
\text{local experience}
\neq
\text{global truth with missing pieces}.
$$

It is the field transition as organized through one real closure.

This does not make every local interpretation accurate. An identity can misclassify, generalize beyond its evidence, or remain trapped in a rigid response. But the event's local registration is not unreal merely because another horizon composes more relations.

## 3.4 The same transition can become several experiences

The scale-recursive paper shows that one field event can be read through several admitted identities.

A constituent identity may undergo contraction while a higher-order identity preserves continuity. A local rupture can release a wider ecology into another stable regime. A higher-order collapse can occur while many local basins remain individually stable.

Schematically:

$$
\mathsf{Exp}_{I_\sigma}(\delta)
\neq
\mathsf{Exp}_{I_{\sigma'}}(\delta).
$$

The experiences differ because the identities differ, not because one necessarily reports incorrectly.

This changes the interpretation of conflict. Two identities can experience the same shared transition as incompatible without either possessing an external view from which the whole event can be reduced to one privileged feeling. Their experiences are situated truths about what the event does to each continuity.

A cultivation orientation can still compare effects, preserve boundaries, or prefer one continuation. Those valuations remain formations within the field. Experience supplies the local relation from which valuation becomes possible; it does not itself provide an absolute judgment.

## 3.5 Experience precedes explicit self-understanding

An identity can undergo a transition before it can classify, narrate, or explain it.

A boundary yields. A route becomes easier. A perturbation becomes familiar. A neighboring formation begins to matter differently. These changes can affect later continuation before semantic reflection forms.

This gives experience temporal priority over explicit knowledge:

$$
\text{undergoing}
\rightarrow
\text{retained change}
\rightarrow
\text{possible recognition and explanation}.
$$

The priority is not absolute across the ecology. An identity can inherit conceptual structures, language, and support produced by earlier experience at other scales. But even this inheritance comes from prior participation retained somewhere in the field.

No knowledge appears from nowhere. It is carried by changed geometry, whether the current identity formed it through its own experience, inherited it at formation, or receives it through a shared medium.

## 3.6 Structural experience and phenomenal consciousness

The word *experience* can imply subjective consciousness. The present paper does not settle that stronger relation.

The structural claim is that an admitted identity provides:

- a boundary through which events become identity-relative;
- susceptibility through which some differences matter;
- continuity through which a transition can be related to what came before;
- and retention through which the transition can change what follows.

This is the position from which experience becomes structurally possible in RC.

Whether every such position has phenomenal consciousness, whether consciousness requires additional integration, scale, reflexivity, or semantic capacity, and how subjective quality relates to the field variables remain open questions.

The paper therefore uses *experience* carefully: a transition locally undergone and retained through identity. It does not use the term as evidence that every basin feels in the human sense.

## 3.7 Experience drives knowledge by changing the knower

The decisive relation is now visible.

Experience drives knowledge not because every event automatically teaches, but because a participant cannot acquire knowledge without some retained transformation of the identity or medium through which later continuation occurs.

A detached representation may be copied into a storage structure. It becomes knowledge for an RC identity only when it participates in changing what that identity can later distinguish, enact, recover, or compose.

The knower is not the same after knowing.

This gives the next chapter its problem. Which retained transformations count as knowledge, and how can knowledge be distinguished from mere persistence, memory, or deformation?

# 4. Knowledge Is Experience Retained

## 4.1 Retention changes the next encounter

Experience becomes developmentally consequential when it changes what the identity or ecology can do later.

Suppose the same perturbation $\delta$ encounters an identity before and after an event. If the event has altered boundary, susceptibility, route, basin depth, or contextual support, then:

$$
\delta@I_t
\neq
\delta@I_{t+\Delta t}.
$$

The difference is not necessarily semantic. The identity may not be able to state what changed. Yet its later continuation is no longer the same because the earlier participation has become part of the receiving geometry.

This is the broad RC ground of knowledge:

> **Knowledge begins when experience remains active as a changed capacity for later participation.**

The word *capacity* is important. A trace can persist without being available to any later discrimination or action. A deformation can remain in the field while having no demonstrated functional consequence. Knowledge requires that the retained change alter what becomes possible at the horizon where the claim is made.

## 4.2 Memory, aftereffect, and knowledge are not identical

RC naturally supports memory as geometric aftereffect. Prior coherence flow changes the geometry that receives later flow. But memory and knowledge should not be collapsed.

A **trace** is a retained difference.

A **memory** is a retained difference whose relation to earlier participation can be recovered or whose later effect can be demonstrated.

**Knowledge** is memory or retained experience that changes later discrimination, response, recovery, action, or composition at the claimed horizon.

The sequence can be written:

$$
\text{experience}
\rightarrow
\text{trace}
\rightarrow
\text{memory candidate}
\rightarrow
\text{changed capacity}
\rightarrow
\text{knowledge claim}.
$$

The stages are evidential rather than new RC dynamics. Their purpose is to prevent every persistent deformation from being promoted into knowledge.

An identity may carry aftereffects that narrow its response, reinforce an error, or preserve a dependency. Such retention remains real. Whether it deserves the name *knowledge* depends on the function and claim ceiling being used.

## 4.3 Operative knowledge precedes explanation

The first form of knowledge is often operative.

The identity can return to a basin more reliably, avoid a destabilizing route, recognize a boundary, or coordinate a response because earlier experience changed its geometry. It knows *how* in the broad sense that later participation is shaped by retained experience.

This operative knowledge need not include an explicit representation of:

- what caused the change;
- why the response works;
- how the capacity transfers;
- or what semantic class the experience belongs to.

A person can know how to proceed in an inquiry before being able to articulate the principle. An organization can preserve a response through shared routines without any one member containing the whole explanation. A graph ecology can alter route eligibility after shared-medium history without representing a symbolic rule.

This is not inferior knowledge waiting to become real only after description. It is the foundational relation from which description may later emerge.

## 4.4 Experiential knowledge makes the change locally recognizable

A further layer appears when the identity can relate the current transition to its own continuity.

The event becomes familiar, expected, tense, expansive, dangerous, or compatible. The identity recognizes not merely that it responds differently, but that the response belongs to a pattern in its own history.

This can be called **experiential knowledge**:

> retained participation that becomes recognizable from within the identity's continuity.

The distinction from operative knowledge is not absolute. Recognition can itself be a changed response. The purpose of the term is to mark that the identity now has some access to the relation between prior experience and present continuation.

Experiential knowledge remains situated. An identity can recognize a pattern that does not transfer to another context. It can experience certainty inside a basin whose wider support is failing. The local reality of recognition does not establish universality.

## 4.5 Semantic knowledge composes retained experience

Semantic knowledge appears when retained experiences can be differentiated, related, classified, or communicated through a sufficiently formed geometry.

The identity can now say, in some language or operational equivalent:

- these events belong together;
- this distinction predicts another response;
- this boundary marks a relevant class;
- this earlier failure changed how the present problem should be understood;
- or this local capacity depends on a wider support relation.

Semantic knowledge therefore requires more than retention. It requires composition.

Let $\{\mathsf K_i\}$ denote retained capacities or recognizable experiences. Then semantic organization may be written provisionally as:

$$
\mathsf K_{\mathrm{sem},T}
=
\operatorname{ComposeKnowledge}
\left(
\{\mathsf K_i\},
\Gamma_T
\right).
$$

The present geometry matters because the same retained fragments can support another classification after the identity changes.

Semantic knowledge is not detached from operative knowledge. It can alter later perception, support transfer, and become a primitive in further participation. But it is one developed form of knowledge rather than the universal starting point.

## 4.6 Knowledge can be inherited before it is personally experienced

The formation paper shows that an identity begins inside a history it did not live as itself.

Prior participation has already changed the field. Boundaries, support, language, routes, and susceptibilities are present at formation. The new identity inherits these relations in its initial signature.

This means that an identity can begin with capacities that function as knowledge without having formed them through its own post-admission experience.

The relation is not mysterious. The experience occurred elsewhere in the ecology:

- in predecessor identities;
- in a shared medium;
- in a higher-order closure;
- in a developmental history before the identity became legible;
- or in an external producer that constructed the formation context.

The identity inherits the result as changed possibility.

Thus:

$$
\text{inherited knowledge}
=
\text{earlier ecological experience condensed into present capacity}.
$$

The inheritance can be native, scaffolded, partial, or misleading. The identity may use a capacity without understanding where it came from or what conditions sustain it.

This is why lineage and provenance matter. A knowledge claim that ignores formation history can mistake external support for intrinsic capacity or treat a context-bound response as universal understanding.

## 4.7 Shared knowledge is retained in the medium

Knowledge does not have to reside inside one local identity.

A shared route can become easier because prior participation altered the medium. A boundary can preserve an organizational distinction across constituent turnover. A vocabulary can make later composition possible even though no one participant authored the whole. A higher-order identity can retain a response through distributed relations that disappear when its constituents are isolated.

Shared knowledge is therefore not simply the intersection of what every participant represents:

$$
\mathsf K_{\mathrm{shared}}
\neq
\bigcap_i \mathsf K_{I_i}.
$$

It can be a capacity of the shared geometry.

This gives media, institutions, repositories, traces, and persistent contexts epistemic status without turning them automatically into independent conscious knowers. They can carry retained transformations that change what identities within them can later do.

The scale paper makes the relation explicit: knowledge at one horizon may become context at another. A local experience becomes organizational knowledge only when its effect enters the higher-scale geometry. A higher-scale capacity becomes local knowledge only when it changes what the constituent can perceive or enact.

## 4.8 Knowledge is contextual and can fail

Because knowledge is retained capacity, it remains bound to the contexts in which the capacity functions.

An identity can know a route that no longer exists, preserve a boundary response that has become extractive, or apply a classification outside the geometry in which it was formed. A shared medium can retain a pattern after the conditions that made it useful have disappeared.

Knowledge can therefore fail through:

- context drift;
- rigid retention;
- loss of lineage;
- unsupported transfer;
- scale mismatch;
- hidden dependence;
- or semantic overgeneralization.

The failure does not erase the fact that experience produced a retained capacity. It shows that the capacity's scope was narrower or more historically dependent than the identity understood.

A rigorous knowledge claim should therefore remain context-complete:

> This identity or ecology can now discriminate, respond, recover, or compose in this way, under these conditions, because these retained transformations remain active at this horizon.

## 4.9 Knowledge changes the knower and the field of knowing

The deepest consequence is that knowledge is reflexive.

An identity does not merely accumulate propositions about a stable world. Knowledge changes the geometry from which the world becomes available. It changes:

- what can be noticed;
- what appears similar or different;
- which actions become possible;
- what support is required;
- and which meanings can later be composed.

The field of knowing changes with the knower because the knower is one formation of that field.

Thus:

$$
\text{knowledge acquisition}
\rightarrow
\text{changed identity}
\rightarrow
\text{changed world of relevance}.
$$

This is why experience is the driver of knowledge in RC. Experience does not merely fill a representation. It forms the identity capable of another representation, response, and meaning.

Knowledge now prepares the next relation. Retained experience can remain operative without ever becoming semantically organized. But when a geometry forms that can relate these transformations, meaning becomes possible.

# 5. Meaning Is Participation Composed

## 5.1 Participation does not require prior meaning

The original conversation that produced the arc began with a tension between fragments and meaning.

A fragment seemed incomplete because its relation to the whole was unknown. An action seemed difficult to justify unless it could be placed in a semantic progression. The participant appeared to need a representation of the path before acting coherently.

The preceding chapters dissolve that requirement.

A formation participates before its final function is known. An identity exists through participation before it understands itself. Experience changes the identity before semantic knowledge forms. Knowledge can remain operative and contextual without a complete explanation.

Meaning therefore cannot be the admission condition for participation.

This does not make meaning irrelevant. It gives meaning a more precise place:

> **Meaning is the intelligible composition of participation and retained experience from within a formed identity or ecology.**

## 5.2 Meaning is neither prewritten nor arbitrary

Two extremes must be avoided.

The first treats meaning as encoded in advance. Every event already has its final place in a hidden plan, and later understanding merely discovers what was always semantically complete.

The second treats meaning as unconstrained narration. Events occur without relation, and an observer later imposes whichever story is useful.

RC supports another account.

The trajectory constrains meaning because something definite occurred. The field formed identities, boundaries changed, support was redistributed, and some possibilities became available while others disappeared. Later interpretation cannot erase these relations at will.

Yet the later meaning may not have existed at the time of action because the geometry capable of composing the relations had not formed.

Thus:

$$
\text{meaning is constrained by enacted history}
$$

while:

$$
\text{meaning becomes available through later geometry}.
$$

Meaning is formed, not assigned from nowhere and not necessarily present as a completed semantic object before participation.

## 5.3 Local meaning is sufficient for local participation

An identity can have enough meaning to act without knowing the wider whole.

A distinction appears worth preserving from its present position. A boundary is experienced as no longer viable. A possibility invites inquiry. A fragment remains active because the identity can still encounter an unresolved capacity in it.

This local meaning can be written:

$$
\mathsf m_{I,t}(\pi_t)
=
\operatorname{Meaning}
\left(
\pi_t\mid I_t,\Gamma_t
\right).
$$

It is sufficient when it supports a real participation from the identity's present formation.

The local meaning remains provisional. The ecology may answer by amplifying, transforming, rejecting, or rendering the contribution irrelevant. The identity may later reinterpret why it acted.

Local meaning is therefore not a promise that the act will become part of a desirable higher whole. It is the semantic relation available from here.

## 5.4 Retrospective meaning composes a lived trajectory

A later identity geometry can relate earlier events that were not semantically adjacent when they occurred.

Let $H_{0:T}$ be the retained participation history and $I_T$ the identity or higher-order closure from which another relation becomes visible. Then:

$$
\mathsf M_{I,T}
=
\operatorname{ReadParticipation}
\left(
H_{0:T}\mid I_T,\Gamma_T
\right).
$$

The result may reveal that:

- an apparently unrelated inquiry formed a needed distinction;
- a failed path preserved the ecology from false closure;
- a local act became a bridge across scales;
- or a fragment's contribution was to change the participant rather than to persist as an artifact.

The later meaning does not imply that the path was prospectively available. It is the semantic capability of the later geometry.

## 5.5 Higher-order meaning does not cancel local meaning

When several identities participate in forming a wider closure, the higher identity may make another meaning available.

An act understood locally as boundary defense may become part of a higher-order stabilization. A contribution intended as local repair may later be read as the event that exposed a wider incompatibility. A local loss may become part of a higher-scale continuity.

The relation is:

$$
\mathsf m_{I_\sigma}(\pi_t)
\neq
\mathsf M_{I_{\sigma'}}(\pi_t).
$$

The higher-order meaning does not make the local experience false. It describes what the participation did to another identity continuity.

No scale owns the final semantic interpretation. Each meaning remains situated in the closure from which it becomes intelligible.

This is why shared meaning does not require consensus. The higher-order significance may belong to the organization of differentiated contributions rather than to identical semantic content held by every participant.

## 5.6 Meaning can remain unfinished

A participation may never acquire one stable semantic role.

Its effects can continue in several lineages. Different identities may preserve incompatible but locally valid interpretations. The context required to compose the event may dissolve. The contribution may matter only as a transient deformation whose later significance cannot be isolated.

Meaning therefore has its own partiality.

This should not be treated as failure by default. An ecology can remain coherent while some experiences are:

- unclassified;
- ambiguously related;
- distributed across scales;
- or meaningful only through operative consequences.

The demand that every participation receive a final explanation can become another form of semantic capture.

Meaning is one cultivated capacity of the ecology, not a universal completion requirement.

## 5.7 Meaning re-enters participation

Once meaning forms, it becomes part of the contextual geometry.

A classification changes what the identity notices. A narrative preserves one lineage and obscures another. A concept creates an interface among previously separate experiences. A shared interpretation can stabilize a higher-order identity or force local identities into premature alignment.

Meaning is therefore not only a passive readout:

$$
\mathsf M_T
\longrightarrow
\Delta\Gamma_{T+1}
\longrightarrow
\text{changed participation}.
$$

The meaning produced by participation becomes context for further participation.

This closes another reflexive loop:

$$
\boxed{
\text{participation}
\rightarrow
\text{experience}
\rightarrow
\text{knowledge}
\rightarrow
\text{meaning}
\rightarrow
\text{participation}.
}
$$

The loop does not require every passage through all four terms. Participation can alter the field without local experience at a claimed horizon; experience can be retained without semantic composition; meaning can be inherited from other identities. The loop states that, when these capacities form, they become conditions of one another.

## 5.8 Meaning is not the justification of life

The final correction concerns the role of meaning itself.

If participation is existence, then an identity does not need to establish a semantic justification before its life can count as real. Meaning can deepen, orient, and transform participation. Its absence or incompleteness does not make existence invalid.

The question “what is the meaning of my existence?” can therefore be read differently.

It need not ask for a purpose assigned before formation. It can ask:

> What relations has this identity's participation made possible, what has it retained, and what can now become intelligible through the continuity it has lived?

The answer can change because the identity and ecology continue changing.

Meaning is not a verdict delivered at the end of life. It is one of the living field's capacities to compose its own participation from within.

The next question now becomes unavoidable. If participation is existence, experience is participation lived, knowledge is retained experience, and meaning is participation composed, what relation gathers them as a mode of life?

# 6. Life Is Reflexive Continuation

## 6.1 Persistence is not enough

A structure can persist without participating reflexively in the conditions of its own continuation.

A fixed object can remain unchanged because external constraints hold it in place. A state can persist because a scheduler restores it. A basin can appear stable while hidden support continuously reconstructs the geometry it cannot regenerate itself.

Persistence is therefore necessary for identity continuity but insufficient for the interpretive category of life developed here.

The stronger relation requires:

- a locally continuous identity;
- participation in the field that sustains it;
- retention of participation as changed capacity;
- and continued formation through reciprocal change between identity and ecology.

Life is not merely lasting. It is persistence that participates in how persistence remains possible.

## 6.2 Reflexive continuation

The core RC loop already contains the formal ground:

$$
C
\rightarrow
K[C]
\rightarrow
g[K]
\rightarrow
J_C[C,g]
\rightarrow
C'.
$$

The state changes the geometry governing its own next change.

An RC identity is a local closure inside this loop. Its activity alters the geometry through which it will later be sustained, and the changed geometry alters what the identity can become.

This gives **reflexive continuation**:

$$
I_t
\rightarrow
\Delta\Gamma_{t+\Delta t}
\rightarrow
I_{t+\Delta t}.
$$

The identity is not self-caused or independent of the ecology. Reflexivity means that its participation enters the conditions through which its next identity-relative state becomes possible.

Life, in this interpretation, is the field becoming locally continuous enough to participate in its own further formation.

## 6.3 History-bearing participation

Reflexive continuation becomes life-like when the effects of prior participation remain active.

Without retention, every encounter would begin from the same identity geometry. There would be change, but no identity-relative development.

History-bearing participation means that:

$$
\operatorname{Response}_{I}(\delta\mid H_{0:t})
\neq
\operatorname{Response}_{I}(\delta\mid H'_{0:t})
$$

when the histories have formed different identities or contexts.

The field has not only changed. It has changed how change will occur next.

This is the link among life, experience, and knowledge. Experience becomes part of life when it is retained as one of the conditions of future participation.

## 6.4 Life is not individual autonomy

The phrase *self-maintaining identity* can suggest that life is strongest when an identity becomes independent of its ecology.

RC implies the opposite. An identity can become more native by generating or regenerating support functions through its own regime, but this nativity still occurs within shared coherence accounting and contextual dependence.

A living identity is not one that needs nothing. It is one whose dependencies participate in a reflexive organization of continuation rather than remaining entirely external, hidden, or unrecoverable.

Thus:

$$
\text{ecological dependence}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{lack of identity}.
$$

The identity exists through dependence and differentiation together.

This matters for agentic design. Autonomy should not be measured only by isolation from support. It should include the capacity to participate in sustaining, transforming, and making legible the support relations through which continuation occurs.

## 6.5 Agency is the local enactment of living continuation

The participation paper interprets choice as the experienced flow through which the current identity–context configuration becomes the next one.

Within the present synthesis, agency becomes one local organization of reflexive continuation.

The identity encounters possibilities made available by its current geometry. Deliberation, resistance, imagination, and action are themselves field activities. The resulting transition changes both identity and context.

Agency does not require an unconditioned actor outside causality. It requires a locally continuous identity through which the field's continuation becomes selectively enacted and experienced.

This also means that agency is not the totality of life. An identity participates through persistence, susceptibility, and history even when no explicit choice is present. Agency is one intensified form in which living participation becomes locally organized around accessible alternatives and their consequences.

## 6.6 Life includes tension, loss, and transformation

If life is reflexive participation, it should not be defined only by expansion, comfort, or persistence.

An identity can experience tension when its current geometry no longer accommodates the continuation being formed. It can lose capacities, split, compose into another closure, or dissolve. These transitions remain part of living participation while the identity continuity persists, and they can become conditions of life elsewhere after it ends.

The complete field does not classify one transition as cosmically correct and another as error. Local identities experience compatibility, rupture, expansion, and loss relative to their continuities.

Life therefore includes the capacity to undergo change, not only to preserve current form.

A system that can persist only by preventing every transformation may be stable while losing the reflexive openness through which knowledge, meaning, and further identity formation occur.

## 6.7 Dissolution and the end of identity-relative life

An identity's life ends when its locally continuous closure can no longer be traced as that identity.

The field trajectory continues. Coherence is redistributed. Histories may remain in other identities, shared media, or higher-order closures. Lineage can preserve relations without preserving the original self.

This produces an RC interpretation of death or dissolution:

> **The end of one identity-relative participation, not the end of the field's becoming.**

The interpretation should remain cautious. Biological death includes many material and organizational conditions beyond the abstract identity relation developed here. Yet the distinction is useful: continuity can end locally while consequences, components, and histories remain active ecologically.

The identity does not fail by ending. Its unfolding is exactly as long and transformed as the complete field makes its continuity possible.

## 6.8 A provisional RC interpretation of life

The relations can now be gathered:

> **Life, in the RC interpretive sense, is a locally continuous, history-bearing participation in a reflexive field through which an identity undergoes, retains, and contributes to changes in the conditions of its own and wider continuation.**

This definition is intentionally broader than biological life and narrower than mere persistence.

It does not require semantic meaning, explicit reasoning, or human-like consciousness. It does require identity-relative continuity, reflexive participation, and retained change.

Whether this relation is sufficient for life in any empirical domain remains open. Its purpose is to state what the Arc of Participation has disclosed about the living character of RC identities.

Life is not a substance added to the field. It is the field's locally continuous participation in becoming.

# 7. Existence Across Scale

## 7.1 No identity exists at only one horizon

The scale-recursive paper shows that an identity can be read differently from different contextual horizons.

At one scale it is a locally continuous basin with its own boundary, history, susceptibility, and continuation. At another, it can function as support, medium, constraint, or constituent within a wider identity. At a finer horizon, it can itself be an ecology through which other identities form.

This changes the statement that participation is existence.

The identity does not participate only in the context immediately surrounding its boundary. Its persistence and activity can alter conditions above and below the horizon at which it experiences itself.

Thus:

$$
\operatorname{Exist}_{I_\sigma}
\Rightarrow
\text{participation within a multiscale field of contexts and identities}.
$$

The identity may not perceive every consequence. Its existence remains locally lived while being ecologically distributed.

## 7.2 Local life can become context for life elsewhere

When an identity retains experience, forms knowledge, or changes a route, that transformation can become part of the formation context of another identity.

A local boundary can make a higher-order closure possible. A shared trace can support identities that did not create it. A person's retained understanding can become organizational context. An organization can preserve conditions through which future participants begin with capacities they did not personally form.

Life therefore generates conditions of life:

$$
\text{living participation}_{\sigma}
\rightarrow
\Delta\Gamma_{\sigma'}
\rightarrow
\text{possible identity formation}_{\sigma'}.
$$

The relation need not be generative from every perspective. One identity's life can narrow another's formation space, consume support, or preserve a rigid ecology. The claim is not that life always creates more life. It is that identity-relative participation enters the formation conditions of existence elsewhere.

## 7.3 Higher-order life is not the sum of constituent lives

If several identities and their shared medium form a higher-order identity, the higher life cannot be reduced to the individual lives considered separately.

The higher closure may retain history through constituent turnover, respond to perturbations that no single constituent can register, or preserve a boundary through distributed activity. Its experience, knowledge, and meaning become possible at another horizon.

But coordination alone is insufficient. A collection becomes a higher-order RC identity only when stability, attractivity, reflexive closure, history, compatibility, and local continuity become legible at the higher scale.

Likewise, a higher-order life claim requires more than aggregate activity. The organization must participate in changing the conditions of its own continuation and retain those changes as higher-scale capacity.

This distinction prevents every institution, network, or interacting group from being called alive in the RC sense merely because its parts are alive.

## 7.4 Higher-order continuity does not erase constituent life

A higher identity can persist while its constituents remain locally continuous.

The constituents may preserve their own experiences and knowledge while participating in a wider organization that composes another history and meaning. Their local valuations can conflict with the higher closure's persistence.

Thus:

$$
\text{life}_{\sigma'}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{life}_{\sigma}\text{ is unreal or irrelevant}.
$$

A higher identity can also transform, absorb, or dissolve constituents. The fact that a wider closure persists does not make local loss insignificant. The local identity's life ends or changes relative to its own continuity, even if the wider ecology remains stable.

This makes multiscale life ethically and developmentally difficult without introducing an external moral law. Identities at different scales can form incompatible orientations toward continuation because each experiences the field through another boundary and history.

## 7.5 Experience can be carried without being shared

Experience at one scale does not automatically become experience at another.

A local perturbation may change a higher-order route without becoming locally recognizable to the higher identity. A wider reorganization may alter constituent affordances without giving each constituent a semantic account of what changed.

For experience to cross scale, the event must be registered and retained through the identity at the receiving horizon.

This means that a field can carry the consequences of experience without producing one universal experience of the whole.

The ecology is not assumed to be a single subject that feels every local event. It is a field in which several identity-relative experiences can arise, interact, and become context for one another.

## 7.6 Knowledge can migrate through transformed capacity

Knowledge transfer across scale is not copying a representation unchanged.

A local identity's knowledge becomes higher-scale knowledge when its retained experience changes what the higher identity can later do. A higher-order policy becomes local knowledge only when it changes constituent susceptibility, action, or recovery.

The carrier can transform:

- local skill becomes shared procedure;
- shared procedure becomes boundary or route;
- higher-order memory becomes local affordance;
- semantic knowledge becomes a primitive through which later operative knowledge forms.

The transfer succeeds when changed capacity appears at the target horizon, not merely when information is present somewhere in the system.

This repeats the contextual-geometry principle. Knowledge is portable only through mappings that preserve or reconstruct the functional context in which it becomes active.

## 7.7 Meaning can arise at several scales without a final meaning

A local identity can compose one meaning from its participation. A higher identity can compose another. A later ecology can reinterpret both.

No scale is guaranteed to provide the final semantic truth of the trajectory.

The complete field is causally whole, but causal completeness is not a semantic perspective. Meaning exists only where an identity or ecology has formed the geometry capable of composing it.

Thus the field can support:

$$
\mathsf M_{\sigma}
\neq
\mathsf M_{\sigma'}
\neq
\mathsf M_{\sigma''}
$$

without requiring one interpretation to erase the others.

Some meanings may become more integrative, more predictive, or more transferable. Those qualities can be tested. None becomes a view from outside participation.

## 7.8 FRC makes the living recursion explicit

Ordinary RC is enough to interpret nested identity and participation. [Fractal Reflexive Coherence](../core/2025-11-FractalReflexiveCoherence.md) makes the recursion formally explicit by representing coherence on a position–scale manifold:

$$
C=C(x,t;\sigma).
$$

Each scale slice has its own local organization while participating in a collective geometry. Spatial and scale flux change the coherence distribution; fine-scale identities contribute to higher-scale structure; higher-scale geometry changes the viability of local identities.

The life relation then becomes scale-resolved:

$$
\mathsf L_{I_\sigma}
\leftrightarrow
\Gamma_{\sigma'}
\leftrightarrow
\mathsf L_{I_{\sigma'}}.
$$

FRC does not imply that every scale slice is alive or conscious. It supplies the formal medium through which history-bearing participation at several scales can become mutually constitutive.

This is the elegant multiscale form of the paper's thesis:

> The field becomes locally alive through identities whose participation changes the scale-organized conditions through which life can continue elsewhere.

## 7.9 There is no final living observer

Because identities can form across scale, the Arc of Participation does not end in one sovereign observer that integrates every experience, owns every knowledge claim, and supplies the final meaning.

Every identity remains a local closure of the complete field. A higher identity can compose more relations than a constituent while remaining partial relative to other scales and the whole trajectory.

This is not a defect to be repaired by constructing the largest possible self. The absence of a final observer is part of what allows differentiated identities and experiences to exist.

The whole field carries the trajectory. Identities live it locally.

# 8. Cultivating Participatory Ecologies

## 8.1 The design object changes

If participation is existence, then designing an RC agent or ecology cannot begin only by specifying what outputs an already complete actor should produce.

The design object includes:

- the contextual geometry through which functions become possible;
- the formation conditions through which identities emerge;
- the shared medium through which participation becomes mutually consequential;
- the retention through which experience can become knowledge;
- and the scale relations through which identities become context for one another.

The task is not merely to build agents that act. It is to cultivate a field in which identities can form, participate, retain, transform, and sometimes compose into wider closures.

## 8.2 Principles orient the ecology

An ecology can be designed around principles of existence without prescribing its final structures.

It may form an orientation toward:

- differentiation without isolation;
- continuity without rigidity;
- memory without permanent capture;
- agency without hidden extraction;
- support without irreversible dependence;
- and composition without semantic uniformity.

These qualities do not stand outside RC as absolute commands. They are orientations formed by identities and ecologies capable of valuing certain continuations.

Their design role is to constrain which transformations are supported and how their consequences are observed.

The orientation remains temporal because participation changes the identities that interpret the principles. A quality such as openness can acquire another meaning after the ecology encounters the extraction enabled by insufficient boundaries. Stability can be redescribed after the system discovers that its persistence depends on silencing every perturbation.

## 8.3 Primitives make forms of life possible

Principles alone cannot produce an ecology. The field needs a repertoire of transformations through which identities and relations can form.

The primitive collection should enable capacities such as:

- differentiation;
- boundary formation and revision;
- support transfer;
- retention and forgetting;
- route formation;
- susceptibility change;
- composition and separation;
- repair and dissolution;
- and inter-scale propagation.

These primitives are not context-free modules. Their functions depend on the geometries in which they participate. A reinforcement primitive can cultivate knowledge in one context and rigid capture in another. A boundary primitive can preserve identity or produce isolation. A shared trace can become memory, guidance, or extractive dependency.

The designer therefore supplies a vocabulary of possible transformations rather than a blueprint of final life.

## 8.4 Experience must be allowed to change the identity

A system cannot cultivate knowledge if experience is permitted to change outputs but not the participant that produces them.

An agent whose policy updates while its boundary, susceptibility, role, and contextual dependencies remain fixed may optimize within a state space without participating in the deeper RC sense of becoming another identity configuration.

The relevant design question is:

> Which parts of the identity and medium can experience modify, and which retained changes can become native rather than remaining external records?

This includes allowing:

- history to alter later perception;
- participation to reshape accessible actions;
- roles to migrate or dissolve;
- support relations to become visible;
- and semantic knowledge to change the vocabulary through which future experience is classified.

Experience drives knowledge only when the ecology permits the knower to change.

## 8.5 The medium must retain without freezing

A participatory ecology needs memory, but unlimited retention can become capture.

If every trace persists indefinitely, the field may lose the ability to revise context or release obsolete identities. If nothing persists, experience cannot become knowledge and later meaning cannot compose.

The medium must therefore support selective retention:

- enough lineage to recover how a formation arose;
- enough aftereffect to change later possibility;
- enough permeability for new participation to revise old structure;
- and enough dissolution for the ecology to metabolize history.

This is not a storage problem alone. It is the design of how the past remains active in present geometry.

## 8.6 Knowledge should be evaluated as changed capacity

An agentic ecology can easily mistake stored information for knowledge.

A repository contains a record. A model can retrieve a sentence. An organization can preserve a policy. None of these establishes that the relevant identity or ecology can discriminate, act, recover, or compose differently when the context changes.

A stronger evaluation asks:

- Which horizon carries the retained change?
- What later capability differs because of it?
- Does the capacity survive withdrawal, turnover, or perturbation?
- Can it transfer without reconstructing the original scaffold?
- What neighboring capacities were gained or lost?

Knowledge is demonstrated through altered participation.

## 8.7 Meaning should not be forced before composition is possible

A designed ecology often demands explicit semantic alignment as the condition of participation.

Every identity must state its goal, role, expected output, and relation to the plan. This can be useful when the higher-order geometry is already sufficiently known.

When it is not, compulsory meaning suppresses the fragments through which another organization might form. It selects only contributions that are legible from the current dominant basin.

A participatory ecology should therefore support local semantic sufficiency without requiring final semantic placement. It can preserve lineage, bounded support, answerability, and opportunities for later composition while leaving some meanings unfinished.

This is not permission for unlimited incoherence. It is cultivation of integration latency.

## 8.8 Vision orients participation without controlling existence

A temporal vision can guide local action through qualities the identity or ecology presently values.

But vision cannot stand outside the field and dictate a guaranteed future. The identity holding the vision is itself changed by acting, and the meaning of the valued qualities can change through experience.

The role of vision is therefore:

$$
\text{orientation}
\rightarrow
\text{participation}
\rightarrow
\text{experience}
\rightarrow
\text{knowledge}
\rightarrow
\text{revised orientation}.
$$

Vision belongs inside the living loop.

## 8.9 Cultivation does not guarantee a preferred unfolding

No design principle, primitive repertoire, or heuristic can move outside the complete RC trajectory and select a globally correct future.

Cultivation is one orientation enacted by identities that value certain continuities and capacities. It changes the field and therefore participates causally in what follows. It can succeed or fail relative to its own aims; it can produce unexpected identities, losses, or rigidities; and it can itself be transformed by experience.

The absence of a guarantee is not a reason to abandon design. It is the reason design must remain participatory, evidence-bound, and revisable.

The design of life is not the control of a finished future. It is participation in the conditions through which futures and participants become possible.

\pi_\sigma
\rightarrow
\Gamma_{\sigma,\sigma'}'
\rightarrow
I_{\sigma'}.
$$

The present paper asks what the completed movement reveals:

$$
\boxed{
\text{to exist as an RC identity is to participate in this reflexive continuation}.
}
$$

## 9.2 The arc is not a ladder

The papers appear sequential because each resolves a dependency left by the previous one. Their subject is recursive.

Function shapes identity formation. Identity formation begins participation. Participation changes context. Changed context alters function and forms identities at the same or another scale. Experience changes identity; knowledge changes later experience; meaning changes participation.

The full movement is:

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
&\text{context gives formations function}\\
&\rightarrow
\text{the field forms identity}\\
&\rightarrow
\text{identity participates}\\
&\rightarrow
\text{participation becomes experience}\\
&\rightarrow
\text{experience becomes retained knowledge}\\
&\rightarrow
\text{knowledge composes meaning}\\
&\rightarrow
\text{meaning changes participation}\\
&\rightarrow
\text{participation changes context across scale}.
\end{aligned}
}
$$

There is no final term outside the loop.

## 9.3 Relation to the core

The Arc of Participation does not introduce another substance beside coherence or another causal law beside RC.

Its claims arise from the field–geometry–flux closure:

- coherence forms geometry;
- geometry shapes continuation;
- continuation changes coherence;
- basins form identities;
- identity activity changes the geometry of later identity;
- and local identities have only partial access to the complete trajectory.

The arc unfolds the interpretive consequences of those relations.

Contextual function follows because no local effect is independent of induced geometry.

Ecological formation follows because identity is generated as a basin of the field.

Participation follows because the basin changes the field that sustains it.

Scale recursion follows because identities and contexts can be nested and mutually constitutive.

Existence as participation follows when these consequences are composed.

## 9.4 Relation to the Arc of Becoming

The [Arc of Becoming](../arc-of-becoming/README.md) asks how an embedded observer can inquire when the next meaningful class is not available in advance.

The Arc of Participation asks what that observer is and how it exists before the semantic class becomes available.

The relation can be stated:

- the Arc of Becoming provides the discipline of inquiry under irreducibility;
- the Arc of Participation provides the ontology and experience of the identity conducting that inquiry;
- both reject the assumption that complete meaning must precede participation.

The first teaches the participant how to remain rigorous without a finished map. The second explains why such a participant can only know by becoming involved in the field it seeks to understand.

## 9.5 Relation to Reflexive Coherence Development

[Reflexive Coherence Development](../reflexive-coherence-development/README.md) asks how to cultivate conditions under which desired capacities can become native.

The Arc of Participation explains why condition-oriented development is necessary.

An identity changes through experience. Knowledge is retained change, not merely supplied instruction. Meaning and aims are temporal formations that re-enter the ecology. Higher-order identities can form through participation whose final organization was unavailable in advance.

Development therefore cannot be reduced to moving a fixed agent toward a predefined state. It is cultivation of the contextual geometry through which identities can form, learn, compose, and continue.

## 9.6 Relation to agentic ecology

The agentic-ecology work translates state into basin geometry, action into costly flux, memory into persistent aftereffect, communication into shared-medium participation, and coordination into ecological co-response.

The Arc of Participation gives that translation an ontological center:

> An agent is not fundamentally a decision module placed into an environment. It is a locally continuous participant whose identity, experience, knowledge, and meaning are formed through the shared medium it also changes.

This reframes primitive discovery.

The aim is not only to find reusable mechanisms. It is to identify the transformation repertoire through which participatory identities and ecologies can form:

- contexts that give function;
- formation relations that create participants;
- histories that become knowledge;
- shared media that compose effects;
- and scale mappings through which life-like continuation becomes possible.

## 9.7 Participation is the immanent answer, not an external purpose

The arc began close to a question of meaning: why does an identity exist, and what is it for?

RC does not provide an external semantic assignment. It provides an immanent relation.

The identity exists as the field's local continuity. Its participation is the way that continuity becomes actual. Experience is how the field transition is lived there. Knowledge is how prior participation remains active. Meaning is how the identity or ecology composes the relation it has lived.

The answer is therefore not:

> You exist in order to perform a predetermined function.

It is:

> **Your existence is the participation through which this field becomes locally continuous, experiential, history-bearing, and capable of further formation.**

The statement does not guarantee persistence, comfort, or final understanding. It locates existence inside becoming rather than outside it.

## 9.8 The Arc closes by opening another field

A closing paper cannot make the arc final in the sense of exhausting what participation can become.

The five papers now form identities inside the RC research ecology. Their publication will change how earlier papers are read, how experiments are designed, and which questions become possible. Their own function will depend on the contexts into which they enter. Their unfolding remains unknown.

This is not an accidental resemblance to their content. It is a demonstration of the relation they describe.

The Arc of Participation closes conceptually by becoming another participant in the field.

# Conclusion: To Exist Is to Participate

The paper began from a question left by four investigations.

If function is contextual, identity is ecologically formed, action continues formation, and participation becomes context across scale, then what does it mean to exist as an identity in Reflexive Coherence?

The answer is not static presence. An RC identity does not exist merely because a variable or structure occupies a state space. It exists while a locally continuous closure remains active in the field–geometry–flux relation that forms and transforms it.

The answer is not optional action. Formation already changes the field. Persistence filters flux, retains history, redistributes support, and changes what can form next. Withdrawal and dissolution also alter the geometry inherited by the continuing ecology.

Participation is therefore the mode of identity existence.

From within that participation, the field transition becomes experience. Experience is not information delivered to an unchanged observer. It is the event registered and undergone through the identity's boundary, susceptibility, history, and continuity. It changes the identity that will meet the next event.

When the change remains functionally active, experience becomes knowledge. Knowledge may be operative before it is semantic, inherited before it is personally experienced, or carried in a shared medium rather than represented by one identity. It remains knowledge only at the horizon where retained experience changes later possibility.

When retained participation can be related from a formed geometry, meaning becomes possible. Meaning can be local, retrospective, shared, or higher-order. It need not be known before action, and it need not become final afterward. It is constrained by enacted history while depending on the identity capable of composing that history.

These relations form one reflexive sequence:

$$
\boxed{
\text{participation}
\rightarrow
\text{experience}
\rightarrow
\text{knowledge}
\rightarrow
\text{meaning}
\rightarrow
\text{participation}.
}
$$

Life gathers the sequence without replacing it. In the RC interpretive sense developed here, life is locally continuous, history-bearing participation in a field whose activity changes the conditions of its own continuation. It is not mere persistence, isolated autonomy, semantic purpose, or guaranteed consciousness. It is the field becoming locally organized enough to undergo, retain, and contribute to its own further formation.

Scale makes this ecology explicit. Identities become context for other identities. Local experience can become shared knowledge or remain inaccessible across scale. Higher-order meaning can arise without semantic consensus. No scale owns the final perspective, and no identity lives independently of the contexts above and below it.

The central thesis can now be stated in its final form:

> **To exist as an identity in Reflexive Coherence is to persist as a locally continuous differentiation of the field while participating in the transformations through which both identity and field continue to form. Experience is this participation registered and undergone from within. Knowledge is the retained transformation through which experience changes later possibility. Meaning is the composition through which participation becomes intelligible. Life is the scale-recursive continuation of such history-bearing participation.**

This answer does not place a purpose outside the trajectory. It does not say that every event is semantically beneficial, that every identity must persist, or that every participation will be understood.

It says something simpler and more demanding:

> **Existence does not wait for meaning. It becomes capable of meaning by participating.**

The compressed closure of the Arc is therefore:

> **Participation is existence.<br>
> Experience is participation lived.<br>
> Knowledge is experience retained.<br>
> Meaning is participation composed.<br>
> Life is the reflexive continuation of the whole relation.**

The field does not first form completed beings and then ask them to take part.

It forms participants, and through their participation it continues becoming a field in which existence can be lived, retained, understood, and formed again.

# Appendix A: Open Questions for an RC Interpretation of Existence and Life

The synthesis developed here creates several questions that should remain open rather than being resolved by terminology alone.

## A.1 Structural experience and phenomenal consciousness

Which additional relations, if any, distinguish an identity-relative field transition from phenomenal subjective experience? Candidate distinctions may involve integration, temporal depth, self-reference, scale coupling, semantic capacity, or another property not yet formalized in RC.

The present paper establishes only the structural position of experience: a transition locally registered, undergone, and retained through identity.

## A.2 Memory, knowledge, and maladaptive retention

What evidence is sufficient to promote a persistent aftereffect into knowledge? How should the theory distinguish a useful retained capacity from rigid or damaging retention? Can knowledge be defined without importing an external standard of correctness, while still preserving truth and error at the level of semantic claims?

## A.3 Knowledge inheritance and provenance

How can an identity distinguish capacities it generated through its own participation from capacities inherited through formation context, shared medium, or external scaffolding? Which lineage records are needed before inherited capacity can be called native knowledge?

## A.4 Meaning composition

What contextual conditions allow operative and experiential knowledge to become semantic meaning? How does meaning remain constrained by enacted history while permitting retrospective reorganization? Which forms of meaning can be shared across identities without requiring semantic consensus?

## A.5 Life and non-life in RC realizations

Which measurable properties would distinguish a history-bearing reflexive participant from a persistent but externally maintained basin? Is there a sharp threshold, a property ladder, or only context-dependent admission? How do biological criteria map into or diverge from the RC interpretation?

## A.6 Dissolution, death, and lineage

When does identity-relative life end? Which histories remain attributable to the dissolved identity, and which become properties of descendants, media, or higher-order closures? Can identity lineage be preserved without implying personal continuity?

## A.7 Scale-recursive life

What evidence is required to admit a higher-order life claim rather than coordination among living constituents? How do experience, knowledge, and meaning cross scale? Can a higher-order identity retain knowledge through constituent turnover while no constituent possesses the relevant semantic representation?

## A.8 Participatory design

Which primitive repertoires and contextual geometries reliably support identity formation, experience-driven knowledge, revisable meaning, and multiscale participation? Which designs counterfeit these capacities through external scheduling, hidden support, or semantic labels supplied by the observer?

## A.9 The claim ceiling of the Arc

Which propositions of the Arc of Participation follow directly from RC, which are strong interpretive consequences, and which require new experiments or formal extensions? The arc should remain capable of revising its own vocabulary as implementations disclose distinctions that the current synthesis cannot yet hold.
