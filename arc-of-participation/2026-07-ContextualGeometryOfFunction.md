## Regimes, Realizations, and the Diversity of Becoming

**Date:** 2026-07-22<br>
**Status:** Theoretical investigation with bounded evidential appendices

## Abstract

This paper begins from a question about progression. Development is often imagined as a semantically continuous path in which each step extends an increasingly visible whole. Yet chronology and path dependence do not require prospective semantic continuity. A reflexive system may form locally coherent fragments that remain uncomposed until a later formation changes the geometry from which their relation can be understood. The enacted history is real, while its semantic unity may be retrospective.

Several forms—sequential progression, fragmentary composition, tension-responsive reorganization, and generative affordance—appear compatible with Reflexive Coherence (RC). A natural first response is to organize this diversity through parameters, models, realizations, and regimes. Those levels remain necessary, but they can suggest that a realization or parameter region carries a regime directly. This paper proposes a deeper ordering: parameters and realizations constrain which contextual geometries can be formed, reached, sustained, and transformed; functions and regimes arise through those geometries.

RC is defined by the joint closure of a non-negative coherence field and flux, continuity, coherence-induced geometry, a coherence functional and variational condition, and system-level coherence invariance. Within that closure, local structures act through the geometry they help induce and the flux it makes possible. Function is therefore geometrically mediated. The stronger thesis investigated here is contextual constitution: a formation acquires its effective role through a dynamically sustained context of surrounding basins, boundaries, flux routes, support, history, and scale.

A regime is consequently a persistent or recurrent geometry–flux organization expressed in a region, over an interval, at a scale, under a history—not a label attached to a realization as a whole. One realization may sustain several regimes, while structurally different realizations may support functionally corresponding roles when declared observables, invariants, and intervention responses are preserved. A primitive likewise becomes a contextually typed transformation. Portability may require adapting the primitive, adapting the target context, or co-adapting both, while accounting for invariant preservation, contextual distortion, support debt, and naturalization.

The contextual thesis changes the scope of truth without making truth subjective. Formal truth remains grounded in the RC equations; functional propositions become objective only when the contexts, controls, tolerances, and claim ceilings on which they depend are registered. Bounded functional correspondence is therefore used instead of presupposing an equivalence relation that later work has not established. The resulting constructive object is an Atlas of contextual realizability: a map from realizations and histories through reachable contextual-geometry trajectories to functional roles, regimes, transitions, and preservation contracts, and back from selected functions toward realizable geometries and developmental paths. Existing experiments are retained only as bounded evidence in the appendix. They motivate, but do not prove, geometric multiple realizability or native contextual regeneration.

## Formal Status

The defining RC equations recalled here retain the status assigned to them in the existing core formulation. The notation introduced for contextual geometry, function, reachability, bounded correspondence, and portability is a formal scaffold. It makes dependencies and claim boundaries explicit; it does not assert that a final geometry of contexts, a complete regime taxonomy, or a categorical structure has already been established.

The core formulation assumes that a complete admissible state has a single-valued continuation. This paper does not establish the existence, uniqueness, or well-posedness conditions required to prove that assumption for every RC realization. The formal terms are **field trajectory**, **identity-relative trace**, and **lineage**; **immanent unfolding** names the experiential and developmental interpretation of a trace, not a second trajectory.

The paper separates two claim levels.

First, **geometric mediation of function** follows closely from the RC closure: coherence writes geometry, geometry shapes flux, and flux updates coherence. The effect of a local structure is therefore mediated by the geometry and flux organization in which it participates.

Second, **contextual constitution of function** is the central investigated consequence. It proposes that, for a substantial class of RC functions and phenomenologies, the effective role is not an intrinsic property of an isolated structure but a relational property of a dynamically sustained contextual geometry. Multiple regimes within one realization, bounded functional correspondence across different realizations, contextually typed primitives, and transformational portability follow as hypotheses and structural consequences of that proposition. They remain bounded by what can be derived, implemented, and observed.

The purpose of the investigation is not to declare contextual geometry solved. It is to identify the object that later formalization and experiment must resolve.

# 1. From Progression to Context

## 1.1 The comfort of semantic progression

The inquiry began from a familiar comfort: linear progression is easy to inhabit. One understands something, extends it, resolves the next difficulty, and moves toward a whole whose outline becomes clearer with every step. The image is architectural. A wall is built by placing each brick next to an already placed brick until the intended structure is complete.

This image combines several relations that do not always belong together. Time advances; therefore meaning appears to advance in the same direction. Earlier activity changes later possibility; therefore the next meaningful activity should be locally recognizable as a continuation of the earlier one. A whole eventually becomes coherent; therefore its coherence is projected backward as though it had guided the process from the beginning.

Many developmental processes do not appear this way from within. A question becomes active and is carried far enough to change the field. Another question appears elsewhere. A distinction is preserved without knowing where it belongs. A method is learned before the situation that makes it useful exists. Several partial formations remain disconnected, not because they are necessarily incompatible, but because the geometry that could relate them has not yet formed. Much later, a new basin makes their relation visible. What looked like deviation becomes preparation; what looked central becomes peripheral; what looked like failure becomes the observation that prevented a false completion.

The later whole may be coherent. The process that produced it did not have to be semantically continuous while it unfolded.

That difference matters because the phrase *progression* can hide several distinct claims. Chronological progression says that events occur in order. Path dependence says that their order changes what becomes possible. Semantic progression says something stronger: each step is already intelligible as the next part of one construction. RC development requires the first two, but the original question was whether it requires the third.

## 1.2 The enacted path and the understood path

Let an evolving condition be written schematically as

$$
M_0 \longrightarrow M_1 \longrightarrow M_2 \longrightarrow \cdots \longrightarrow M_n.
$$

This sequence has chronology. If each transition alters the geometry inherited by the next one, it also has path dependence. Nothing in those two facts guarantees that an embedded actor can read

$$
M_{k+1}
$$

as the semantic continuation of

$$
M_k.
$$

The distinction becomes clearer when the path is separated into two relations.

The **enacted path** is the actual history of field changes: perturbations, collapses, supports, withdrawals, basin reinforcement, boundary deformation, flux rerouting, and integration. It exists whether or not the actor understands its global meaning.

The **understood path** is the organization of that history that becomes possible from a later formation. If $H_{0:n}$ is the preserved activity history and $B_n$ is a later basin from which previously separate events can be related, then the understood path may be represented as

$$
\mathsf{Path}_{\mathrm{ret}}
=
\operatorname{ReadPath}(H_{0:n}\mid B_n).
$$

The qualification is essential:

$$
\mathsf{Path}_{\mathrm{ret}}
\neq
\text{a prospective plan that necessarily generated }B_n.
$$

Retrospective understanding is constrained by what actually occurred, so it is not arbitrary storytelling. Yet it must not be projected backward as though the later geometry had already been available to the earlier observer. The path becomes semantically visible because the condition of reading has changed.

This is already consistent with the trajectory language of [Attunement of Becoming](../reflexive-coherence-development/2026-06-AttunementOfBecoming.md), where the path is not only a series of local loops but the non-erasable history of what those loops did to the field that receives later contact. The present inquiry adds a further possibility: the history can remain developmentally real while its global composition is still unavailable.

## 1.3 Fragments are not yet fragmentation

Once semantic continuity is no longer assumed, partial formations need a different status. A fragment in this investigation is not simply a broken remnant. It is a locally coherent formation whose relation to a larger whole is absent, weak, or not yet readable.

Suppose the current field contains a family of partial formations

$$
\mathcal F_t=\{f_1,\ldots,f_n\},
$$

with an expressed coupling structure $\Gamma_t$. Semantic progression imagines that a new partial normally attaches to the already assembled component. Fragment-compositional development permits $\Gamma_t$ to remain disconnected. The fragments may persist in separate regions, histories, practices, or scales until a later reorganization changes the coupling:

$$
\Gamma_t \longrightarrow \Gamma_{t+1}.
$$

A higher-order formation may then become possible:

$$
B
=
\operatorname{Compose}
\left(
\mathcal F_t,\Gamma_{t+1},H_{0:t}
\right).
$$

The later basin does not merely collect unchanged pieces. Composition can alter what each fragment is. A distinction that once appeared local may become an interface. A method may become a bridge. An apparent contradiction may become a scale difference. The meaning of the parts changes because the relation among them has changed.

This is why *fragment* must be distinguished from the existing notion of fragmented coherence in [The Language of Becoming](../arc-of-becoming/2026-01-LanguageOfBecoming.md). Fragmentation names a condition in which locally coherent regions lack sufficient global integration. The present concept is earlier and more neutral: several formations may be uncomposed while the larger field still preserves their accessibility, boundaries, histories, and capacity for later relation.

The difference lies in the holding geometry. If partials can remain differentiated without being forgotten, flattened, or maintained through escalating extraction, the system may be in suspended composition. If the field can no longer preserve or revisit them, the same visible disconnection becomes fragmentation. The observer cannot decide which condition is present merely by counting links among parts. The relevant question is what geometry keeps those parts available and what future couplings it still permits.

The capacity to remain with the undone is therefore not just an attitude toward incompletion. It is a condition in which partial coherence can persist without premature integration and without losing the possibility of later composition.

## 1.4 Tension reveals one movement, not every movement

The next question follows from the fragments themselves. If development does not always extend the currently active formation, what moves attention and flux from one region to another?

Tension offers a powerful answer. An incompatibility, pressure, ambiguity, boundary strain, or unstable multi-basin relation becomes active. Reorganization changes the geometry; another unresolved relation then becomes salient elsewhere. From the internal perspective it can feel as though tension has moved from one basin to another. More precisely, one deformation has changed the field so that another region now carries the most accessible expression of unresolved global incompatibility.

RC gives this account real formal support. Collapse, curvature degeneracy, basin competition, breakage, and spark formation are all described through geometric instability in [Reflexive Coherence: Identity, Choice, and Abundance](../core/2025-11-RC-IdentityChoiceAbundance.md). But the fact that tension can reorganize a field does not establish that tension is the universal source of movement.

A coherent condition may develop when nothing is presently failing. Distinct formations may become available for relation because the field can support more differentiation than it currently expresses. A new perspective may arise not by repairing a deficit but by composing capacities that were already viable separately. The provisional term **generative affordance** names this possibility: a relation made available by the current geometry through which a formation not predetermined by its parts may become possible.

Tension and generative affordance initially seemed like competing drivers. That framing also proved too quick. A generative composition can expose an incompatibility that was previously invisible. Tension can prevent a false composition or clear a boundary so that another relation becomes possible. The same process can therefore include both, and a single realization may express them in different regions or phases.

The important result of the distinction is not that generative affordance should replace tension as an absolute driver. It is that neither should be promoted from a possible morphology of becoming into a universal motive of RC without first locating where the morphology resides.

## 1.5 The question changes location

At this point the original question cannot be answered only at the level of interpretation.

If becoming can appear semantically sequential, fragment-compositional, tension-responsive, or generatively compositional, one might classify different RC systems according to which tendency they express. An initial attempt to organize the problem followed that path. It separated the fixed theory from its models, parameterizations, realizations, trajectories, and regimes, then proposed parameter–regime cartography as the bridge to application.

That clarification was valuable, especially because it exposed the hidden quantifier in claims such as “RC tends toward abundance” or “RC is driven by tension.” Yet the organization still placed causal weight too early. It suggested a chain of the form

$$
\text{realization and parameters}
\longrightarrow
\text{regime}
\longrightarrow
\text{phenomenology}.
$$

The difficulty is that a realization may contain several different geometries at once. The same parameterization may sustain distinct local contexts, different histories may make the same encounter function differently, and a regime may be present in one region while another regime organizes the surrounding field. Conversely, different implementations may form dissimilar literal structures that nevertheless support the same functional relation.

The missing term is the geometry actually composed in context.

The causal order therefore needs an intermediate layer:

$$
\boxed{
\text{realization, parameters, history, and boundaries}
\longrightarrow
\text{reachable contextual geometries}
\longrightarrow
\text{regimes}
\longrightarrow
\text{phenomenology}
}
$$

This does not make parameters irrelevant. It changes what they are parameters *of*. They do not directly carry tension, generativity, fragmentation, or semantic progression. They shape the space of geometries through which those functions may become available.

## 1.6 The investigation

The paper therefore asks a more basic question:

> In Reflexive Coherence, does a structure possess a function independently, or does its function arise from the contextual geometry through which coherence and flux relate it to the rest of the field?

The answer will not be assumed in one step. The full RC formalism first has to be restored, because the paper is not about any system with feedback. From that ground, geometric mediation can be separated from the stronger thesis of contextual constitution. Only then can regimes, primitives, portability, truth, and the Atlas be reconsidered.

The movement of the paper is therefore continuous. The failure of semantic progression to explain every path opens the possibility of fragments. Fragments require a holding context. The contrast between tension and generative affordance shows that several developmental morphologies may inhabit that context. Their coexistence makes it insufficient to classify the realization directly. Returning to the RC equations then becomes necessary, not as a theoretical detour, but to determine where function can exist at all.

# 2. The Reflexive Ground of Function

## 2.1 RC is not a generic reflexive loop

The contextual thesis would become trivial if RC were reduced to the statement that a system feeds its state back into itself. Many dynamical systems are reflexive in that broad sense. Reflexive Coherence is more constrained. The investigation must therefore begin from the full formulation rather than from the loop as a metaphor.

A reflexive coherence system is represented by a non-negative coherence density and its flux,

$$
\mathcal S_{\mathrm{coh}}=(C(x,t),J_C(x,t)),
\qquad
C(x,t)\ge 0,
\qquad
J_C^\mu=Cv_C^\mu.
$$

The coherence field satisfies local continuity,

$$
\partial_t C+\nabla_\mu J_C^\mu=0,
$$

while the geometry through which this continuity is evaluated is itself induced by the coherence tensor,

$$
K_{\mu\nu}[C]
=
\lambda_C Cg_{\mu\nu}
+
\xi_C\nabla_\mu C\nabla_\nu C
+
\zeta_C j_\mu j_\nu,
$$

with

$$
g_{\mu\nu}=g_{\mu\nu}[K[C]].
$$

The coherence functional

$$
\mathcal P[C]
=
\int
\left(
\frac{\kappa_C}{2}\nabla C\cdot\nabla C
-
V(C)
\right)
\sqrt{-g[C]}
\,d^4x
$$

supplies the variational condition, including geometric back-reaction because changing $C$ also changes the metric through $K[C]$. A valid solution must satisfy the continuity law, the induced metric relation, and the variational condition simultaneously. For a closed whole, the total coherence

$$
C_{\mathrm{sys}}(t)
=
\int_{\Omega_t}C(x,t)\,dV_g
$$

remains invariant.

The familiar reflexive loop

$$
C
\xrightarrow{K[C]}
g[K]
\xrightarrow{\nabla,dV_g}J_C
\xrightarrow{\partial_t C+\nabla\cdot J_C=0}C'
$$

compresses these relations. It does not replace them. The context considered in this paper is therefore not an optional semantic wrapper around a generic feedback system. It is formed inside a theory in which the state, the geometry of relation, and the transport that changes the state are mutually determining.

## 2.2 Why function is geometrically mediated

The full closure changes how a local structure can be understood.

Suppose a region $U\subset\Omega_t$ contains a recognizable formation $X$: a basin, ridge, channel, boundary, interface, local flux pattern, or composite of these. In a fixed-background theory it may be tempting to assign $X$ a function directly. A ridge is a boundary. A valley is a route. A basin is an identity. A high-coherence region is a source. The structure is named, and its behavior is treated as a consequence of what it is.

RC interrupts this assignment. The region contributes to $K_{\mu\nu}$, but the metric governing its gradients and flux is produced by the coherence organization of which the region is only a part. The flux through and around $U$ depends on that metric, the boundary relation, the surrounding field, and the current distribution of coherence. The update generated by the flux changes both $U$ and the geometry that gave the structure its role.

The effective movement is therefore not

$$
X\longrightarrow q,
$$

where $q$ is a function intrinsic to $X$. It is closer to

$$
(X,\Gamma)
\longrightarrow
(X',\Gamma',q),
$$

where $\Gamma$ is the geometry–flux context in which $X$ participates. The same visible local formation can have a different effect when the surrounding geometry routes coherence differently. Conversely, a different local structure can provide a similar effect when its relation to the wider field is transformed appropriately.

This is the first and strongest claim of the paper:

> **Within RC, function is geometrically mediated because every local effect is produced through the same field-induced geometry and flux that the local structure helps modify.**

Geometric mediation does not yet establish that function is wholly constituted by context. A structure may still carry constraints that remain decisive across contexts. A particular realization may make some transformations impossible. A basin with no retained coherence cannot be made into a stable identity merely by interpretation. The claim is narrower: no functional account is complete if it ignores how the structure participates in the self-induced geometry and the flow it organizes.

## 2.3 Local shape is not enough

The word *geometry* can still be misunderstood as a static shape. RC geometry is not only a visible profile of $C$ at one instant. It includes the metric and derivative relations induced by the field, the directions in which flux is available, the volume and boundary measures through which conservation is evaluated, and the history retained in the shape that receives the next update.

Two snapshots can look similar while belonging to different dynamics. One basin may be deepening under recurrent inflow; another may be temporarily preserved by external support. One channel may be a weak relaxation preference; another may be an active separatrix. One local multi-basin arrangement may be stable because the surrounding field absorbs pressure; another may be near collapse because the same local ambiguity is embedded in a rigid global geometry.

A regime therefore cannot be read from shape alone. The relevant object is a **geometric-dynamic organization**: shape together with the flux, boundary, support, scale, and history that maintain or transform it.

The core formulation already treats identity this way. An identity is not a point or label but a stable, attractive, reflexively closed basin whose continued engagement updates the geometry that makes future return possible. Collapse is not an external decision operator but a geometry-driven reorientation of flux among compatible basins. Learning is the retained geometric deformation through which later flow differs from earlier flow. Sparks occur when the present curvature organization can no longer sustain its attractor structure. These functions are not added to geometry; they are descriptions of how geometry and flux are organized through time.

The contextual thesis extends this same logic beyond the named core phenomena. If identity, choice, learning, and spark already depend on sustained geometric relations, then it is plausible that more interpretive functions—tension response, generative affordance, fragment holding, semantic progression, extraction, cultivation—also belong to contextual geometry rather than to isolated structures or realization labels.

## 2.4 Context is internal to the closure

Context often means surroundings external to the object under study. That meaning is insufficient here. In RC, the context of a region is partly produced by the same system and partly inherited through its relation with a wider field.

For a region $U$, a time interval $I=[t_0,t_1]$, a scale or scale range $\sigma$, and an activity history $H$, let a contextual geometry be represented provisionally as

$$
\Gamma(U,I,\sigma;H)
=
\left[
C,
J_C,
K,
g,
\partial U,
\mathcal N(U),
\mathcal E_{\mathrm{sup}},
H
\right]_{U,I,\sigma}.
$$

The notation is deliberately bracketed rather than asserted as a final state tuple. It indicates the relations that may be required to understand function:

- the local and neighboring coherence field;
- the flux entering, leaving, and circulating through the region;
- the induced tensor and metric relations;
- the region's boundary and permeability;
- the wider neighborhood or nonlocal coupling in which the region is embedded;
- the support ecology that stabilizes or perturbs it;
- the history through which the present geometry was formed;
- and the scale at which the function is being read.

These are not separate ingredients placed beside one another. Their composition is the context. A boundary matters because it filters flux. History matters because it changes the geometry encountered by the next event. Scale matters because a local basin can be a component of a different higher-scale regime. Support matters because the same apparent stability can be native in one case and externally maintained in another.

The context is therefore not a container around a formation. It is the relational condition through which the formation acquires a function.

## 2.5 History changes the meaning of the same encounter

The role of history deserves separate emphasis because it makes contextuality irreducible to spatial embedding.

Suppose the same perturbation $\delta$ is applied to an initial field $S_0$ and to a field $S_1$ formed after prior engagement. Even if the constitutive parameters and the perturbation definition remain fixed,

$$
\delta@S_0
\neq
\delta@S_1
$$

may hold because $S_1$ carries a different curvature, support distribution, boundary susceptibility, or flux alignment. The encounter differs not because the perturbation changed, but because the field that receives it has become different.

This is the field-theoretic expression of the enacted/understood-path distinction. Earlier activity does not only add content to a record. It changes the geometry through which later activity receives meaning and consequence. What looked like an unrelated fragment may later alter the response to another fragment because both have entered the same contextual history.

[Attunement of Becoming](../reflexive-coherence-development/2026-06-AttunementOfBecoming.md) states this relation in developmental language: the loop matters because it enters a history, and the history matters because it changes the loop. The contextual geometry of function supplies a formal interpretation of that sentence. A history is developmentally relevant when it deforms the condition under which future functions are expressed.

## 2.6 Scale makes context plural

The same region can participate in more than one contextual geometry because function is scale-dependent. [Fractal Reflexive Coherence](../core/2025-11-FractalReflexiveCoherence.md) makes this explicit by allowing coherence to be resolved across a generation or scale coordinate and by letting contributions from multiple scales participate in a collective geometry.

A local process may therefore be tension-responsive at one scale while contributing to generative composition at another. A basin can preserve identity locally while its interaction with neighboring basins forms a higher-order structure. A fragment can remain uncomposed at one observational scale while already participating in a wider coupling that only becomes legible later. Conversely, a globally coherent formation may contain local extraction or collapse.

This prevents the regime label from being treated as a single property of the whole realization. A statement such as “the system is generative” is incomplete unless it declares the region, time horizon, scale, and relation being described. The same realization may sustain

$$
R(U_1,I_1,\sigma_1)
\neq
R(U_2,I_2,\sigma_2)
$$

without contradiction. The difference may be the point of its organization.

## 2.7 Parameters constrain geometry; they do not replace it

The parameter-centered account was not wrong to focus on coefficients, potentials, initial conditions, boundaries, histories, scales, and implementations. These determine what the RC equations can express in a particular model. The correction is that their phenomenological effect is mediated by the geometries they make reachable.

Let $\mathcal M$ be an RC model or faithful realization, $\theta$ its admissible parameterization, $\eta$ its initial and boundary conditions, and $H$ its inherited history. Then the relevant object is not a direct regime function

$$
\Phi(\mathcal M,\theta,\eta,H)=R,
$$

but a reachable contextual-geometry relation

$$
\mathfrak G(\mathcal M,\theta,\eta,H)
=
\{\Gamma:\Gamma\text{ is reachable and sustainable under the declared conditions}\}.
$$

Regimes are then expressed through members or trajectories of this set:

$$
\Gamma\in\mathfrak G
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
R=\mathscr R[\Gamma].
$$

The relation may be many-to-many. One parameterization can reach several contexts. Several parameterizations can reach functionally similar contexts. A context may support more than one interpretation depending on scale and claim level. Some contexts may exist mathematically but remain unreachable from realistic histories. Others may appear only under continuous scaffolding and therefore fail naturalization.

Parameters remain indispensable for prediction and design, but they are one side of a mediation problem. The applied question is not merely which coefficient value produces a regime. It is which parameter and condition envelope makes the required contextual geometry reachable, stable, revisable, and native enough for the target function.

## 2.8 Mediation and constitution

The paper can now state its claim boundary precisely.

**Geometric mediation** says that function is expressed through the field-induced geometry and flux organization. This follows from the RC closure and should be treated as a strong consequence of the formalism.

**Contextual constitution** says that the identity of a function may itself depend on the contextual geometry: the same literal structure can become a different functional primitive in another context, and different literal structures can perform functionally corresponding roles when they preserve the relevant relational organization. This is the deeper thesis of the investigation.

The distinction matters because mediation alone is compatible with an intrinsic-function account. One could say that a structure has a fixed function but the geometry modulates its strength. Contextual constitution is stronger. It says that the geometry may determine which function the structure instantiates at all.

The remainder of the paper explores what follows if this stronger possibility is correct. The next step is not to enumerate types of context. It is to understand how a structure and its context compose into a function.

# 3. Contextual Geometry of Function

## 3.1 Function appears in a relation

A function is usually attributed to an object by compressing away the conditions that make the function reliable. A channel carries. A membrane contains. A basin preserves identity. A probe reveals. A classifier distinguishes. The compression is useful when the omitted context is stable, shared, and already understood. It becomes misleading when the same structure changes role as soon as its embedding changes.

RC makes this instability central rather than exceptional. A local structure contributes to the geometry that routes coherence, while the surrounding geometry determines how that contribution is received. The function therefore appears in the relation between a formation and a context, not in either one alone.

Let $X$ denote a local formation and let $\Gamma$ denote the contextual geometry in which it is embedded. A context-sensitive functional reading can be written schematically as

$$
\operatorname{Role}(X\mid\Gamma)=q,
$$

where $q$ names the selected functional or phenomenological class. The notation is intentionally asymmetric. It does not say that $\Gamma$ merely adds a parameter to an otherwise complete $X$. It says that the role cannot be evaluated without the context. In the stronger form,

$$
\operatorname{Role}(X\mid\Gamma_a)
\neq
\operatorname{Role}(X\mid\Gamma_b),
$$

not because the formation failed to perform an intrinsic role, but because the role itself changed with the relation.

The immediate consequence is methodological. When a structure is observed to produce an effect, the relevant claim is not “$X$ does $q$.” It is $\operatorname{Role}(X\mid\Gamma)=q$. That statement carries more information and leaves open the right next question: which parts of $\Gamma$ are necessary, which are incidental, and which can be transformed while $q$ remains available?

## 3.2 The context is a composition, not an inventory

The contextual notation from the previous chapter named coherence, flux, geometry, boundary, neighborhood, support, history, and scale. Read as a list, it would merely replace one classification scheme with a larger one. The point is not that function has many determinants. The point is that those determinants become functional only through their composition.

A boundary is not important simply because every region has one. It matters because it changes which fluxes cross, which gradients accumulate, and which outside structures become dynamically relevant. A history is not important as stored chronology. It matters because it changes present susceptibility. A scale is not an extra coordinate attached to an otherwise complete state. It matters because the same local event can stabilize one level while destabilizing another. Support is not an amount added to a basin. It matters because it may alter whether the basin can regenerate the conditions of its own persistence.

The context is therefore better understood as a network of functional dependencies. If one relation changes, the meaning of the others can change with it. Increasing boundary rigidity may turn a buffer into a pressure trap. Deepening a basin may turn a neighboring channel from a weak preference into an extractive drain. Adding a support structure may stabilize an identity while simultaneously preventing the identity from learning to create its own support.

This is why contextual geometry cannot be reconstructed from a list of local components. It is the composed organization through which those components constrain one another.

## 3.3 A primitive is an interface-bearing witness

The existing [Reflexive Coherence Language investigation](../investigations/2025-12-22-RCL-iteration3.md) already contains a useful precursor. It treats operational experience fragments as pattern witnesses with supports, interfaces, and phenomenological records. Expressions are not linear strings but composition trees whose seams must satisfy interface compatibility. Semantic sameness is defined through a quotient over compatible interfaces and observationally equivalent phenomenology rather than through equality of raw trajectories.

The present paper shifts that insight from language semantics toward dynamical function.

A primitive should not be represented only by an internal structure. It should also declare the conditions at its boundary through which it can compose. Provisionally, write

$$
\mathfrak p
=
\left(
X,
\partial X,
\mathcal C_{\mathfrak p},
\mathcal T_{\mathfrak p},
\mathcal I_{\mathfrak p}
\right),
$$

where $X$ is the realized formation, $\partial X$ its relevant interface, $\mathcal C_{\mathfrak p}$ the class of contextual geometries in which the primitive is admissible, $\mathcal T_{\mathfrak p}$ the transformation it performs there, and $\mathcal I_{\mathfrak p}$ the invariants or relations that must survive for the function to remain the same.

The distinction between $X$ and $\mathfrak p$ is deliberate. $X$ names a formation that participates in a contextual function; $\mathfrak p$ names the disciplined primitive obtained when that formation is specified with an interface, an admissibility domain, a transformation, and preservation obligations.

This is not a final definition of an RC primitive. It is a correction to the untyped picture. A primitive without an admissible context class is like an operator without a domain. The fact that it ran somewhere does not establish that it can be applied everywhere.

The interface also cannot be assumed complete. RCL already notes that persistent compositional failure may mean that the current interface is too coarse for the context class. In the present setting, a transfer that preserves mass and nominal boundary labels may still fail because it omitted pressure distribution, history, multiscale coupling, or support dependency. Contextual portability therefore requires interface enrichment until the relations that matter for function are visible.

## 3.4 The same structure can instantiate different functions

Consider a basin-like formation. In one context it may serve as an identity-bearing attractor: surrounding gradients feed it without allowing it to absorb neighboring structures, its boundary remains revisable, and repeated engagement deepens a stable return path. In another context the same local basin profile may act as an extractive sink because the surrounding field supplies no compensating support and nearby formations lose their own basin-forming capacity. In a third, it may be a transient pressure concentration maintained by an external fixture.

The literal object is similar. The function differs because the relational geometry differs.

The same applies to boundaries. A ridge can contain, isolate, reflect, filter, or create a false separatrix depending on the geometry around it. A valley can guide, leak, split, or remain subthreshold. A hub can coordinate, drain, trap, or merely mark topology. A feedback signal can become learning, brittle reinforcement, or proxy optimization depending on what part of the geometry receives the update and whether the effect survives withdrawal.

This is the practical meaning of contextual constitution. The function is not hidden inside the object waiting to be released. It is enacted by the object-context composition.

The proposition can be written as

$$
X_a\cong X_b
\quad\not\Rightarrow\quad
\operatorname{Role}(X_a\mid\Gamma_a)
=
\operatorname{Role}(X_b\mid\Gamma_b),
$$

where $\cong$ indicates literal or structural similarity. Structural sameness does not guarantee functional sameness.

## 3.5 Different structures may instantiate functionally corresponding roles

The inverse possibility is equally important. Two literal structures may differ substantially while preserving the relations required for a function.

A continuous PDE field may realize a holding boundary through a smooth pressure gradient. An adaptive support realization may realize a comparable role through local refinement and conservative face transport. A graph realization may realize it through edge conductance, packet constraints, and lineage-aware continuation. The structures are not identical, and their total dynamics need not be equivalent. Yet they may correspond with respect to a bounded function if they preserve the registered relations that make that function possible.

Let $q$ be a declared function or phenomenology. Write the bounded functional correspondence

$$
(X_a,\Gamma_a)
\approx_q
(X_b,\Gamma_b)
$$

when the two compositions satisfy the registered observables, invariants, and intervention responses required for $q$ within a declared tolerance and scope. This notation does not yet assert reflexivity, symmetry, transitivity, or closure under composition; later formal work must determine whether correspondence for a given $q$ forms an equivalence relation, a preorder, or another structure.

The relation is indexed by $q$ because correspondence is never complete by default. Two realizations may correspond with respect to conservative transfer but not with respect to causal history. They may both preserve a basin signature while differing in topology mutation, recovery, or naturalization. They may support the same phenomenological label while paying radically different ecological costs.

Bounded functional correspondence therefore does not collapse the differences among realizations. It specifies the exact sense in which one result may provisionally stand in for another.

## 3.6 Function is distributed across context

Once function is relational, it may not have a single local bearer.

A local primitive may appear to produce an effect, but the effect can depend on timing supplied elsewhere, boundary integrity supplied by another region, reserve supplied by a shared medium, or a classifier supplied by an observer. Removing any of these may dissolve the function while leaving the local structure unchanged.

This does not mean that the function is unreal. It means that its causal organization is distributed. A useful functional analysis asks not only which component changed, but which relations had to remain in place for the change to count as the same function.

The distinction between correlation and coupling in [Attunement of Becoming](../reflexive-coherence-development/2026-06-AttunementOfBecoming.md) becomes relevant here. Two regions can vary together because a third context drives both. Functional coupling requires a stronger result: changing one relation changes another region's capacity under comparator and withdrawal discipline. Contextual geometry therefore has to distinguish co-variation from the supply of a function.

The same caution applies to observers. A detector, prompt, schedule, or post-processing rule can supply the very boundary that makes a function appear. The context must include these supports when they are causally necessary. Otherwise the function is attributed to the wrong part of the system.

## 3.7 Context can be self-produced

If context were always externally imposed, RC function would depend on an infinite regress of containers. The theory avoids this because geometry is reflexively generated. A region participates in forming the context that later conditions its own function.

A basin can deepen through repeated return. A boundary can become more stable through the flows it filters. A path can become easier because prior activity changed curvature. A higher-scale regime can emerge from relations among lower-scale trajectories and then reshape the conditions under which those trajectories continue. The context is therefore both inherited and produced.

This reflexivity is the basis of naturalization. [Naturalization of Becoming](../arc-of-becoming/2026-05-NaturalizationOfBecoming.md) distinguishes the shape of a support from the function the support performs. A system does not naturalize a capacity merely by hard-coding a copy of the probe. It naturalizes the capacity when its own regime begins to generate a corresponding support function.

In contextual terms, naturalization means that the system can move from a context that must be externally assembled

$$
\Gamma_{\mathrm{scaffolded}}
$$

toward a context it can regenerate through its own dynamics

$$
\Gamma_{\mathrm{native}},
$$

while preserving the relevant function:

$$
(\mathfrak p,\Gamma_{\mathrm{scaffolded}})
\approx_q
(\mathfrak p',\Gamma_{\mathrm{native}}),
$$

with $\operatorname{Role}(\mathfrak p\mid\Gamma_{\mathrm{scaffolded}})=q=\operatorname{Role}(\mathfrak p'\mid\Gamma_{\mathrm{native}})$ under the registered tests.

The primitive may change during this movement. The context certainly does. What must persist is the functional relation and the declared RC constraints, not the literal shape of the original support.

## 3.8 Context Participates in Identity Unfolding Without Scripting It

Context participates in forming the identity and constraining its presently accessible continuations, but it does not contain a readable script of what the identity will become. The identity's unfolding is generated through a context that is itself rewritten by participation.

A seed begins a local differentiation within a particular geometry. That geometry shapes which continuations are currently available, how the identity exchanges coherence with its surroundings, and which functions it can express. Yet the context is not static. Participation changes coherence, altered coherence rewrites geometry, and the changed geometry forms the conditions of the next continuation.

The complete field, rather than the identity alone, carries the unique trajectory. Let $\Xi_t$ denote the complete admissible RC state. Subject to the core assumption of single-valued continuation,

$$
\tau_{\mathrm{RC}}
=
\operatorname{Evolve}(\Xi_{t_s}),
\qquad
\tau_{I_k}
=
\operatorname{Trace}_{I_k}(\tau_{\mathrm{RC}}).
$$

Under that assumption, immanent unfolding is the identity-relative expression and experience of $\tau_{I_k}$. If splitting, composition, change of scale, or dissolution makes one trace misleading, the broader record is $\mathcal L_{I_k}=\operatorname{Lineage}_{I_k}(\tau_{\mathrm{RC}})$. The field trajectory is determined by the complete configuration but unavailable as advance semantic knowledge; the identity encounters its unfolding only through transformations in which both identity and context change. The fuller account belongs to [The Ecological Formation of Identity](./2026-07-EcologicalFormationOfIdentity.md) and [The Ecology of Participation](./2026-07-EcologyOfParticipationInRC.md).

Context is therefore not external circumstance surrounding an already complete self. It is one of the constitutive relations through which identity forms and unfolding acquires its situated possibilities. Unfolding does not disclose a finished form hidden in the seed; that form becomes expressible only through the changing relation of identity and field.

Because this unfolding depends on persistent and recurrent organizations of contextual geometry, the next concept cannot be a static classification of a system. A regime must name how such a geometry continues through time.

# 4. Regimes as Geometric-Dynamic Organizations

## 4.1 A realization does not have to be one thing

Treating a regime as a recurring qualitative organization within a realization already prevents a trajectory from being promoted too quickly into a property of the theory. Contextual geometry requires a further step: a realization may sustain several regimes even at one time.

A complete RC realization is an evolving field. Different regions can carry different basin structures, boundary relations, support dependencies, and flux morphologies. Different scales can organize the same activity differently. One local context may be defensive and tension-responsive, another exploratory, another homeostatic, and another fragmenting. A higher-scale formation may coordinate these differences without making them uniform.

It is therefore misleading to say simply that a realization *is* generative, extractive, sequential, or fragmented. The more precise statement is indexed:

$$
R=R(U,I,\sigma;H),
$$

where $U$ is the region or support, $I$ the time interval, $\sigma$ the scale, and $H$ the relevant history.

The indexing is not clerical detail. It is what prevents a local regime from being projected onto the whole and what allows several forms of becoming to coexist without contradiction.

## 4.2 A regime sustains characteristic continuation

A geometric snapshot shows what shape exists. A regime shows what kind of continuation that shape makes possible.

A **regime** can therefore be understood as a persistent or recurrent geometry–flux organization through which a characteristic family of trajectories becomes available, stable, or likely. It includes more than the current basin arrangement. It includes how boundaries respond, where flux is routed, which supports are required, what histories are retained, what perturbations can be absorbed, and how the system tends to regenerate or lose the organization after disturbance.

This is close to the developmental definition in [Attunement of Becoming](../reflexive-coherence-development/2026-06-AttunementOfBecoming.md), where a regime is the condition-field that sustains trajectories. The contextual geometry thesis gives that condition-field a more explicit RC grounding. The regime is the lived persistence of a geometry, not merely a statistical label placed over repeated outputs.

Let $\Gamma_t$ denote the evolving context. A regime $R$ is suggested when there is a family of contexts

$$
\{\Gamma_t:t\in I\}
$$

that remains within a functionally recognizable organization despite internal variation and bounded perturbation. The contexts need not be identical. They must preserve the relations that make the characteristic continuation possible.

This means persistence is not stasis. A generative regime may continually form and dissolve local structures while preserving the wider capacity for composition. A homeostatic regime may redistribute coherence while maintaining a bounded identity. A fragment-compositional regime may preserve disconnection locally while increasing the future possibility of higher-order relation. What persists is the organization of possibility.

## 4.3 Regimes can coexist and contain one another

Once regimes are indexed by context, coexistence becomes expected rather than anomalous.

Suppose a realization contains regions $U_1$ and $U_2$. It may support

$$
R_{\mathrm{tension}}(U_1)
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
R_{\mathrm{generative}}(U_2)
$$

at the same time. The first region may be repairing a boundary rupture while the second explores new basin relations. A larger region $U_3\supset U_1\cup U_2$ may express a third regime that coordinates repair and exploration as complementary functions.

Likewise, a locally extractive process can participate in a globally generative regime if the extraction is bounded, replenished, and does not destroy neighboring capacity. The reverse is also possible: a locally stable or cooperative interaction can contribute to a globally extractive ecology if it depends on hidden support from elsewhere.

The regime relation is therefore not a flat partition of the state space. It is nested and scale-sensitive. A regime can contain subregimes, and a transition at one scale can occur without the same transition at another.

This resolves part of the earlier tension between competing developmental descriptions. Tension and generative affordance do not need to be rival essences of the whole system. They can be different local or temporal organizations inside one reflexive ecology.

## 4.4 Sequential and fragment-compositional regimes

The original question can now be restated in contextual terms.

A **semantically sequential regime** is one in which the active geometry strongly couples new formation to an already assembled basin. The next accessible movement tends to deepen, extend, refine, or differentiate the currently intelligible construction. Earlier and later activity remain locally connected enough that the actor can often understand the path prospectively.

This does not make the process simple or predetermined. It means that the context continuously preserves semantic adjacency. The geometry routes flux toward extensions of the active formation, and the history remains readable as one developing line.

A **fragment-compositional regime** has a different holding geometry. It allows several locally coherent formations to persist without immediate semantic coupling. Flux does not force each new formation to attach to the currently dominant basin. Boundaries preserve differentiation; memory preserves accessibility; support prevents the fragments from decaying; and later contextual change can create cross-couplings that were previously unavailable.

The regime is not defined by visible disconnection alone. To distinguish composition from fragmentation, it must preserve at least the possibility of later relation. That possibility may be carried by latent compatibility, a shared higher-scale geometry, retained interfaces, or a support ecology that keeps the fragments revisable.

The same realization may move between these regimes. A research process may begin fragment-compositionally while several concepts form independently, then enter a semantically sequential phase once a higher-order thesis becomes visible. A system may also move in the opposite direction when an existing path bifurcates and the formerly unified class no longer holds its differences.

The path reconstructed later is therefore not proof that the earlier regime was sequential. It may be the semantic readout produced when a fragment-compositional regime successfully formed a higher-order basin.

## 4.5 Tension-responsive and generative-affordance regimes

The same reframing applies to tension and generative affordance.

A **tension-responsive regime** is one in which significant reorganization is primarily activated by mismatch, instability, boundary pressure, incompatible continuations, or insufficient capacity. Activity falls toward maintenance or quiescence when those conditions are absent. The geometry routes flux toward repair, compression, selection, separation, or release.

Such a regime may be entirely appropriate. [Language of Becoming: Reinforcement](../arc-of-becoming/2026-01-LoB-Reinforcement.md) already distinguishes legitimate completion under scarcity, temporal pressure, and excessive perturbation from completion that has generalized beyond its originating conditions. Contextual geometry explains the distinction: the same compressive response can be competent in one geometry and rigid in another.

A **generative-affordance regime** is one in which differentiated availability can itself activate exploratory or compositional flux. The current organization does not have to be failing. The geometry makes room for new relation, and the system can engage that possibility without requiring tension to be created first.

The two regimes are not mutually exclusive. Generative activity can produce tension when a new formation exceeds current capacity. Tension resolution can release coherence or change boundaries so that generative composition becomes possible. A tension-responsive subregime can protect a larger generative regime from destructive coupling. What matters is the organization and scale of the relation, not the moral value of the label.

The corrected question is therefore not which driver RC has. It is which contextual geometries make one morphology dominant, how the system moves between them, and what those movements cost the surrounding field.

## 4.6 Dominance is also contextual

Even after a regime has been identified, the word *dominant* can obscure several different relations.

A regime may dominate through temporal occupancy, by being the state in which the system spends most of its time; through perturbational accessibility, when most accessible perturbations evoke it; or through basin extent, when it attracts most initial conditions. Repeated activity can also deepen its return paths, giving it historical dominance. At a higher scale, dominance may instead consist in constraining several different local regimes.

These forms of dominance need not coincide. A system may occupy a low-activity homeostatic state most of the time but return to generative exploration whenever reserve becomes available. A tension response may be brief in duration yet dominate the system's developmental history because every major transition must pass through it. A local regime may have high occupancy but little influence on the geometry of the whole.

The contextual thesis therefore turns dominance into a relation among reachability, persistence, recovery, scale, and flux—not a single scalar property of the realization.

This has practical consequences. Selecting parameters to “maximize generativity” may simply increase one visible output while shrinking the context that allows generativity to remain viable. A better design question asks whether the realization reliably returns to a geometry in which generative composition is available without rising extraction, fragility, or external support.

## 4.7 Regime transition is contextual reorganization

A regime transition occurs when the relations sustaining one characteristic continuation can no longer hold or when another organization becomes reachable.

Parameters can trigger such a transition, but so can history, boundary change, support withdrawal, topology mutation, scale shift, or the formation of a new basin. The same nominal parameter vector can therefore support different regimes depending on how the context was reached. This is the geometric basis of hysteresis and developmental path dependence.

Write the transition schematically as

$$
\Gamma_a
\xrightarrow{\Delta}
\Gamma_b,
$$

where $\Delta$ may be an external perturbation, an endogenous instability, an accumulated history, or a change in support. The regime changes when

$$
\mathscr R[\Gamma_a]
\neq
\mathscr R[\Gamma_b].
$$

The transition is not merely a label switch. It changes what later events can do. A fragment that was held may become integrable. A probe that once supplied a function may become unnecessary. A boundary that once protected identity may become an obstacle to cooperation. A tension that once demanded closure may become tolerable because the surrounding geometry has developed more integration bandwidth.

This is why regime analysis belongs to development rather than only to classification. The transition modifies the field of future possibility.

## 4.8 Phenomenology may be multiply realized

If regimes are contextual geometry–flux organizations, then corresponding phenomenology may appear across different realizations without requiring identical literal shapes.

Let $q$ be a phenomenological or functional class—for example, stable identity, fragment holding, generative persistence, or tension-responsive collapse. Define the candidate functional context class

$$
\mathfrak C_q
=
\{\Gamma:\Gamma\text{ satisfies the registered contextual conditions for }q\}
$$

without assuming that its members have already been partitioned by an equivalence relation.

A realization $\mathcal M$ can express $q$ when its reachable contextual geometry intersects that candidate class:

$$
q\text{ is realizable in }\mathcal M
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\mathfrak G(\mathcal M,\theta,\eta,H)
\cap
\mathfrak C_q
\neq
\varnothing.
$$

The condition is stronger than the existence of a similar-looking pattern. The context must be reachable, sustainable over the relevant interval, and sufficiently native for the claim being made. A geometry that appears only because an external process continuously restores it is evidence of supported expression, not necessarily native regime capacity.

This possibility of multiple realization is one of the strongest open consequences of the paper. It would explain how different RC substrates can support comparable phenomenology while preserving different internal structures. It would also explain why literal transfer often fails: the target realization does not need the same shape; it needs access to a context satisfying the same registered functional conditions.

The possibility remains bounded. Some realizations may not possess the dimensionality, memory, topology, boundary expressivity, or scale coupling required to reach a given context class. Contextuality does not make every function universally available. It gives a more precise criterion for availability.

## 4.9 Regime truth is not observer convenience

Because regimes are identified through context, one might worry that they become arbitrary descriptions chosen by the observer. That conclusion does not follow.

The observer may choose a region, interval, scale, and set of observables, but the field constrains what can be responsibly classified. Comparator behavior, perturbation, withdrawal, recurrence, and conservation accounting can show that a proposed regime boundary is wrong or too coarse. A context may be real even when no observer can reconstruct it completely; local irreducibility affects access, not the existence of the relation.

The observer's task is therefore not to invent a useful regime but to discover the lowest contextually adequate organization that explains and survives the available tests. This continues the observation-first discipline of the Arc of Becoming while relocating the object of classification from isolated output to geometry–flux context.

Once that relocation is made, primitives and implementations also have to be reconsidered. A primitive cannot be portable merely because its code, topology, or label survives. What has to survive is its functional context or a valid transformation of it.

# 5. Primitives, Implementation, and Portability

## 5.1 The static primitive assumption

Implementation work often proceeds by identifying a useful building block and then asking where else it can be installed. The primitive is treated as a stable unit: preserve its internal logic, adapt its input and output formats, and expect its function to remain available.

That assumption is reasonable when the surrounding execution model is fixed. It becomes unreliable in RC because the primitive participates in the geometry that determines its own effect. A basin, channel, split, boundary, packet loop, or learning update does not enter a neutral runtime. It enters a coherence field whose existing shape, flux routes, support ecology, history, and scale relations can change what the primitive becomes.

The primitive must therefore be understood less like a detachable part and more like a relation that can be enacted only when its conditions compose.

Instead of

$$
\mathfrak p:x\mapsto y,
$$

the relevant form is

$$
\mathfrak p_{\Gamma}:(x,\Gamma)
\longmapsto
(y,\Gamma'),
$$

where the primitive acts on the context and the context acts through the primitive. The output is not only a value. It is also a changed geometry that will condition later activity.

This formulation explains why a primitive can appear successful in one experiment yet fail when transferred unchanged. The failure may not be in the primitive's internal operation. The target context may not provide the geometry that lets the operation count as the same function.

## 5.2 Contextual typing

A portable primitive therefore needs a type that includes more than data shape.

A provisional contextual type can be written as

$$
\mathfrak p:
\mathcal C_{\mathrm{in}}
\longrightarrow
\mathcal C_{\mathrm{out}},
$$

where $\mathcal C_{\mathrm{in}}$ and $\mathcal C_{\mathrm{out}}$ are classes of contextual geometries rather than simple value domains. The type states what boundary, support, flux, history, and scale relations must hold before the primitive's function is admissible and what relations are expected after it acts.

For example, a split primitive is not fully typed by the fact that one support becomes two. Its contextual type may require conservation within tolerance, inherited boundary compatibility, reduction of the insufficiency that made the split necessary, and preservation of the external interface of the parent formation. A learning primitive is not fully typed by a changed state. It may require that prior engagement alters later response under the same perturbation, that the effect localizes to qualified geometry, and that the change survives active nulls and withdrawal.

The contextual type is therefore also a claim boundary. It prevents an implementation from promoting a structural change into a function when the relations that define the function are absent.

## 5.3 Literal copying and functional translation

The difference between structural and functional transfer can be stated directly:

> Copying a primitive preserves its form. Porting a primitive must preserve or reconstruct its functional geometry.

Suppose a source realization contains $(\mathfrak p_a,\Gamma_a)$ and a target realization contains context $\Gamma_b$. A literal copy creates $\mathfrak p_a$ inside $\Gamma_b$. Nothing guarantees that

$$
\operatorname{Role}(\mathfrak p_a\mid\Gamma_a)
=
\operatorname{Role}(\mathfrak p_a\mid\Gamma_b).
$$

The target may route flux differently, assign a different boundary role, lack the required support, or amplify a relation that was subthreshold in the source. The copied primitive can become inert, leaky, extractive, unstable, or functionally unrelated to its source reading.

Functional translation asks a different question: what structure in the target can participate in a context that satisfies the registered conditions for the declared target function?

The translated primitive $\mathfrak p_b$ may look different from $\mathfrak p_a$. The source channel may become a graded pressure imprint; the source node may become a distributed basin; the source memory trace may become history-dependent conductance; the source boundary may become a recurrent support relation. The preservation target is not the source appearance but the source function under declared invariants.

## 5.4 Three portability movements

Once context is included, portability has three basic forms. They are not separate techniques placed beside one another; they arise from deciding which side of the primitive-context relation can move.

### Adapt the primitive

If the target context should remain largely intact, the primitive can be transformed so that it performs the corresponding role within that geometry. Let

$$
F:\Gamma_a\rightarrow\Gamma_b
$$

be a declared context relation and let $T_F$ translate the primitive:

$$
\mathfrak p_b=T_F(\mathfrak p_a).
$$

The transfer succeeds functionally when the following relation approximately commutes:

$$
F\bigl(\mathfrak p_a(\Gamma_a)\bigr)
\approx_q
\mathfrak p_b\bigl(F(\Gamma_a)\bigr).
$$

This is the preferred movement when preserving the target context is itself part of the requirement. It treats the primitive as adaptable rather than universal.

### Adapt the context

If the primitive is fixed or well understood, the target context can be transformed into a geometry in which the primitive is known to function. Let $A$ be a context transformation such that

$$
A(\Gamma_b)
\in
\mathfrak C_q.
$$

The primitive can then operate in the adapted context. This movement is common in practice: install a runtime, create a boundary, add a scheduler, normalize inputs, or build a support layer that recreates the source conditions.

The danger is that the function may belong to the enclave created by $A$, not to the target realization. If $A$ must remain active indefinitely, the result is scaffolded portability.

### Co-adapt primitive and context

Often neither side can remain fixed. The target function appears only when the primitive and context move together toward a third organization:

$$
(\mathfrak p_a,\Gamma_a)
\longrightarrow
(\mathfrak p_c,\Gamma_c)
\longleftarrow
(\mathfrak p_b,\Gamma_b).
$$

This is likely the most general case for cross-substrate RC transfer. The primitive is re-expressed in the target's native geometry while the target develops enough support and interface structure to make the new expression possible.

Co-adaptation is not a compromise between imperfect copies. It is composition: the function is recreated through a new relation that preserves what matters and releases what belonged only to the source implementation.

## 5.5 The preservation contract

A mapping cannot be evaluated by its target output alone. It needs a preservation contract that states what bounded functional correspondence is being claimed.

For a proposed mapping

$$
F:(\mathfrak p_a,\Gamma_a)\rightarrow(\mathfrak p_b,\Gamma_b),
$$

the contract should declare:

$$
\mathfrak M_q
=
\left(
\mathrm{Src},
\mathrm{Tgt},
F,
\mathcal I^+,
\mathcal I^-,
\varepsilon,
\Sigma_{\mathrm{valid}},
\mathcal E
\right),
$$

where $\mathrm{Src}$ and $\mathrm{Tgt}$ are the source and target domains, $\mathcal I^+$ the relations claimed to be preserved, $\mathcal I^-$ the relations explicitly not preserved, $\varepsilon$ the tolerance, $\Sigma_{\mathrm{valid}}$ the valid scale and condition range, and $\mathcal E$ the evidence supporting the mapping.

This formal scaffold matters because two transfers can produce the same visible sign while preserving different functions. A basin label may survive while boundary integrity changes. A route may remain readable while conservation is violated. A stable output may persist only because hidden producer support reconstructs the source context after every step.

The contract converts “it works there too” into a bounded relation: it corresponds with respect to $q$, under these contexts, while preserving these invariants and losing these others.

## 5.6 Context imposition creates debt

Transforming a target context is not neutral. The new geometry can alter neighboring regimes, consume reserve, suppress incompatible structures, or require maintenance that is invisible if only the target primitive is measured.

This cost can be called **context-imposition debt**. It is the difference between making the function appear and making it belong coherently to the receiving field.

Debt may take several forms. The target context may need continuous external scheduling. A hard boundary may protect the primitive by exporting pressure. A support layer may deepen one basin while flattening neighboring basin-forming capacity. A translated route may create leakage or false separatrices. A local success may narrow the system's future reachable geometries.

The relevant accounting is ecological. A function is not generative merely because it persists. It is generative when its persistence does not depend on destroying or invisibly consuming the conditions of other viable formations. The generative/extractive distinction therefore applies not only to regimes but to portability itself.

A transfer evaluation should ask:

$$
\text{target function}
+
\text{RC invariants}
+
\text{neighboring capacity}
+
\text{support cost}
+
\text{recoverability}.
$$

The terms belong together because a mapping that preserves only the first can counterfeit portability by moving the missing function into the context.

## 5.7 Naturalization is contextual regeneration

Context-imposition debt leads directly to naturalization.

A scaffolded transfer may be valuable. It can show that the target realization is capable of expressing the function under selected conditions. The next question is whether the target can generate or substitute those conditions through its own dynamics.

Naturalization therefore has a contextual definition:

> A function is naturalized when the receiving RC regime can recurrently generate, restore, or substitute the contextual geometry required for that function without the original external scaffold.

This is stronger than survival after support withdrawal. A frozen trace may persist because it has not yet been challenged. Naturalization requires regeneration: perturb the required context, remove part of it, or move the system away from it, then observe whether the system can return to a functionally corresponding geometry under the registered tests.

Let $A$ be the external adapter that created the target context. A naturalization test asks whether there exists an endogenous evolution $N$ such that

$$
(\mathfrak p_b,N(\Gamma_b))
\approx_q
(\mathfrak p_b,A(\Gamma_b))
$$

and whether the relation recurs after bounded disruption.

The target does not need to copy the adapter. It needs to supply a corresponding role. That is why [Naturalization of Becoming](../arc-of-becoming/2026-05-NaturalizationOfBecoming.md) insists that the object is not the support shape but the function the support performed.

## 5.8 Realizations become geometry generators

The corrected view also changes the status of an implementation.

A realization is not only an embodiment of equations and not only a container for primitives. It is a generator and transformer of contextual geometries. Its important characteristics include:

- which contexts it can form without external construction;
- which functions those contexts support;
- which contexts can coexist or nest;
- which transitions it can make endogenously;
- which histories it can retain;
- which mappings preserve function into and out of it;
- and which adaptations create unacceptable debt.

This gives substrate differences a precise role. PDE, voxel, graph, and event-driven realizations need not compete for the title of the one true implementation. They may expose different reachable geometry spaces. One may be especially expressive for continuous pressure and curvature. Another may make adaptive support capacity visible. Another may represent causal history, topology mutation, and lineage more directly.

The question is not which substrate is universally superior. It is which contextual functions each substrate can realize faithfully, which mappings connect those functions, and where correspondence fails.

## 5.9 Portability returns us to truth

The portability problem reveals a philosophical issue that was already latent in the original progression question. If the same primitive can change function across contexts, and different structures can realize the same function, what remains absolutely true about the primitive or the regime?

One response would be to retreat to local descriptions: everything is true only in its own context. That would make mappings impossible and theory weak. Another response would be to ignore context and search for an intrinsic structure common to every realization. That would repeat the mistake the contextual thesis has exposed.

A third possibility is available. Truth can be located in the relation that remains invariant across declared contextual transformations. The next chapter develops that possibility.

# 6. Truth, Invariance, and the Scope of Claims

## 6.1 What the contextual thesis challenges

The contextual geometry of function challenges a common form of absolute statement:

> Structure $X$ has function $q$.

The statement appears universal because it omits the conditions under which $X$ was observed. If $X$ changes function when its geometry, boundary, history, support, or scale changes, then the omission is not abstraction but loss of the object that made the claim true.

The corrected proposition is not weaker:

$$
\operatorname{Role}(X\mid\Gamma)=q
$$

states that $X$ expresses $q$ in contextual geometry $\Gamma$. It becomes stronger when the context class is widened and the function remains invariant:

$$
\forall\Gamma\in\mathcal C,
\qquad
\operatorname{Role}(X\mid\Gamma)=q.
$$

The difference is that universality is now quantified rather than assumed.

This does not mean that there is no absolute truth inside RC. It means that absoluteness cannot be obtained merely by deleting context from a sentence. A theorem about every admissible RC model is absolute relative to the theory. A property invariant under a declared class of transformations is universal across that class. A contextual observation can be objectively true of one region, interval, scale, and history. The scope changes; truth does not dissolve.

## 6.2 Context-complete propositions

A functional statement becomes **context-complete** when it includes the relations required to determine its claim ceiling.

A minimal form is

$$
\mathsf{Claim}
=
\left(
q,
X,
\Gamma,
\mathcal O,
\mathcal I,
\mathcal W,
\ell
\right),
$$

where $q$ is the selected function or phenomenology, $X$ the participating structure, $\Gamma$ the context, $\mathcal O$ the observables, $\mathcal I$ the preserved invariants, $\mathcal W$ the interventions or withdrawal conditions, and $\ell$ the claim ceiling.

The notation is not intended to turn every sentence into a tuple. It reveals what is lost when a claim is compressed too early. Calling a basin generative without measuring neighborhood capacity omits the relation that distinguishes generativity from focal survival. A claim that a mapping preserves identity remains incomplete unless it declares the basin signature, boundary mapping, and hidden support that make identity transfer meaningful. Likewise, describing a state as native without testing withdrawal omits the relation that distinguishes internal capacity from scaffolded expression.

A context-complete claim can later be compressed into ordinary language because the omitted relations have been registered elsewhere. Without that registration, the compression becomes overclaim.

## 6.3 Formal truth

The strongest truths in the present investigation are formal consequences of the RC definition.

A valid RC model must satisfy the defining continuity, induced-geometry, variational, and invariant relations under its declared assumptions. A faithful realization must implement or preserve the relevant form of those relations at its chosen substrate and resolution. Any function expressed by the system is mediated through the closure because the update of $C$ depends on flux evaluated in the geometry induced by $C$.

These truths are not context-free in the sense of being free from assumptions. They are universally quantified over the models admitted by those assumptions:

$$
\forall\mathcal M\in\operatorname{Mod}(\mathrm{RC}),
\qquad
\mathcal M\models\mathcal A_{\mathrm{RC}}.
$$

The absolute content lies in the quantification and the invariant relation, not in pretending that every solution looks the same.

This distinction is important for existing claims about abundance, identity proliferation, or instability. A proposition may be formally universal, conditional on a potential or coefficient region, generic over a model family, or demonstrated only in one realization. The contextual thesis does not decide those quantifiers automatically. It makes their declaration unavoidable.

## 6.4 Conditional and contextual truth

Many useful RC claims will be conditional:

$$
\forall\Gamma\in\mathcal C_H,
\qquad
H(\Gamma)\Rightarrow \operatorname{Supports}(\Gamma,q),
$$

where $H$ states the geometric conditions under which the function is expected.

For example, fragment-compositional development may require persistent local basins, retained interface accessibility, bounded mutual interference, sufficient support across integration latency, and a later mechanism of endogenous cross-coupling. If those conditions are made explicit, the claim can be tested across several realizations. It becomes more universal as the condition class is shown to be sufficient across wider variations.

A contextual truth can also concern one actual case:

$$
\operatorname{Supports}\bigl(\Gamma(U,I,\sigma;H),q\bigr)=\mathrm{true}.
$$

Its specificity does not make it subjective. The context is part of the physical or computational state being described. The proposition can be wrong if the flux, support, boundary, or history does not support the claimed function.

Context therefore changes the address of truth, not its objectivity.

A context-complete functional proposition can be objectively true without making the function desirable. That a geometry supports persistence, generativity, extraction, collapse, or identity dissolution is a claim about dynamics. Whether such a function is valued, resisted, or cultivated is indexed to an identity or ecology formed within those dynamics. Contextual truth identifies what relation exists; contextual valuation describes how that relation is experienced and oriented from within it. Thus

$$
\operatorname{Supports}(\Gamma,q)=\mathrm{true}
\quad\not\Rightarrow\quad
q\text{ ought to be cultivated}.
$$

## 6.5 Relational truth and preserved function

Portability introduces another form: truth about a mapping.

A mapping $F$ preserves function $q$ when

$$
(\mathfrak p_a,\Gamma_a)
\approx_q
(\mathfrak p_b,\Gamma_b)
$$

under the declared preservation contract. The truth is neither a property of the source alone nor of the target alone. It is a property of their relation.

This kind of truth may be more fundamental for an Atlas than object-level identity. Two realizations can disagree in almost every literal detail yet support a reliable mapping for one function. Another pair can look similar and fail because the mapping does not preserve a hidden support relation.

Relational truth is also compositional only under conditions. If

$$
F:(\mathfrak p_a,\Gamma_a)\rightarrow(\mathfrak p_b,\Gamma_b)
$$

preserves $q_1$, and

$$
G:(\mathfrak p_b,\Gamma_b)\rightarrow(\mathfrak p_c,\Gamma_c)
$$

preserves $q_2$, it does not automatically follow that $G\circ F$ preserves either function. The losses and context assumptions must compose. This is one reason a later categorical treatment is needed: not to decorate the theory with abstract structure, but to determine when relational truths survive composition.

## 6.6 Existence and impossibility

The reachable contextual-geometry space supports two further claims.

An existence claim states that a realization can reach a context supporting function $q$:

$$
\exists\Gamma\in\mathfrak G(\mathcal M,\theta,\eta,H)
\quad\text{such that}\quad
\operatorname{Supports}(\Gamma,q).
$$

An impossibility claim states that no reachable context in the declared space can support it:

$$
\nexists\Gamma\in\mathfrak G(\mathcal M,\theta,\eta,H)
\quad\text{such that}\quad
\operatorname{Supports}(\Gamma,q).
$$

The second claim is much stronger than failure to observe $q$. A negative experiment may mean that the relevant context was not reached, that the primitive was mistranslated, that the observation interface was insufficient, or that the support was withdrawn too early. Proving impossibility requires showing that the realization's admissible geometry cannot satisfy the functional conditions.

This distinction protects both theory and implementation. It prevents a failed literal transfer from being treated as evidence that the phenomenology is substrate-specific, and it prevents one successful scaffolded demonstration from being treated as evidence of native universal capacity.

## 6.7 Phenomenological truth

The Arc of Becoming and Reflexive Coherence Development use terms such as cultivation, fragmentation, generative persistence, completion-drive, agency, and attunement. These are not merely subjective labels, but neither are they identical to the underlying field variables.

A phenomenological claim maps a measured or observed geometry–flux organization into a developmental class:

$$
\iota:
(\Gamma,H,\mathcal O)
\longmapsto
q_{\mathrm{phen}}.
$$

The mapping is bounded by its interface and evidence. The same field behavior may admit several interpretations at different scales. A felt tension may correspond to several geometric mechanisms. A generative output may be produced extractively. A locally coherent regime may sustain one identity while reducing measured neighboring capacity or being experienced as loss from another position.

Three claim types must therefore remain distinct. That a transition necessarily occurred is a **trajectory truth** about the field's evolution. That it was experienced as tension, expansion, or loss is a **phenomenological truth** about its relation to a local identity. That it was good, bad, or worth cultivating expresses an **identity-relative valuation** formed within an identity or ecology. These claims can all be true at once because they answer different questions.

The first relation belongs to the complete field trajectory $\tau_{\mathrm{RC}}$. Immanent unfolding names the expression and experience of an identity-relative trace $\tau_{I_k}=\operatorname{Trace}_{I_k}(\tau_{\mathrm{RC}})$, not a separate continuation or a semantic interpretation written into that trace in advance. Meaning belongs to the geometries through which the trace becomes intelligible, while valuation belongs to formations capable of preferring, resisting, or cultivating what they experience.

Phenomenological truth therefore requires a preservation path back to the dynamics. The label must identify which relations it compresses and what observations would demote or refine it. This is consistent with RCL's treatment of phenomenological sameness as a quotient over typed interfaces and observational records rather than raw trajectory equality.

The contextual thesis adds that the interface itself may need to change when a label fails under new composition. Truth remains revisable because the context class can disclose distinctions the previous vocabulary could not hold.

## 6.8 Native and emergent are relational

It remains necessary to distinguish formal nativity, model emergence, realization nativity, regime nativity, supported expression, and interpretation. Contextual geometry shows that each is relationally situated.

A property is **formal-native** when it follows from the RC equations under declared assumptions.

A function is **model-emergent** when a model admits contextual geometries that express it without the function being explicitly inserted as an operator.

A function is **realization-native** when the implementation generates and sustains the required context through its own registered dynamics rather than through hidden external construction.

A function is **regime-native** when the current regime recurrently generates the preconditions for its expression, including after bounded disruption.

A function is **supported** when an external context or adapter supplies a necessary relation.

A function is **interpreted** when a phenomenological vocabulary compresses the observed organization at a declared claim ceiling.

The same function can occupy different levels in different realizations. Generative persistence may be externally staged in one experiment, realization-native but not self-restoring in another, and regime-native in a third. “Native” is therefore not an intrinsic badge attached to a primitive. It describes where the required context comes from.

## 6.9 Universality as contextual invariance

The contextual account now permits a stronger formulation of universality:

> A function is universal across a class of RC contexts when it remains invariant under every admissible transformation in that class, subject to declared tolerances and preserved constraints.

Let $\mathcal F$ be a family of contextual transformations. Then $q$ is invariant over $\mathcal F$ when

$$
\forall F\in\mathcal F,
\qquad
(X,\Gamma)
\approx_q
(F_X(X),F_\Gamma(\Gamma)).
$$

This is not the removal of context. It is a theorem about how context may vary without changing the function.

Such universality is more demanding than a context-free label. It requires discovering the relevant context class, building transformations, testing preservation, and identifying failure boundaries. Yet it is also more useful because it tells us when knowledge transfers.

Absolute truth, under this view, is not a view from nowhere. It is what remains valid under every transformation admitted by the proposition.

## 6.10 The claim discipline of the paper

The central thesis can now be stated with its own ceiling.

The paper claims strongly that RC function is geometrically mediated. It proposes that many RC functions and phenomenologies are contextually constituted. It derives the structural consequences that would follow: regimes become local and multiscale geometry–flux organizations; primitives become contextually typed; portability becomes a mapping of primitive and context; and universality becomes invariance across context classes.

It does not claim that every function is entirely contextual, that every realization can express every regime, that the algebraic status of the required correspondence relations is already known, or that current experiments prove native contextual regeneration.

This boundary determines what the final constructive object can be. The Atlas should not catalogue claims as though their correspondence and portability were already settled. It should record the contexts, mappings, reachability, and evidence through which those claims may become settled.

# 7. Toward an Atlas of Contextual Realizability

## 7.1 Why Contextual Geometry Requires an Atlas

The preceding chapters have changed what it means to describe an RC realization. A realization cannot be characterized adequately by listing its parameters, primitives, or observed regimes, because none of these carries a complete functional meaning in isolation. Parameters constrain what may form. Primitives participate in transformations. Regimes arise through sustained organizations of geometry and flux. Their significance becomes determinate only within the contexts that compose them.

This creates a practical problem for the theory. If the same realization can sustain different contexts across its regions, scales, and histories, then assigning it one functional profile conceals its internal diversity. If the same phenomenology can emerge through different literal structures, then organizing knowledge only by realization conceals cross-realization continuity. And if a primitive changes its role when its context changes, then cataloguing primitives independently of their admissible geometries records their form while losing their function.

What must be organized is therefore not a collection of isolated systems, but a chain of realizability:

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{realization, parameters, boundaries, and history}\\
&\qquad\longrightarrow
\text{reachable contextual-geometry trajectories}\\
&\qquad\longrightarrow
\text{regimes and contextual functional roles}\\
&\qquad\longrightarrow
\text{transitions, mappings, and preserved relations}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Each part of this chain changes the meaning of the next. A realization matters through the contextual-geometry trajectories it can generate and sustain. Such a trajectory matters through the regimes and contextual functional roles it makes possible. A function becomes portable only through mappings that preserve or reconstruct the relations on which it depends. A regime becomes applicable only when the path by which its geometry can be reached and sustained is also understood.

The Atlas follows from this dependency. It is not merely a catalogue of RC implementations, nor a taxonomy imposed upon their behavior. It is a map of **contextual realizability**: which geometry–flux organizations support which functions, where those organizations can arise, how they transform, what they cost to maintain, and which aspects of them survive movement across contexts or realizations.

This also gives the Atlas its applicative role. It should make it possible to move in both directions: from a realization and its conditions toward the functions it can support, and from a function selected by an identity, ecology, or designer toward the contextual geometry, developmental path, and realization capable of sustaining it. To do that, however, the Atlas must first distinguish the kinds of objects and relations it records.

## 7.2 The objects of the Atlas

The first object is the **realization profile**. It records the fixed RC fidelity boundary, the substrate, constitutive specification, numerical or operational policies, and the kinds of state and history the realization can represent. This is still necessary because the reachable context space depends on the capacities of the realization.

But the profile is only the entrance to the Atlas. The central object is a **context witness**:

$$
W_\Gamma
=
\left(
\Gamma,
\mathcal O,
H,
\mathcal B,
U,
\sigma,
\ell
\right),
$$

where $\Gamma$ is the observed geometric organization, $\mathcal O$ the evidence and observables, $H$ the path by which it was formed, $\mathcal B$ the boundary and support conditions, $U$ its region, $\sigma$ its scale or scale range, and $\ell$ the claim ceiling.

A context witness can support a **function record** that states which primitive or composite participated, what function appeared, what controls separated it from alternatives, and what neighboring capacities changed.

Repeated or robust context witnesses can support a **regime record**. A regime record does not assert that the whole realization has become one thing. It records the region, interval, scale, persistence, recovery, support dependency, and transition relations through which a characteristic geometry–flux organization recurs.

Mappings among these records then form a **transfer record**: what was transformed, what function was preserved, what context changed, what debt accumulated, and whether the result became native.

These objects compose into one Atlas entry because none is sufficient alone. A realization without contexts says only what was built. A context without history says only what appeared. A function without controls risks attribution error. A transfer without debt accounting can hide extraction. The Atlas becomes useful when these fragments remain connected.

## 7.3 The forward question

The applicative value of the Atlas begins with prediction.

Given a realization $\mathcal M$, parameterization $\theta$, conditions $\eta$, and history $H$, the first forward question is not simply which regime will appear. It is:

$$
(\mathcal M,\theta,\eta,H)
\longmapsto
\mathfrak G_{\mathrm{reachable}}.
$$

Which contextual geometries can this system form? Which are stable, transient, metastable, scaffolded, or inaccessible? Which can coexist? Which can be restored after perturbation? Which are available only at one scale?

Only after this map is partly known can the Atlas ask

$$
\Gamma
\longmapsto
\{q_1,R_1;q_2,R_2;\ldots\},
$$

where the same context may support several bounded functions and interpretations.

The practical output is therefore an expectation envelope rather than one label. It might say that a parameter and history region reliably forms a geometry that preserves multiple local basins, supports long integration latency, and permits later cross-coupling, while also identifying a neighboring transition into fragmentation when support falls below a threshold.

This is more informative than saying that the parameterization is fragment-compositional. It explains the geometric condition through which the regime exists and therefore what should be measured when the implementation changes.

## 7.4 The inverse question

The inverse problem also gains an intermediate step.

Suppose an identity, designer, or ecology has formed an orientation toward generative-affordance-dominant fragment composition. That orientation supplies the target phenomenology; RC does not select it as globally preferable. A parameter-first search would tune coefficients until selected indicators rise. Contextual design first asks what geometry makes the selected function possible:

$$
q^*
\longmapsto
\mathfrak C_{q^*}.
$$

Only then does it ask which realizations and operating envelopes can reach that geometry class:

$$
\mathfrak C_{q^*}
\longmapsto
\left\{
(\mathcal M,\theta,\eta,H)
:
\mathfrak G(\mathcal M,\theta,\eta,H)
\cap
\mathfrak C_{q^*}
\neq\varnothing
\right\}.
$$

This two-stage inverse problem prevents blind optimization. It also allows cross-realization learning. The literal parameter values of a PDE and a graph model may not correspond, but both may have controls over boundary permeability, memory, basin depth, support distribution, or cross-basin coupling that move them toward contexts satisfying the same registered functional conditions.

The Atlas can therefore recommend not only parameter values but **geometric levers**: deepen these basins without increasing neighboring extraction; preserve these interfaces across withdrawal; reduce immediate coupling while retaining future accessibility; increase history retention without freezing the field; create support that can later be regenerated internally.

Parameters remain how the lever is implemented. Contextual geometry explains what the lever is meant to change.

## 7.5 Progression as a contextual-realizability inquiry

The inverse question becomes concrete in the progression problem with which the paper began. Rather than deciding abstractly which form of development characterizes RC, the Atlas can treat the alternatives as target phenomenologies and ask which contextual geometries make each one available.

The relevant targets are:

- semantically sequential development;
- fragment-compositional development;
- tension-responsive reorganization;
- generative-affordance-driven composition.

These labels should not be assigned to whole realizations. The inquiry should instead seek the geometry classes that distinguish them.

For semantic progression, the investigation would ask which contexts keep later formation adjacent to the active basin and preserve prospective intelligibility.

For fragment composition, it would ask which contexts preserve multiple differentiated partial formations, allow long integration latency, maintain revisability, and later support endogenous higher-order coupling without continuous external assembly.

For tension response, it would ask whether significant flux reorganization depends causally on mismatch, pressure, or instability and whether low-tension conditions produce maintenance rather than exploration.

For generative affordance, it would ask whether low-tension differentiated availability can initiate composition, whether the new formation expands future viable relations, and whether the surrounding field pays an extractive cost.

These questions can be tested within one realization as well as across several. The strongest same-realization test would hold constitutive specification fixed while allowing different histories or regions to form different contexts, then apply matched encounters. The strongest cross-realization test would preserve a declared functional geometry while changing substrate and literal structure.

The progression question has therefore not been displaced by the broader theory. It has become a concrete case through which the theory can demonstrate its usefulness.

## 7.6 Parameters remain applicable

The contextual correction should not be mistaken for a retreat from application into abstraction. It changes the applied workflow.

A parameter sweep without a geometric hypothesis produces correlations. A contextual investigation uses parameters to probe the reachability and stability of a proposed geometry. It asks which coefficient or policy changes boundary elasticity, which changes cross-basin coupling, which changes support cost, which changes history retention, and which changes the transition surface between two context classes.

The applied arc is therefore:

$$
\text{function selected by a present orientation}
\rightarrow
\text{required contextual geometry}
\rightarrow
\text{candidate geometric levers}
\rightarrow
\text{realization-specific parameters}
\rightarrow
\text{regime and withdrawal tests}.
$$

This is more precise than direct parameter–regime cartography because it makes the mechanism visible. It is also more portable because the same geometric lever may be realized through different parameters on different substrates.

## 7.7 The relation to a later categorical paper

The present investigation introduces bounded functional correspondence, context mappings, preservation contracts, and composition. It does not yet determine whether the relevant correspondence is an equivalence relation, a preorder, or another structure, nor whether the objects and mappings form one category or several linked categories.

A later categorical paper would have a concrete object to formalize. It could ask whether contextual geometries, contextually typed primitives, and function-preserving transformations admit identities and composition; whether regime fibers over realizations have a useful indexed structure; whether model-to-realization, realization-to-context, and context-to-phenomenology mappings behave functorially; and how approximate or lossy mappings should be represented.

The order matters. The category should be built from distinctions that survive actual RC comparison, not imposed before the objects of comparison are clear.

The present paper therefore prepares the categorical work by identifying what a morphism would have to preserve: not merely structure, but the contextual geometry of function.

## 7.8 A coupled theoretical and applicative program

The Atlas and the categorical work described above require theoretical refinement and applicative testing to advance together. The resulting research program is organized through two coupled movements.

The theoretical movement is:

$$
\text{contextual geometry investigation}
\rightarrow
\text{Atlas v0}
\rightarrow
\text{categorical formalization}
\rightarrow
\text{Atlas v1}.
$$

The applicative movement runs through the same stages:

$$
\text{identify functional geometry}
\rightarrow
\text{map existing evidence and gaps}
\rightarrow
\text{formalize transferable mappings}
\rightarrow
\text{support prediction and inverse design}.
$$

The two movements are not parallel tracks. Each corrects the other. Atlas v0 tests whether the theoretical context distinctions can classify real experiments. Failed transfer reveals missing geometry and forces refinement. Categorical formalization should include only the mappings that survive. Atlas v1 then becomes a tool for finding which contextual transformations can cultivate a function selected by the present orientation of an identity or ecology.

This is the reflexive completion of the paper's argument. A question about the shape of a path disclosed a question about the location of function. That question reorganized the meaning of parameters, regimes, primitives, truth, and application. The resulting framework returns to the original path not with a single answer, but with a way to determine which answer the formed geometry supports.

# Conclusion: Function Belongs to the Geometry of Relation

The inquiry began with the observation that development can be chronologically continuous without being semantically continuous. An actor may move through partial formations whose relation remains unavailable until a later whole changes the geometry of understanding. What looks like a path retrospectively may not have been available as a path prospectively.

That observation first suggested a plurality of developmental regimes: sequential and fragment-compositional, tension-responsive and generative. Trying to classify those regimes exposed a hidden scope problem. The RC formalism is precise, but not every phenomenological claim made from it is necessarily universal. Parameters, realizations, histories, and conditions matter.

Within this inquiry, a natural first response was to make those levels the primary organization of diversity. The correction developed here is that they act through a more immediate object: the contextual geometry formed by the RC field–geometry–flux closure.

A local structure in RC does not produce its effect independently. It changes and is changed through the metric, gradients, boundaries, flux routes, supports, histories, and scales in which it participates. Function is therefore geometrically mediated. The stronger possibility is that function is contextually constituted: a literal structure can change functional identity across contexts, while different structures can perform functionally corresponding roles when they preserve the registered geometry–flux relations.

Contextual geometry does not assign a predetermined semantic purpose to a structure. It participates in the determined but irreducible field trajectory through which that structure's functional identity is expressed, transformed, or dissolved. The complete state $\Xi_t$ carries the field's continuation; a local context contributes to an identity's trace without containing its script.

From this possibility, the rest of the paper follows.

A realization is not one regime. It is a generator of reachable contextual geometries and may support several regimes at once. A regime is not a parameter label. It is a sustained organization of geometry and flux through which characteristic continuation becomes available. A primitive is not context-free. It is typed by the geometries in which its function can be enacted. Portability is not copying. It is the transformation of primitive, context, or both under a preservation contract. Naturalization is not retaining a scaffolded shape. It is the endogenous regeneration or substitution of the function that the scaffold supplied.

Truth is not lost in this movement. It becomes more carefully located. Formal truths remain consequences of the RC equations. Contextual truths are objective claims about declared regions, scales, histories, and geometries. Universal truths are invariants across specified classes of contextual transformation. Relational truths state what a mapping preserves. The context-free sentence is not stronger merely because it is shorter.

The resulting thesis can be stated compactly:

> **Reflexive Coherence permits—and its closure may require for many functions—a contextual geometry of function. Realizations and parameters constrain which geometries can form; contextual geometries sustain regimes; regimes make functions and phenomenologies available; and valid transfer depends on preserving or reconstructing the relations through which those functions exist.**

This is an investigation, not a completed theorem. Existing experiments already show that structural roles can change under substrate geometry, that history can alter later response, that support capacity and policy can be jointly necessary, that bounded basin signatures can survive declared mappings, and that focal persistence can be separated from effects on neighboring capacity. These results make the thesis plausible. They do not yet prove full multiple realizability or native contextual regeneration.

The next work is therefore not to classify every RC system into a fixed taxonomy. It is to learn how to recognize the geometry through which a function becomes possible, how to test whether that geometry is native, how to transform it without hidden loss, and how to state what remains true across the transformation.

The original question can now be asked with greater precision:

> Which contextual geometries allow a reflexive system to proceed through semantically continuous construction, and which allow it to preserve disconnected partial formations until a later composition makes their path visible?

The answer will not be found in a universal preference for tension, generative affordance, linearity, or fragmentation. It will be found in the shapes of relation that RC can form—and in the invariants through which different shapes can support boundedly corresponding roles without becoming the same structure.

# Appendix A: Existing Evidence and Present Claim Ceiling

This appendix does not provide the foundation of the paper. The contextual thesis was developed from the RC closure and its consequences for function. The experiments collected here serve a narrower purpose: they show that several predicted boundaries have already appeared in practice and identify where the stronger thesis remains unresolved.

## A.1 Functional geometry in fixed-substrate PDE-RC

[Paper 26G: Functional Geometry Tutorial](https://github.com/urosj/reflexive-coherence-sim/blob/main/experiments/papers/26G-FunctionalGeometryTutorialFromAbstractCellsToStablePDECells.md) provides the clearest existing precursor to contextual function.

The experiment began with abstract cell structures whose intended semantics were legible: nucleus, membrane, organelles, channels, and routing hub. Under the fixed PDE-RC runtime, those literal structures did not preserve their intended roles. A high-coherence nucleus could become an independent support source. A strong membrane could become a separatrix. Low-coherence channels could become detector-legible grooves or leakage surfaces. A hub could become a source, sink, or false routing center.

The stable versions were obtained by translating semantic parts into pressure roles. The nucleus became identifiable through a harder local boundary surrounded by pressure buffers rather than through a standalone high basin. Organelles became low-contrast baffles with internal damping. Channels became subthreshold relaxation imprints. The hub became pressure-neutral before it could be investigated as a router.

The bounded result supports three propositions:

1. literal structural similarity does not preserve functional role under a changed RC context;
2. the surrounding geometry can determine whether a structure contains, routes, splits, leaks, or acts as an independent support;
3. functional transfer may require co-adaptation of the primitive and its surrounding pressure geometry.

The experiment does not prove that all RC function is contextually constituted. It is a source-geometry result under one PDE runtime and a retained-control claim discipline. It nevertheless provides direct implementation evidence against context-free primitive identity.

## A.2 History-dependent response in PDE-RC

[Paper 29: Learning as Geometric Aftereffect](https://github.com/urosj/reflexive-coherence-sim/blob/main/experiments/papers/29-LearningAsGeometricAftereffectInPDE-RC.md) formalizes the comparison

$$
\delta@S_0\neq\delta@S_1,
$$

where the same perturbation $\delta$ is applied to an initial substrate $S_0$ and to a post-engagement substrate $S_1$. The program separates a changed state from a changed response, a diagnostic geometry-mediated response, regional specificity, active-null replication, persistence, functional retuning, and learning-profile dynamics.

This experiment directly supports the need to include history in context. The same input cannot be assumed to instantiate the same functional encounter when the field that receives it has changed. The paper's ladder also illustrates the claim discipline required here: a response difference does not automatically establish geometry-mediated causation, semantic learning, or native memory.

The current evidence therefore supports history-dependent contextual response and a method for strengthening the claim. It does not by itself establish that a full regime has changed or that the aftereffect corresponds functionally across realizations.

## A.3 Joint necessity of support capacity and policy

[Paper 31: Adaptive Voxel Substrate for PDE-RC](https://github.com/urosj/reflexive-coherence-sim/blob/main/experiments/papers/31-AdaptiveVoxelSubstrateForPDE-RC.md) tested whether adaptive local support capacity materially changes a carrier relation relative to matched fixed-resolution controls.

Its important decomposition is contextual. Adaptive support capacity alone was not sufficient. The declared topology/readback policy alone on fixed support was also not sufficient. The bounded carrier-relation candidate appeared through their combination and was not reproduced by matched high-resolution fixed-support policy controls in the tested support-ecology class.

This supports the compositional account of context. The function did not belong simply to a parameter, policy, or substrate object. It appeared through a relation between adaptive support capacity and a policy surface. The result also bounds correspondence: increasing fixed resolution under the same policy did not reproduce the adaptive relation, so literal resolution cannot stand in automatically for adaptive contextual capacity.

The result remains bounded to the tested fixtures and does not establish identity, abundance, native topology memory, or a universal superiority of adaptive support.

## A.4 Basin-signature transfer under declared mappings

[N27: LGRC Configuration / Substrate Transfer](https://github.com/urosj/graph-reflexive-coherence/blob/main/experiments/2026-06-N27-lgrc-configuration-substrate-transfer/README.md) asks whether a basin signature can persist after a declared configuration, fixture, topology, or substrate mapping.

The experiment does not treat a matching label as transfer. It requires source-current pre- and post-transfer signatures, boundary mapping, support and coherence preservation, flux balance, absence of hidden support reconstruction, replay, active nulls, and rejection of same-label/different-basin substitutions. It closes at bounded artifact-level transfer inside declared LGRC-visible mappings.

This is an early preservation contract of the kind proposed in Chapter 5. It shows that transfer can be treated as preservation of a relational signature under contextual change rather than as structural identity. It does not establish semantic identity, agency, native support, or unrestricted cross-substrate correspondence.

## A.5 Generative and extractive persistence

[N28: LGRC Generative vs Extractive Persistence](https://github.com/urosj/graph-reflexive-coherence/blob/main/experiments/2026-06-N28-lgrc-generative-vs-extractive-persistence/README.md) distinguishes focal survival from the effect of that survival on neighboring basin-forming capacity.

Generative persistence requires the focal basin to remain stable while neighboring or sub-basin structures become more distinguishable, better supported, or more capable of preserving their boundaries. Extractive persistence requires focal stability through flattening, draining, merging, or suppressing neighboring capacity.

This distinction supports the ecological side of contextual function. The same visible fact—persistence of the focal basin—can belong to different functional regimes depending on its relation to the neighborhood. Function is therefore not determined by the focal structure alone.

The evidence is bounded to LGRC-visible focal and neighborhood traces. It does not establish semantic cooperation, agency, native support, or general ecological health.

## A.6 Naturalization and producer dissolution

[N12: LGRC Native Naturalization and Producer Dissolution](https://github.com/urosj/graph-reflexive-coherence/blob/main/experiments/2026-06-N12-lgrc-native-naturalization-and-producer-dissolution/README.md) and [Naturalization of Becoming](../arc-of-becoming/2026-05-NaturalizationOfBecoming.md) provide the current conceptual and experimental boundary for context regeneration.

The naturalization track distinguishes producer-layer mechanisms, experiment-local scaffolds, native-absorption candidates, and theory-sensitive mechanisms. The theoretical paper makes the central correction explicit: naturalization is not copying the support shape but generating the function the support supplied.

These works support the need to track context-imposition debt and to separate supported function from native regime expression. They do not yet provide a general demonstration that an RC system can regenerate every contextual geometry required for a transferred function. Native contextual regeneration remains one of the strongest open hypotheses.

# Appendix B: Open Hypotheses and Discriminating Questions

The central thesis can be developed through six linked hypotheses. They are written separately here for experimental use, but they belong to one compositional argument: if function is contextual, a fixed realization should support more than one function through different geometries; if the relevant relation can be preserved, the function should transfer across structures; if the context is imposed, its cost and eventual naturalization must be measured.

## B.1 Contextual regime plurality

**Hypothesis.** One RC realization with one fixed constitutive specification can express different regimes in different internally formed contextual geometries, simultaneously or sequentially.

The strongest test holds the realization and constitutive parameters fixed, forms different contexts through history or spatial organization, and applies matched encounters. A positive result requires a difference in sustained functional role, not only in scalar response amplitude.

## B.2 Geometric multiple realizability

**Hypothesis.** Different faithful RC realizations can express functionally corresponding phenomenology when they preserve the registered geometry–flux relations necessary for it.

The test requires a declared candidate functional context class $\mathfrak C_q$, bounded cross-realization correspondences, matched interventions, preservation of RC invariants, and explicit correspondence failures. Similar labels or output traces are insufficient.

## B.3 Contextual primitive identity

**Hypothesis.** The same literal primitive can instantiate different functions in different contextual geometries.

The test keeps the primitive fixed while changing the context and measures whether its causal role changes: route versus separatrix, boundary versus trap, identity support versus extractive sink, learning update versus proxy reinforcement.

## B.4 Transformational portability

**Hypothesis.** A function can be restored in a target realization by adapting the primitive, adapting the context, or co-adapting both, even when literal copying fails.

The discriminating design compares four arms: literal copy, primitive-only adaptation, context-only adaptation, and co-adaptation. The result must include function, invariants, support cost, neighboring capacity, and withdrawal.

## B.5 Context-imposition debt

**Hypothesis.** Forcing a target context into the source primitive's admissible geometry can create measurable extraction, leakage, rigidity, maintenance cost, or loss of neighboring possibility.

The test must therefore observe the ecology around the transferred function, not only the focal output. A successful function with rising hidden support remains a debt-bearing transfer.

## B.6 Context naturalization

**Hypothesis.** A receiving RC regime can eventually generate, restore, or substitute the contextual geometry required for a function without the original external adapter.

The strongest test withdraws the scaffold, perturbs or partially dissolves the required context, preserves only native state and history, and asks whether a functionally corresponding geometry recurs over repeated recovery cycles.

These hypotheses do not require six unrelated experiment programs. They can be organized into three families: same-realization contextual differentiation; cross-context and cross-realization portability; and contextual naturalization after withdrawal. Their purpose is to turn the theoretical thesis into a sequence of bounded questions without reducing the paper's argument to the current evidence.
