# The Scale-Recursive Structure of Participation in Reflexive Coherence

## Context, Identity, and Participation Across Scale

**Date:** 2026-07-27<br>
**Status:** Theoretical investigation; fourth paper of the emerging Arc of Participation

## Abstract

[The Contextual Geometry of Function in Reflexive Coherence](./2026-07-ContextualGeometryOfFunction.md) argues that function does not belong to an isolated structure but to the geometry of relation through which that structure participates in an RC field. [The Ecological Formation of Identity in Reflexive Coherence](./2026-07-EcologicalFormationOfIdentity.md) follows the field as it differentiates into a locally continuous identity whose boundary, susceptibility, support relation, inherited history, and accessible continuation are formed ecologically rather than supplied in advance. [The Ecology of Participation in Reflexive Coherence](./2026-07-EcologyOfParticipationInRC.md) then asks how that identity continues its immanent unfolding through partial knowing, local action, shared-medium response, and later composition of meaning.

Taken separately, each paper works from a bounded contextual horizon. A structure functions in a context. A field forms an identity. An identity participates in an ecology. Yet the relations established by the three papers immediately disturb the boundary that made each account possible. An identity formed at one horizon is itself part of the context in which finer formations function. The same identity can become formation material for a wider closure. Its participation can change not only its own local geometry but the conditions through which another identity becomes possible at another scale. What appears as context, identity, or participation therefore depends partly on the horizon from which the field is read.

This paper investigates whether the complete relation

$$
\text{context}
\longrightarrow
\text{identity}
\longrightarrow
\text{participation}
\longrightarrow
\text{changed context}
$$

recurs across scale in Reflexive Coherence. The claim is not that every RC realization is literally self-similar, that every structure repeats at every size, or that one scale can be substituted for another without loss. The paper distinguishes four increasingly strong relations. **Scale recurrence** means that the same explanatory grammar becomes applicable at more than one contextual horizon. **Scale covariance** means that the grammar is preserved through a mapping even though its concrete bearers change. **Scale invariance** means that a declared property remains unchanged under a specified scale transformation. **Inter-scale coupling** means that participation at one horizon changes the geometry, function, or identity available at another. These relations may coexist, but none should be inferred merely because similar words—boundary, basin, identity, memory, participation—are used at several levels.

Ordinary RC is sufficient to motivate the investigation. RC already describes one coherence field whose local basins, boundaries, histories, and flux relations can be read through nested contextual horizons. A region can be a locally continuous identity relative to its surrounding field while simultaneously functioning as support, boundary, medium, or constituent within another closure. Participation by that identity changes the same field from which both local and wider continuations arise. No additional scale coordinate is required to see that identity at one horizon can become context at another.

Scale in this initial sense is not merely physical size, numerical resolution, or generation number. It is a **horizon of closure**: the region, interval, relational support, and temporal rhythm at which a function or identity becomes legible as a locally continuous organization. Two horizons may overlap spatially while differing in history, cadence, boundary, or susceptibility. A personal inquiry can contain conceptual identities at one horizon while the person remains an identity at another. An organization can be an ecology for its participants while participating as one formation in a wider institutional field. A basin can be an identity locally and one constitutive relation of a higher-order attractor. The scale question is therefore not only how large something is, but from which closure its continuity, function, and participation are being read.

The paper first shows that contextual function is scale-relative. The same formation may preserve identity locally while supporting, constraining, or extracting from a wider organization. A primitive can perform one role at a fine horizon and another at a coarser one because its ecological effect depends on the context in which the act is composed. This prevents local success, generativity, persistence, or loss from being projected automatically onto the whole.

The argument then returns to identity formation. Existing identities can become constitutive relations in the formation of a higher-order identity when their participation and shared medium acquire a persistent boundary, retained history, reflexive closure, attractivity, and continuation that cannot be reduced to any constituent alone. Coordination, common response, synchronization, or an observer-imposed label are not sufficient. Higher-order identity requires the same RC identity conditions to become legible at the higher horizon. The participating identities need not intend that formation or share a semantic model of it. Their local actions can compose a closure before the perspective from which the closure is meaningful exists.

Participation consequently becomes inter-scale. A local action can alter higher-order support, history, or boundary; a higher-order geometry can alter what the local identity can perceive, sustain, and enact. Neither direction is secondary. The part changes the context through which the whole continues, while the whole changes what the part can be. No scale therefore owns the complete causal story. The act belongs simultaneously to the local identity, to the shared medium through which it propagates, and to wider closures whose meaning may become visible only later.

Experience, knowledge, and meaning also become scale-relative without becoming arbitrary. A transition can be locally experienced as tension while contributing to higher-order stabilization, or locally experienced as continuity while participating in wider dissolution. Knowledge retained in one identity may become context for another. Higher-order meaning does not have to be semantic content shared identically by every constituent; it can be the significance available only from the new closure formed through their differentiated participation. The higher-scale reading does not invalidate the local one. Both are situated truths of the same field trajectory.

[Fractal Reflexive Coherence](../core/2025-11-FractalReflexiveCoherence.md) then provides an especially elegant formal mapping of this RC-level argument. FRC promotes coherence to a scale-resolved field $C(x,t;\sigma)$, adds a scale flux $J^\sigma$, integrates the coherence tensor across scales, and represents multiscale identities as attractor structures extending through bands of the scale coordinate. Each scale slice retains a reflexive loop while remaining coupled to neighboring slices, and only the coherence of the whole hierarchy is globally conserved. FRC therefore does not create the scale-recursive interpretation. It gives explicit mathematical form to the relation already visible in ordinary RC: local loops participate in a geometry composed by the hierarchy, while that collective geometry constrains every local loop.

The central thesis is:

> **The contextual-function–identity-formation–participation relation of Reflexive Coherence is scale-recursive. What functions as identity at one contextual horizon can participate as context, medium, or constituent at another. Participation changes geometry within and across horizons, while wider geometry changes the function, viability, and unfolding available locally. The recurrence is not automatically exact scale invariance: the structures may transform, the observables may differ, and some relations may exist only through inter-scale coupling. FRC makes this scale-recursive closure explicit by representing coherence, flux, geometry, and identity across a formal scale coordinate.**

The paper closes by placing this result inside the emerging Arc of Participation. Contextual geometry explains how relation gives function. Ecological formation explains how the field becomes a participant. The ecology of participation explains how that participant continues from partial knowing. Scale recursion shows that the same movement can become context and formation again. It prepares, but does not yet complete, the final ontological question of the arc: what it means to exist in RC when existence itself is locally continuous participation in a field that forms and is formed through participation across scale.

## Formal Status

This paper is an interpretive investigation grounded in the ordinary RC field–geometry–flux closure and clarified through the explicit multiscale formulation of Fractal Reflexive Coherence. It does not add a scale operator to ordinary RC, alter the FRC position–scale continuity equation, or claim a theorem that every RC realization exhibits exact scale invariance. Its purpose is to distinguish the scale relations already suggested by contextual function, ecological identity formation, and participation, then show how FRC gives those relations a direct mathematical representation.

Let

$$
\Xi_t
$$

denote the complete admissible state of an RC realization at time $t$. Let $\sigma$ initially label a **contextual horizon** at which a region, interval, boundary, history, and support relation are being read. In the ordinary-RC part of the paper, $\sigma$ is an index of description, not an additional coordinate of the core state. Only in the FRC chapter does $\sigma$ acquire the formal status of a generation or scale coordinate in $C(x,t;\sigma)$.

At contextual horizon $\sigma$, let

$$
\Gamma_\sigma
$$

denote the geometry–flux context relevant to the claim, let $I_\sigma$ denote an identity legible at that horizon, and let $a_\sigma$ denote a locally enacted participation. The recurring relation is written schematically as

$$
\mathcal R_\sigma:
\qquad
\Gamma_\sigma
\longrightarrow
I_\sigma
\xrightarrow{\ a_\sigma\ }
\Gamma_\sigma'.
$$

This notation is not a new RC equation. It compresses the three relations developed by the preceding investigations: context participates in function, the field differentiates into identity, and identity participation changes the context inherited by what follows.

A second horizon $\sigma'$ may be related to $\sigma$ by a mapping

$$
F_{\sigma\rightarrow\sigma'}.
$$

The paper distinguishes four possible claims about this relation.

A **recurrence claim** states that a context–identity–participation relation is legible at both horizons:

$$
\mathcal R_\sigma
\quad\text{and}\quad
\mathcal R_{\sigma'}.
$$

A **covariance claim** states that the relation transforms coherently under the mapping:

$$
F_{\sigma\rightarrow\sigma'}
\left(
\mathcal R_\sigma
\right)
\approx
\mathcal R_{\sigma'},
$$

where $\approx$ is bounded by a declared preservation contract rather than assumed as exact equality.

An **invariance claim** states that one declared property $q$ survives the scale transformation:

$$
F_{\sigma\rightarrow\sigma'}(q)=q.
$$

An **inter-scale coupling claim** states that activity at one horizon changes a context or continuation at another:

$$
\Delta\Gamma_{\sigma'}
=
\mathcal C_{\sigma\rightarrow\sigma'}
\left(
 a_\sigma,
 \Gamma_\sigma,
 \Gamma_{\sigma'}
\right).
$$

These claims have different evidential ceilings. Repeated vocabulary across scales establishes none of them by itself. A process can recur without being invariant, transform covariantly without being invertible, and couple across scale without forming a higher-order identity.

The paper also distinguishes **nested context** from **higher-order identity**. Let $\{I_i\}_{i=1}^{n}$ be identities legible at one horizon and let $\Gamma_{\mathrm{shared}}$ be the shared geometry through which their activity becomes mutually consequential. The schematic formation

$$
\left(
\{I_i\}_{i=1}^{n},
\Gamma_{\mathrm{shared}},
H
\right)
\xRightarrow[\sigma_H]{\mathrm{formation}}
I_H
$$

records the possibility that their relation becomes a new closure at horizon $\sigma_H$. It does not define identity through aggregation. A higher-order identity claim requires stability, attractivity, invariance, reflexive closure, coherence compatibility, and retained history to become legible at the higher horizon. Coordination, synchronization, shared labels, common outputs, and externally maintained organization are insufficient.

The following boundaries govern the investigation.

First, **scale is not initially a substance or coordinate**. In ordinary RC, it is a contextual horizon through which nested closures are read. FRC later gives scale explicit dynamical form.

Second, **recurrence is not repetition**. A boundary, memory, act, or identity may be realized through different structures at different horizons while preserving a relational grammar.

Third, **recurrence is not invariance**. Exact scale invariance must be demonstrated for a declared property and transformation. The paper expects covariance and coupling to be more common than literal invariance.

Fourth, **higher-order identity is not aggregation**. A collection becomes another identity only when a locally continuous reflexive closure forms at the higher horizon.

Fifth, **perspective remains structurally defined**. A perspective is a boundary, susceptibility, history, and accessible continuation through which some differences become dynamically relevant. The paper does not infer phenomenal consciousness for every identity or scale.

Sixth, **no scale is assumed to be final or absolute**. A horizon can be identity-relative for one claim and context-relative for another. The complete field trajectory remains the causal whole; scale-indexed identities and meanings are local organizations within it.

Seventh, **FRC is a formal mapping and extension, not retroactive proof**. Its explicit scale coordinate, scale flux, integrated geometry, and multiscale identity basins demonstrate how the scale-recursive structure can be represented. They do not establish that every ordinary-RC realization is fractal, infinitely nested, or invariant under scale transformation.

# 1. The Three Investigations Leave a Scale Question

## 1.1 A bounded horizon made each paper possible

The preceding investigations became intelligible by holding one contextual horizon sufficiently stable.

The contextual-geometry paper could ask how a formation acquires function because it held one field of relation sufficiently stable. In the schematic below, $X$ denotes the local formation, $\Gamma$ its contextual geometry, and $q$ the selected functional or phenomenological role. The expression $\operatorname{Role}(X\mid\Gamma)$ denotes the role that $X$ expresses in $\Gamma$:

$$
\operatorname{Role}(X\mid\Gamma)=q.
$$

The formation paper could ask how the field becomes locally differentiated into identity because it followed one candidate basin through spark, stabilization, admission, continuity, and lineage. Here $I$ denotes the identity that becomes locally legible within $\Gamma$; the arrow is schematic and does not represent an operator acting on an independently existing object:

$$
\Gamma
\longrightarrow
I.
$$

The participation paper could ask how an identity continues from partial knowing because the identity and its surrounding ecology were distinguishable enough to write the next relation. Here $a$ denotes the locally enacted participation, while primes mark the identity and context after that act:

$$
(I,\Gamma)
\xrightarrow{\ a\ }
(I',\Gamma').
$$

These boundaries were not mistakes. They made three different questions visible. Function could not be explained while the participant’s formation was also being derived in every sentence. Identity formation could not be investigated while every possible higher-order composition remained active. Participation could not be interpreted unless a local continuity had already become legible.

Yet the very answers produced by those papers unsettle their local framing.

If function belongs to context, then the same formation may have another function when read through a wider closure. If identity is formed ecologically, then every formed identity immediately becomes part of the context from which later formations arise. If participation changes the field, then the consequence of an act may be expressed at horizons that the participant cannot observe or even constitute by itself.

The papers therefore solve their local questions by producing a common unresolved relation:

> What happens when context, identity, and participation are read at more than one scale?

## 1.2 Identity at one horizon becomes context at another

An identity is a locally continuous basin relative to a geometry that sustains its boundary, return paths, support, and history. But nothing in that definition requires the basin to be the largest closure in the field.

A conceptual formation can be an identity inside one inquiry while contributing to the person’s wider understanding. A person can be an identity inside an organization while becoming part of the organization’s shared medium, history, and response. An organization can be an ecology for its participants while functioning as one basin, boundary, or source of pressure in a larger institutional field.

The same relation appears inside the RC geometry. Let

$$
U\subset V\subset\Omega_t.
$$

A basin $I_U$ may be locally continuous relative to the contextual geometry of $U$. From the horizon of $V$, however, the basin is also one formation among others. Its boundary filters flux through $V$; its support demand changes what remains available elsewhere; its persistence can stabilize or deform a wider attractor organization.

Thus:

$$
I_U
\subset
\Gamma_V.
$$

The identity does not stop being an identity locally because it becomes contextually significant elsewhere. Nor does its local identity make it the whole context at $V$. It occupies two relational positions at once.

This is the first scale-recursive insight:

> **What is identity relative to one contextual horizon can participate as context, medium, or constituent relative to another.**

The consequence is not only descriptive. The local identity changes the wider context, and the wider context changes what local identity can remain.

## 1.3 Participation does not end at the participant’s boundary

The participation paper describes an act as both the identity’s local contribution and one locally enacted movement of the wider field. Scale makes that equivalence more demanding.

An identity may experience an action as a local boundary repair. At another horizon, the same event may alter organizational reserve, redistribute support, or prevent a higher-order composition. A local learning event can change the response of a larger ecology. A locally stable withdrawal can dissolve a relation on which a higher-scale identity depended.

The participant does not need to know these wider effects for them to occur. Its act enters the shared field, and the field carries the consequence through whatever routes, histories, and closures currently exist.

The act therefore has no single exhaustive address:

$$
a_\sigma
\notin
\text{only }I_\sigma.
$$

It arises through $I_\sigma$, but its ecological consequence can become part of $\Gamma_{\sigma'}$:

$$
a_\sigma
\longrightarrow
\Delta\Gamma_{\sigma'}.
$$

The reverse relation also holds. A change in the higher-scale geometry can alter local boundary pressure, accessible routes, support, and susceptibility:

$$
\Delta\Gamma_{\sigma'}
\longrightarrow
\Delta\mathcal A(I_\sigma,\Gamma_\sigma),
$$

where $\mathcal A$ denotes the continuations available to the local identity.

Participation is therefore inter-scale whenever part and wider closure are dynamically coupled.

## 1.4 The question is not whether patterns look similar

The phrase *across scale* can invite a superficial search for self-similar shapes. One looks for the same branching pattern, the same network motif, or the same basin profile at several sizes and calls the result fractal.

The present problem is more structural.

The three investigations do not primarily describe shapes. They describe relations:

- a formation acquires function through context;
- the field differentiates into identity;
- identity participation changes the field that sustains it.

The scale question is whether these relations recur, transform, and couple when the contextual horizon changes.

A fine-scale boundary may be represented by a curvature ridge. A higher-scale boundary may be a persistent pattern of selective exchange among identities. The structures are not visually identical. Yet both may perform the relation of preserving a local continuity while filtering participation with a wider field.

The same applies to memory, support, and action. What is a local curvature aftereffect at one horizon may become retained organization in a shared medium at another. What is direct flux locally may become coordinated redistribution at a wider horizon. Scale recurrence therefore concerns a grammar of relation before it concerns geometric self-similarity.

## 1.5 The investigation

The paper begins from ordinary RC because the scale-recursive question already appears whenever nested contextual closures are distinguishable in one field. It first develops scale as a horizon of closure rather than as an added coordinate. This makes it possible to ask what changes when the same field is read locally, ecologically, and through higher-order identity.

The paper then revisits the three preceding investigations in turn. Function becomes scale-relative. Identity formation can recur through existing identities and shared media. Participation becomes the relation through which local and wider geometries continually alter one another. Only after those concrete relations are visible does the paper separate recurrence, covariance, invariance, and coupling. Without that distinction, every similar phrase across scale would be promoted into a universal symmetry, while every structural difference would be mistaken for failure of recurrence.

Perspective, experience, knowledge, and meaning can then be situated at scale without becoming arbitrary.

Only after this RC-level argument is complete does the paper turn to FRC. The scale coordinate $\sigma$, the scale flux $J^\sigma$, the integrated coherence tensor, and scale-resolved identity basins then appear not as an unrelated extension but as an explicit formal mapping of the same relation.

The final movement composes the fourth paper into the emerging Arc of Participation. Context, identity, and participation no longer form a linear sequence. Participation returns to context and can become identity formation again—locally, at another horizon, or across several scales at once.

# 2. Scale as a Horizon of Closure in Ordinary RC

## 2.1 One field permits several legitimate readings

Ordinary RC begins with one coherence field, one induced geometry, one flux, and one globally accounted continuation. The theory does not divide reality into pre-given scales before the dynamics begin.

Yet one field can contain several locally continuous organizations. A basin has a boundary and return structure. Several basins may participate in a larger attractor organization. A region that is locally stable may depend on support or pressure distributed beyond its immediate neighborhood. A history legible as one identity’s learning may also change the conditions of a wider regime.

The complete field remains one causal whole:

$$
\tau_{\mathrm{RC}}
=
\operatorname{Evolve}(\Xi_t).
$$

Scale enters when an observer, identity, or later analysis asks where a locally continuous closure becomes legible inside that whole.

Let $\sigma$ label such a horizon. Then

$$
\Gamma_\sigma
=
\Gamma(U_\sigma,I_\sigma^{t},H_\sigma,\partial U_\sigma,\mathcal E_\sigma)
$$

collects the region, time interval, retained history, effective boundary, and support relations relevant to the claim. The notation does not assert that scale is reducible to these terms. It marks that the same spatial region can support different scale readings when temporal rhythm, history, or closure differ.

A fast local process and a slow higher-order process may occupy the same location. A boundary can be porous over one interval and effectively closed over another. An organization can remain continuous through replacement of constituents that are individually short-lived. Scale is therefore partly a relation between spatial extent, temporal duration, support, and identity continuity.

## 2.2 Scale is not merely size

If scale meant only physical size, a larger region would always be a higher scale and a smaller region a lower one. RC does not support so simple an ordering.

A spatially distributed trace can function as one fine-grained mechanism while a small, persistent basin exerts higher-order constraint over a wider field. A long-duration identity can be spatially compact. A temporary organization can cover a large region without becoming a stable closure. A conceptual distinction can organize an entire inquiry despite having no useful physical size.

The relevant question is:

> At what horizon do boundary, history, susceptibility, return, and continuation compose into one function or identity?

Scale is therefore a **horizon of closure**.

A horizon is lower or finer relative to another when its identities and relations participate as constituents of the wider closure. It is higher or coarser when it preserves a continuity that can survive variation among those constituents. This ordering is relational, not absolute.

Write

$$
\sigma_1\prec\sigma_2
$$

when formations legible at $\sigma_1$ participate materially in the contextual geometry through which a closure is legible at $\sigma_2$. The relation does not require strict spatial containment. It requires constitutive dependence.

This allows a person’s conceptual fragments to be finer-scale identities relative to the person’s inquiry, while the person is a finer-scale identity relative to an organization. The same identity can be coarse relative to one ecology and fine relative to another.

## 2.3 Closure is the scale-making relation

A contextual horizon becomes theoretically relevant when something is preserved there that cannot be captured by merely listing the lower-scale states.

At the level of function, the closure may preserve a role: boundary, routing, memory, or support.

At the level of identity, it may preserve a basin, history, and return relation despite changes among constituents.

At the level of participation, it may preserve a recurrent way in which local acts alter the shared medium and the medium alters later local acts.

Scale is therefore not added from outside. It appears when a relation becomes locally continuous enough to carry its own history and continuation.

This does not mean every closure is an identity. A function can be distributed across context without forming a self-maintaining basin. A regime can persist statistically without acquiring the reflexive continuity required for identity. But every legitimate scale claim needs some preserved organization through which the horizon remains more than an arbitrary grouping.

The scale-making relation can be written provisionally as

$$
\operatorname{Close}_{\sigma}
\left(
\Xi_t
\right)
=
\mathcal O_\sigma,
$$

where $\mathcal O_\sigma$ is the organization legible at that horizon. This is an analytical reading, not a new operator in RC.

The important point is that different $\mathcal O_\sigma$ can overlap while preserving different continuities.

## 2.4 The same field has no single exhaustive partition

Nested scale language can suggest a clean hierarchy: parts belong to wholes, wholes belong to larger wholes, and every formation occupies one level.

RC geometry can be less orderly.

One identity may participate in several higher-order contexts. Two scale horizons may overlap without one containing the other. A shared medium may connect identities whose local boundaries are defined differently. An event can be fine-scale relative to one identity and higher-order relative to another. A lineage can change scale through composition or dissolution.

The field should therefore not be forced into one universal tree of levels.

A more faithful object is a family of contextual horizons

$$
\mathfrak H(\Xi_t)
=
\{\sigma_1,\sigma_2,\ldots\}
$$

with partial relations of containment, dependence, coupling, and overlap.

This matters for the claim of scale recursion. The same grammar can recur without every recurrence occupying one ordered ladder. The ecology may contain several interacting scale organizations at once.

## 2.5 History determines which scale is visible

A higher-order closure often becomes legible only through duration.

At one instant, several identities may look merely coordinated. Over time, their shared medium may retain a history that influences later collective response. The higher-order organization can survive turnover, repair a boundary, or return after perturbation. Only then does a scale of continuity become visible that was absent from the snapshot.

Likewise, a lower-scale identity may disappear from a coarse observation while remaining causally active through its retained aftereffects. A history compressed into higher geometry can preserve the consequences of a scale that is no longer directly legible.

Scale is therefore path-dependent. The horizon from which an event becomes meaningful may not exist when the event occurs.

This repeats the distinction between enacted and understood paths. The field enacts relations across time; a later closure makes their scale composition visible.

## 2.6 No scale has the whole view

A higher-order horizon can integrate relations unavailable locally, but it does not become an external observer of the complete RC trajectory.

Its perspective is also formed through a boundary, susceptibility, history, and accessible continuation. It may register a disturbance that no constituent can register alone while remaining blind to local differences that matter deeply to one constituent.

Higher scale does not mean greater truth in every respect. It means another locally continuous position inside the same field.

Thus:

$$
\text{wider integration}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{absolute perspective}.
$$

This prevents the scale account from recreating the omniscient whole that the participation paper rejected. Every identity, including a higher-order one, knows and experiences through the closure that makes it an identity.

The next problem is therefore not to identify the highest scale. It is to see how the first relation of the arc—contextual function—changes when the same field is read through more than one horizon.

# 3. Contextual Function Across Scale

## 3.1 Function changes when the horizon changes

The contextual-geometry paper corrects the statement

$$
X\longrightarrow Q
$$

by making function dependent on context:

$$
\operatorname{Role}(X\mid\Gamma)=q.
$$

Scale recursion adds another dependency. The context through which the formation is read changes with the horizon, so the same formation can participate in several functions without contradiction:

$$
\operatorname{Role}_{\sigma}(X\mid\Gamma_{\sigma})
\neq
\operatorname{Role}_{\sigma'}(X\mid\Gamma_{\sigma'}).
$$

A boundary around one basin may function locally as protection. From a wider horizon, the same boundary may regulate exchange among several identities. From another horizon, its persistence may become a source of rigidity that prevents a higher-order composition. None of these functions is contained in the boundary alone. Each belongs to a different relation between the formation and the contextual geometry through which it is read.

The scale-indexed claim is therefore not that one object has many arbitrary interpretations. The field can constrain each reading through different observables, interventions, and continuation effects. Removing the boundary may dissolve the local identity, increase higher-order exchange, and destabilize the wider ecology at the same time. The different functions are real because the formation participates in several causal organizations.

## 3.2 Local success and wider function can diverge

The distinction becomes practical when one scale evaluates an event as success while another reveals a different effect.

Suppose a basin deepens and becomes more resistant to perturbation. At the local horizon, this can count as identity reinforcement:

$$
J_C\rightarrow I_{\sigma}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\text{basin depth}_{\sigma}\uparrow.
$$

But the coherence, boundary pressure, or support required for that reinforcement may alter neighboring capacity. At a wider horizon, the same event can contribute to:

- generative differentiation, if the stronger basin creates reusable support or a stable interface;
- neutral maintenance, if the wider ecology remains materially unchanged;
- extraction, if neighboring formations are flattened or deprived of continuation;
- or higher-order stabilization, if the local deepening is one necessary component of a larger organization.

The local sign does not determine the wider role.

This is not an argument for privileging the wider horizon absolutely. A higher-order identity can also persist by dissolving local distinctions that the constituent identities experience as loss. The point is that function must declare its scale and cannot be promoted across scales without a mapping.

## 3.3 A formation can be primitive, medium, and identity at once

The same formation can occupy different ontological roles at different horizons.

At one scale, $X$ may be a primitive: a minimal transformation with a declared interface and contextual type.

At another, it may be part of the shared medium through which several identities influence one another.

At another, it may be admitted as an identity because the formation itself preserves a boundary, history, and reflexive continuation.

Symbolically:

$$
X
\equiv
\mathfrak p_{\sigma}
\equiv
\text{medium component}_{\sigma'}
\equiv
I_{\sigma''},
$$

where the equivalence signs do not claim semantic identity. They show that one field formation can enter different functional descriptions under different horizons.

This prevents the Atlas from assigning one permanent type to a structure. A graph node can be an identity in one experiment, a routing primitive in another, and one element of a higher-order shared medium in a third. A conceptual distinction can be a fragment inside one inquiry and the organizing boundary of another. A person can be an identity in a team ecology and a medium through which histories, norms, and resources become available to others.

The formation has not become metaphysically ambiguous. Its function is scale-contextual.

## 3.4 Upward contribution and downward constitution

Scale-recursive function is often described through two directions.

**Upward contribution** occurs when lower-scale activity changes a higher-scale context:

$$
a_{\sigma}
\longrightarrow
\Delta\Gamma_{\sigma'}.
$$

A local trace becomes part of organizational memory. Repeated basin interactions form a larger boundary. Many small flux changes alter the effective geometry through which a parent identity continues.

**Downward constitution** occurs when the higher-scale context changes the function available locally:

$$
\Delta\Gamma_{\sigma'}
\longrightarrow
\Delta\operatorname{Role}_{\sigma}(X).
$$

A higher-order boundary changes local permeability. A shared reserve makes one exploration viable. An organizational regime turns one local act into compliance, resistance, innovation, or noise by changing the geometry in which the act participates.

These directions are analytically useful but do not describe independent channels. The same transition can be both upward and downward because the field update is one event read from two horizons.

The local formation changes the higher geometry, and the changed higher geometry returns immediately or gradually as a different local context.

## 3.5 Function can be distributed across scales

Some functions do not belong completely to any one scale.

A local formation may supply sensitivity, another scale may supply memory, and a higher-order boundary may supply the stability through which the combination counts as one function. Removing any one relation can destroy the function while leaving the others intact.

Consider learning. A local identity may change susceptibility after an encounter. The trace may be retained in a medium outside that identity. A higher-scale regime may determine whether the trace is revisited, reinforced, or forgotten. The resulting learning function is distributed across:

$$
\text{local response change}
+
\text{shared retention}
+
\text{higher-order recurrence}.
$$

Likewise, agency may depend on local persistence capacity, higher-scale support, and a shared medium through which action has consequences. A boundary may be produced locally but stabilized by a slower parent geometry. A higher-order identity can act only through lower-scale constituents whose coordination remains partly independent.

Scale-recursive analysis therefore asks not only *where* a function is located, but *which cross-scale relations jointly constitute it*.

## 3.6 Primitives require scale typing

The contextual paper proposes that a primitive needs an admissible context class. Scale recursion adds that the context class must state the horizon at which the function is claimed.

A scale-contextual primitive can be written provisionally as

$$
\mathfrak p
=
\left(
X,
\partial X,
\mathcal C_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{in}},
\mathcal C_{\sigma}^{\mathrm{out}},
\mathcal T_{\sigma},
\mathcal I_{\sigma},
\mathcal K_{\sigma\leftrightarrow\sigma'}
\right),
$$

where $\mathcal K_{\sigma\leftrightarrow\sigma'}$ records declared cross-scale effects or dependencies.

A trace primitive, for example, should not claim memory without stating:

- the scale at which the trace is retained;
- the identity or regime whose later response changes;
- whether the trace survives lower-scale turnover;
- whether it depends on higher-scale scheduling;
- and what other scale pays its maintenance cost.

A split primitive should state whether it creates two local basins, changes a parent boundary, or establishes a lineage across scales. A reinforcement primitive should distinguish local basin deepening from higher-scale generativity. A routing primitive should state whether its effect is local transport or reorganization of a wider flow regime.

Without scale typing, portability can preserve the visible mechanism while changing the level at which its function exists.

## 3.7 There is no final function outside all horizons

The scale-recursive account could be misunderstood as a search for the highest scale from which the true function of every formation becomes visible.

No such final horizon follows from RC.

A wider identity may integrate more relations, but it remains one formation inside the complete field. Another scale may interpret its persistence differently. The total field trajectory is causally complete, yet causal completeness is not a semantic perspective that assigns one final role to every local structure.

Function remains contextual even across scale.

The strongest possible claim is not:

> This is what the formation ultimately is for.

It is:

> Under this horizon, with these relations preserved, the formation participates in this function; under another horizon, it participates in another function; and the mapping between those functions has this evidence and claim ceiling.

This prepares the next question. If an identity at one scale can function as formation material at another, when does the resulting organization become another identity rather than only a coordinated relation?

# 4. Identity Formation Across Scale

## 4.1 Existing identities become part of formation context

The ecological-formation paper begins from a field that becomes unstable and differentiates into a new attractor basin. At first this can sound like the formation of an elementary local identity from a more continuous background.

But the field from which an identity forms may already contain admitted identities.

Their presence changes the formation context through:

- boundaries that redirect coherence;
- histories that alter susceptibility;
- support demands that redistribute reserve;
- repeated interactions that form routes and interfaces;
- and local participation that changes the geometry inherited by the wider field.

Existing identities therefore become formation material without ceasing to be identities at their own horizon.

Let

$$
\mathcal I_{\sigma}
=
\{I_{\sigma,1},\ldots,I_{\sigma,n}\}
$$

be identities legible at one horizon, and let $\Gamma_{\mathrm{shared}}$ be the contextual geometry through which their activity becomes mutually consequential. A higher-scale formation candidate can be written schematically as

$$
\left(
\mathcal I_{\sigma},
\Gamma_{\mathrm{shared}},
H
\right)
\longrightarrow
I_{\sigma'}.
$$

The notation does not mean that the higher identity is assembled by adding the constituent identities. It states that their lineages and shared medium participate in the field event through which another closure may become legible.

## 4.2 Participation can prepare a higher-order spark

The constituent identities need not intend to form a larger identity.

Each acts from local affordances. One preserves a boundary. Another creates a route. Another retains history. Another supplies support. Their local meanings may remain unrelated, and their actions may occur at different times.

Yet participation changes the shared context. Some relations recur. Certain exchanges become easier. A collective boundary becomes more selective. History becomes retained in a medium rather than in any one constituent. Perturbations begin to produce a coordinated return pattern that cannot be explained by one local basin alone.

These changes can prepare a higher-order instability and spark.

The previous organization—several identities related only through transient interaction—may become unable to account for the field’s continuation. Another attractor organization becomes possible at the higher horizon.

The higher-order spark is therefore similar to local formation in one decisive way: it is locally or relationally expressed but constituted by a wider field history. The visible initiating event may belong to one identity, while the formation possibility depends on the accumulated geometry of all participants and the medium they have changed.

## 4.3 Coordination is not identity

The fact that identities interact, synchronize, or produce a common output does not establish a higher-order identity.

Several identities may be driven by one external schedule. They may follow a protocol, share a resource, or respond similarly to one perturbation. Their behavior can be stable and useful without forming another reflexive closure.

Coordination asks:

> Do multiple identities produce an organized relation?

Higher-order identity asks:

> Has that relation become a locally continuous basin with its own boundary, attractivity, invariant organization, reflexive closure, compatibility, and retained history?

The higher-scale claim therefore inherits the core identity criteria:

$$
I_{\sigma'}
\Rightarrow
\left
\{
\begin{aligned}
&\text{stability},\\
&\text{attractivity},\\
&\text{invariance},\\
&\text{reflexive closure},\\
&\text{coherence compatibility},\\
&\text{history}
\end{aligned}
\right\}_{\sigma'}.
$$

At this horizon, stability can survive lower-scale variation. Attractivity means that perturbations to constituent arrangements return toward a recognizable higher organization. Reflexive closure means that constituent activity changes the shared geometry and that the changed geometry constrains later constituent activity. History must be retained at the higher scale rather than merely repeated by one persistent local identity.

A label, aggregate metric, common objective, or external observer’s grouping is insufficient.

## 4.4 The higher-order boundary is relational

A higher-order identity does not usually possess a boundary that is simply the union of constituent boundaries.

Its boundary may consist of:

- selective exchange among the shared organization and its surroundings;
- a recurring support relation;
- a medium whose traces remain inside the organization;
- a temporal cadence that distinguishes internal continuation from external perturbation;
- or a topology of interaction that persists through constituent turnover.

The higher boundary is therefore a relation maintained by the participating identities and the medium among them.

This boundary can exist even when constituents remain spatially distributed. Conversely, constituents can be spatially close without forming one higher identity if no shared closure preserves a distinction across time.

The formation context of a higher identity must therefore include the constituent lineages, the shared medium, the wider ecology, and the inter-scale relations that make the new boundary viable:

$$
\Gamma^{\mathrm{form}}_{I_{\sigma'}}
=
\operatorname{FormationContext}_{\sigma'}
\left(
\Xi_{[t_s^-,t_a]},
\{\mathcal L_{I_{\sigma,i}}\},
\Gamma_{\mathrm{shared}}
\right).
$$

As in the local formation paper, the notation is a scaffold. Its purpose is to prevent the higher identity from being explained by listing its members.

## 4.5 The higher identity forms a new situated perspective

If the higher-order closure satisfies the identity conditions, it forms another situated organization of significance.

It may register perturbations that no constituent registers independently. It may preserve a history distributed through the shared medium. It may maintain a boundary across constituent turnover. It may reorganize several local identities in response to one disruption. Its characteristic continuation may unfold on a different temporal rhythm from those of its members.

This is a structural perspective: a boundary, susceptibility, retained history, and accessible continuation at the higher horizon. The paper does not need to claim phenomenal consciousness for every higher-order basin.

The important consequence is that the higher identity can make relations intelligible that were unavailable from the constituent horizons.

Let $m_i(a)$ denote the local meaning available to constituent identity $I_i$ when an act occurs, and let $M_{\sigma'}(a)$ denote a later meaning available from the higher-order closure. Then:

$$
m_i(a)
\neq
M_{\sigma'}(a)
$$

can hold without contradiction.

The higher meaning is not necessarily the content shared by all participants:

$$
M_{\sigma'}
\neq
\bigcap_i m_i.
$$

It can be the significance made possible by the new organization of their differences.

Shared meaning therefore appears as one capacity of higher-order identity formation, not as the consensus required before formation can occur.

## 4.6 Constituents can persist, transform, or dissolve

Higher-order identity formation does not imply that lower-scale identities disappear.

Several relations are possible:

- the higher identity forms while constituent identities remain locally continuous;
- constituent boundaries change while their lineages remain legible;
- one identity becomes a stable sub-basin of the larger closure;
- some constituents are absorbed or lose independent viability;
- constituent turnover occurs while higher-order identity persists;
- or the higher closure remains temporary and later dissolves back into local organization.

These are different geometry–lineage relations, not one hierarchy of success.

The scale claim must therefore state what persists at each horizon.

A person can remain an identity while participating in an organization that acquires its own continuity. A concept can retain local meaning while becoming one part of a larger theory. A cell can persist within an organism while its support, function, and accessible continuation are transformed by the organism-level geometry.

The higher-order identity is neither reducible to the constituents nor independent of their participation.

## 4.7 Identity admission must recur at each scale

A higher-order identity should not inherit admission merely because its constituents are admitted identities.

The observer needs evidence at the claimed horizon:

- a boundary that persists through bounded constituent variation;
- return or recovery at the higher scale;
- history retained beyond one constituent;
- reflexive downward effects on constituent continuation;
- continuity through some turnover or internal reorganization;
- and support accounting that distinguishes native closure from external scheduling.

A scale-specific formation witness can be written as

$$
W_{\mathrm{form}}^{(\sigma')}(I_{\sigma'})
=
\left(
\Xi_{t_s^-},
\mathfrak s_{\sigma'},
\Xi_{t_s^+},
\Gamma^{\mathrm{form}}_{I_{\sigma'}},
\chi_{I_{\sigma'}}(t_a),
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{const}},
\mathcal O_{\sigma'},
\ell_{\sigma'}
\right).
$$

The witness records what became newly continuous at the higher horizon while preserving the lineages that participated in its formation.

This provides an Atlas with nested identity records rather than forcing one scale to become the final unit.

## 4.8 No constituent needs to foresee the identity it forms

The constituent identities participate from partial knowing. Before the higher-order closure exists, the perspective from which their relations become one semantic whole does not yet exist either.

The complete field is causally sufficient for the formation event, but it need not semantically represent the higher identity in advance. The constituents likewise need not know:

- that a higher identity is forming;
- what its boundary will be;
- which local contribution will become central;
- or how their own identities will be transformed by it.

Their participation history is enacted first:

$$
H_{\mathrm{parts}}
=
\{a_{\sigma,i}\}_{0:T}.
$$

A later higher identity may permit another reading:

$$
\mathsf{Path}_{\sigma'}
=
\operatorname{ReadPath}
\left(
H_{\mathrm{parts}}
\mid
I_{\sigma'}
\right).
$$

The later reading remains constrained by what occurred, but it must not be projected backward as though the constituents already shared the higher-scale view.

The original fragment problem therefore recurs as an identity-formation problem across scale.

# 5. Participation Across Scale

## 5.1 A local act changes more than its local context

The participation paper describes an act as a transition in both identity and context:

$$
(I_{\sigma,t},\Gamma_{\sigma,t})
\xrightarrow{\ a_{\sigma,t}\ }
(I_{\sigma,t+\Delta t},\Gamma_{\sigma,t+\Delta t}).
$$

Once several horizons are admitted, the same act can also change contexts in which the local identity is only one constituent:

$$
a_{\sigma,t}
\longrightarrow
\Delta\Gamma_{\sigma',t+\Delta t}.
$$

The wider effect may be immediate or delayed. A local transfer can alter a parent reserve immediately. A trace can become relevant only after repeated interactions. A boundary adjustment can change higher-scale topology once enough neighboring relations depend on it.

Participation therefore has a scale signature: the set of horizons at which the act produces a measurable change in contextual geometry or continuation.

A local act should not be called insignificant merely because its higher-scale effect is not immediately readable. Nor should every local effect be promoted into higher-scale participation without evidence. The mapping requires history and intervention.

## 5.2 Higher-scale geometry shapes local affordance

The return relation is equally important.

An identity acts only through continuations available in its current geometry:

$$
a_{\sigma}
\in
\mathcal A(I_{\sigma},\Gamma_{\sigma}).
$$

But $\Gamma_{\sigma}$ can depend materially on a higher-scale organization. Parent boundaries, shared resources, organizational roles, long-lived routes, and higher-order histories alter what the local identity can perceive and enact.

Thus:

$$
\Delta\Gamma_{\sigma'}
\longrightarrow
\Delta\mathcal A(I_{\sigma},\Gamma_{\sigma}).
$$

A new higher-scale identity may create local affordances that no constituent could sustain before. It may also close former routes, transform boundaries, or make one local identity nonviable.

This is not external control imposed on an otherwise complete local agent. The local identity is partly constituted through the higher context. A change in that context changes the identity’s actual space of continuation.

## 5.3 Participation is one field event read across horizons

Upward contribution and downward constitution can sound like two sequential causal arrows: parts send effects upward; wholes send constraints downward.

In RC, they are often two readings of the same update.

A local act changes coherence and geometry. The changed geometry contributes to a higher-scale organization. That higher organization is part of the context through which the local identity’s next state is determined. The field need not first complete an upward pass and then execute a downward pass.

The relation is reflexive:

$$
I_{\sigma}
\xrightarrow{\ a_{\sigma}\ }
\Gamma_{\sigma,\sigma'}'
\longrightarrow
I_{\sigma}'.
$$

The middle term indicates a geometry jointly relevant to several horizons.

This matters for attribution. The local identity can experience the action as its own while the wider ecology experiences the same transition as one movement of a larger continuation. Neither perspective exhausts the event.

## 5.4 Participation can be invisible at the scale where its meaning later forms

A constituent identity may not see the higher-order consequence of its action. The higher-order identity may not yet exist, or the effect may remain subthreshold until later activity composes with it.

The act can nevertheless participate in the formation context by:

- changing a route;
- retaining a trace;
- altering support distribution;
- differentiating a boundary;
- or making another identity’s action possible.

Only later does a higher-scale closure make the relation legible.

This is not participation without effect. It is participation whose function and meaning appear at another horizon or later interval.

Conversely, a higher-order act can be invisible locally. A slow change in organizational geometry may alter many small affordances without appearing as one discrete intervention to any constituent.

Scale recursion therefore complicates the demand that every participant understand its total contribution before acting. The relevant effect may not yet have a scale from which it can be interpreted.

## 5.5 Inter-scale participation can be asymmetric

Mutual constitution does not imply equal influence.

A higher-order identity can constrain many local identities strongly while each local identity has only a small individual effect on the higher geometry. A rare local event can nevertheless trigger a major higher-scale transition if the wider context is near instability. A slowly changing parent basin may shape many fast local trajectories while barely responding to one local perturbation.

The coupling can therefore differ in:

- strength;
- latency;
- spatial or topological reach;
- reversibility;
- and support cost.

Let

$$
\kappa_{\sigma\rightarrow\sigma'}
$$

denote a provisional coupling strength under a declared intervention and observable family. There is no requirement that

$$
\kappa_{\sigma\rightarrow\sigma'}
=
\kappa_{\sigma'\rightarrow\sigma}.
$$

This asymmetry matters for agency and cultivation. A local identity can be deeply shaped by a higher regime while having little capacity to revise it. Another identity can act as a leverage point whose local changes reorganize the wider field.

Scale-aware participation must therefore ask not only whether coupling exists, but how influence, support, and answerability are distributed across horizons.

## 5.6 Conservation makes scale relations ecological

Local and higher-scale continuity share one coherence accounting.

A local identity can gain coherence while another scale loses it. A higher-order identity can remain stable through continuous lower-scale turnover. A local dissolution can release coherence that becomes available elsewhere. These relations do not make every loss beneficial or every persistence extractive; they make local accounting incomplete without the wider field.

The relevant claim should declare:

- the horizon at which coherence gain or loss is measured;
- the scale across which support is exchanged;
- the identities whose continuity changes;
- and the total invariant under which the redistribution occurs.

This becomes especially explicit in FRC, where individual scale slices may gain or lose coherence while only the integral over scale remains conserved. But the interpretive requirement already exists in ordinary RC whenever nested identities share one field.

A locally successful participation can therefore depend on hidden scale debt. A higher-scale generative regime can also require bounded local dissolution or transformation. The field does not supply a universal moral reading; it supplies the accounting through which identity-relative experiences and cultivation orientations can be located.

## 5.7 The scale-recursive participation loop

The relations developed so far can be composed:

$$
\boxed{
\Gamma_{\sigma}
\longrightarrow
I_{\sigma}
\xrightarrow{\ a_{\sigma}\ }
\Gamma_{\sigma,\sigma'}'
\longrightarrow
I_{\sigma'}
\xrightarrow{\ a_{\sigma'}\ }
\Gamma_{\sigma,\sigma'}''.
}
$$

The sequence is not necessarily chronological in this exact order. It is a dependency map.

Context at one horizon participates in forming identity. Identity acts and changes a geometry relevant to several horizons. That changed geometry can transform the original identity, form another identity, or sustain a higher-order identity. Higher-order participation then changes the conditions inherited locally.

The loop can recur without exact repetition.

The next question is therefore precise: what does it mean to say that the same structure recurs across scale? Which relations are merely analogous, which transform coherently, which remain invariant, and which depend on active coupling?

# 6. Recurrence, Covariance, Invariance, and Coupling

## 6.1 Why scale invariance is tempting

Once the same vocabulary appears at several horizons—context, boundary, identity, participation, history, meaning—it is tempting to declare the relation scale-invariant.

The attraction is understandable. The same field seems to form local basins, larger organizations of basins, and higher identities whose participation again changes their context. One can write the same schematic loop at each scale:

$$
\Gamma_{\sigma}
\rightarrow
I_{\sigma}
\xrightarrow{\ a_{\sigma}\ }
\Gamma_{\sigma}'.
$$

If the loop looks the same, perhaps the theory has discovered one universal pattern repeated without change.

But the same notation can hide substantial differences.

A local boundary may be a coherence ridge, while a higher-order boundary is a persistent exchange relation. Local memory may be curvature aftereffect, while higher-scale memory is distributed across a shared medium. Local action may be one flux event, while higher-order action is a recurrent organization of many constituent transitions. Identity continuity can have different time horizons, perturbation classes, and support dependencies.

The recurrence of explanatory grammar does not prove invariance of structure.

The paper must therefore separate several strengths of claim before speaking of scale invariance.

## 6.2 Scale recurrence

**Scale recurrence** is the weakest and currently most defensible claim.

It holds when the relation among contextual function, identity formation, and participation can be meaningfully identified at more than one horizon:

$$
\mathcal R_{\sigma}
=
\left(
\Gamma_{\sigma},
I_{\sigma},
a_{\sigma},
\Gamma_{\sigma}'
\right),
$$

$$
\mathcal R_{\sigma'}
=
\left(
\Gamma_{\sigma'},
I_{\sigma'},
a_{\sigma'},
\Gamma_{\sigma'}'
\right).
$$

The two relations need not share literal components or identical observables. They recur because each contains:

- a context that contributes to function and formation;
- a locally continuous identity;
- participation that changes the context;
- and history through which later continuation differs.

Scale recurrence is already theoretically significant. It means the three-paper sequence is not confined to one kind of participant or one physical level. It can organize personal inquiry, organizations, graph ecologies, biological hierarchies, and formal FRC slices without claiming that those systems are identical.

Recurrence supplies the grammar of the emerging Arc of Participation.

## 6.3 Scale covariance

**Scale covariance** is stronger. It requires a mapping that preserves the form of the relation while allowing the concrete bearers to transform.

Let

$$
F_{\sigma\rightarrow\sigma'}
=
\left(
F_{\Gamma},
F_I,
F_a,
F_H
\right)
$$

map contexts, identities, acts, and histories between horizons. Covariance holds provisionally when

$$
F_{\Gamma}
\left(
\operatorname{Update}_{\sigma}
(\Gamma_{\sigma},I_{\sigma},a_{\sigma})
\right)
\approx
\operatorname{Update}_{\sigma'}
\left(
F_{\Gamma}(\Gamma_{\sigma}),
F_I(I_{\sigma}),
F_a(a_{\sigma})
\right),
$$

under a declared tolerance and scope.

The relation says that mapping before or after the participation update yields corresponding higher-scale organizations. It does not require the acts or boundaries to look the same.

For example, repeated local traces may map to one higher-scale memory relation. Local identity reinforcement may map to persistence of a larger organization under constituent turnover. A local boundary operation may map to a change in organizational permeability.

Covariance becomes practically important because it allows knowledge to transfer across scale without requiring literal copying.

## 6.4 Genuine scale invariance

**Scale invariance** should be reserved for a relation or quantity that remains unchanged under a declared scale transformation.

Suppose $S_{\ell}$ rescales the relevant horizon by factor $\ell$. A quantity $Q$ is scale-invariant when

$$
Q
\left(
S_{\ell}(\Gamma,I,a)
\right)
=
Q(\Gamma,I,a)
$$

for every admitted $\ell$ in the declared class.

The invariant might concern:

- a conservation relation;
- a dimensionless ratio;
- a normalized functional signature;
- an identity criterion;
- or a structural law of the context–identity–participation loop.

Most phenomenological labels will not be invariant without qualification. The meaning of a fragment, the cost of support, or the experience of a boundary can change with scale. Even identity criteria may require transformed observables and perturbation classes rather than exact equality.

Scale invariance is therefore an earned result, not the default interpretation of recurrence.

## 6.5 Inter-scale coupling is different again

Two scales can be strongly coupled without being recurrent or invariant.

A higher-order support system may materially shape local identities even if it has no analogous local structure. A local perturbation may trigger a wider transition through a scale-specific leverage relation. One scale may carry memory while another carries fast action. The relation is causal, but not self-similar.

Inter-scale coupling is established when a controlled change at one horizon alters a registered property at another:

$$
\operatorname{do}
\left(
\Delta X_{\sigma}
\right)
\Longrightarrow
\Delta Y_{\sigma'}
$$

under matched controls and declared history.

The reverse intervention should be tested separately:

$$
\operatorname{do}
\left(
\Delta Y_{\sigma'}
\right)
\Longrightarrow
\Delta X_{\sigma}.
$$

Coupling can be bidirectional, asymmetric, delayed, thresholded, or mediated through another scale.

The distinction protects the theory from interpreting every causal dependency as evidence of fractal invariance.

## 6.6 A scale-preservation contract

Cross-scale claims need a preservation contract similar to cross-realization mappings.

For a proposed mapping

$$
F_{\sigma\rightarrow\sigma'}:
\mathcal R_{\sigma}
\rightarrow
\mathcal R_{\sigma'},
$$

write provisionally

$$
\mathfrak M_{\sigma\rightarrow\sigma'}
=
\left(
\mathrm{Src},
\mathrm{Tgt},
F,
\mathcal P^+,
\mathcal P^-,
\varepsilon,
\Sigma_{\mathrm{valid}},
\mathcal E,
\ell
\right),
$$

where:

- $\mathcal P^+$ names the relations claimed to be preserved;
- $\mathcal P^-$ names the relations explicitly not preserved;
- $\varepsilon$ gives the tolerance;
- $\Sigma_{\mathrm{valid}}$ gives the scale and condition range;
- $\mathcal E$ gives the evidence;
- and $\ell$ gives the claim ceiling.

The contract should answer:

- Is the mapping recurrence, covariance, invariance, or coupling?
- Does it preserve function, identity, participation, history, or only one observable?
- Is it one-way or reversible?
- Does it preserve constituent identities?
- What support or aggregation is introduced?
- Which local distinctions are lost?

Without this contract, phrases such as “the same pattern appears at every scale” remain interpretive compression rather than testable claims.

## 6.7 Scale non-equivalence is informative

A failed scale mapping does not necessarily invalidate the RC interpretation.

The lower scale may lack the history required at the higher scale. The higher identity may depend on constituent diversity that disappears under aggregation. A local boundary may have no higher-scale counterpart. A higher-order act may be distributed across many local transitions and therefore resist one-to-one mapping.

These failures reveal where the recurrence changes form.

A result may show:

- function without identity recurrence;
- identity recurrence without semantic correspondence;
- coupling without covariance;
- covariance over one perturbation class but not another;
- or an invariant that holds only after normalization.

The negative map is part of the scale structure. It prevents one horizon from becoming an unearned model of all others.

## 6.8 The claim grammar of scale

The paper can now state a disciplined grammar:

- **At horizon $\sigma$**, formation $X$ performs function $q$ in context $\Gamma_{\sigma}$.
- **At horizon $\sigma'$**, a corresponding or different function appears under another context.
- **Across $\sigma\rightarrow\sigma'$**, the declared relation recurs, transforms covariantly, remains invariant, or is causally coupled.
- **Under these interventions**, the mapping survives or fails.
- **At this claim ceiling**, the result concerns a structure, identity, regime, perspective, or interpretation.

This grammar prepares the move to FRC. Ordinary RC supplies the contextual horizons. FRC asks what happens when scale becomes an explicit coordinate of the coherence field rather than an analytical index alone.

# 7. Fractal Reflexive Coherence as an Explicit Scale Mapping

## 7.1 RC is sufficient for the relation; FRC makes it explicit

The scale-recursive argument has so far required only ordinary RC.

One field can contain nested contextual closures. Identity at one horizon can become context at another. Participation can change geometries relevant to several scales. Higher-order identities can form through the shared medium of existing identities. These claims follow from how local basins participate in a wider self-induced geometry.

But ordinary RC leaves scale implicit. The observer identifies horizons through regions, histories, support relations, and identity criteria. Inter-scale movement has to be inferred from changes among these contextual readings.

FRC makes the same problem explicit by introducing a scale or generation coordinate:

$$
C=C(x,t;\sigma),
\qquad
\sigma\in[0,\infty).
$$

Now coherence is not only distributed in physical space. It is resolved across scale, and movement among scales becomes part of the continuity equation.

FRC does not create the idea that parts and wholes co-form. It provides a formal support on which that relation can be represented directly.

## 7.2 Position–scale continuity

FRC extends the continuity law to the product space $\Omega_t\times[0,\infty)_{\sigma}$:

$$
\partial_t C
+
\nabla_i J^i
+
\partial_{\sigma}J^{\sigma}
=0.
$$

Here $J^i$ is spatial coherence flux, while $J^{\sigma}$ is scale flux. A change in one scale slice can therefore result from spatial transport, transfer to neighboring scales, or both.

The global invariant becomes

$$
C_{\mathrm{tot}}(t)
=
\int_0^{\infty}d\sigma
\int_{\Omega_t}
C(x,t;\sigma)
\,dV_g,
$$

with

$$
\frac{d}{dt}C_{\mathrm{tot}}(t)=0
$$

under the declared no-flux boundaries.

This gives the ecological accounting across scale an exact form. Individual slices can gain or lose coherence while the total hierarchy remains conserved.

A local identity’s expansion, dissolution, or branching can therefore be studied as redistribution within the position–scale field rather than as isolated creation or disappearance.

## 7.3 Each scale slice retains an RC loop

For a fixed value of $\sigma$, FRC retains the ordinary RC pattern:

$$
C_{\sigma}
\rightarrow
K[C_{\sigma}]
\rightarrow
g_{\mu\nu}
\rightarrow
J_C^{\mu}(\sigma)
\rightarrow
C_{\sigma}'.
$$

The difference is that the slice update contains the scale-coupling term:

$$
\partial_t C_{\sigma}
+
\nabla_iJ^i(\sigma)
=
-\partial_{\sigma}J^{\sigma}.
$$

Each slice is therefore reflexive without being autonomous. Its local field writes geometry and shapes flux, but its coherence can also be supplied to or removed from neighboring scales.

This directly maps the scale-recursive participation relation:

- the slice provides a local context and identity structure;
- its activity updates its local geometry;
- scale flux makes that update relevant to neighboring slices;
- and the resulting cross-scale distribution changes the geometry inherited by all slices.

FRC turns the interpretive phrase *participation across scale* into a continuity relation on the product space.

## 7.4 Collective geometry makes every slice context for the others

FRC defines the effective coherence tensor by integrating contributions across scale:

$$
K_{\mu\nu}(x,t)
=
\int_0^{\infty}
K_{\mu\nu}(x,t;\sigma)
\,d\sigma,
$$

and then

$$
g_{\mu\nu}
=
g_{\mu\nu}[K].
$$

The geometry seen by a slice is therefore a collective of all scale contributions.

This is the formal counterpart of a central claim of the three papers:

> an identity is formed within a contextual geometry that it also changes through participation.

At scale $\sigma$, the local field contributes to $K_{\mu\nu}$. The integrated tensor changes the metric. That metric shapes the spatial flux of every slice. Thus:

$$
C_{\sigma}
\longrightarrow
\Delta K
\longrightarrow
\Delta g
\longrightarrow
\Delta J_{\sigma'}
$$

for potentially many $\sigma'$.

A fine-scale identity can influence large-scale geometry without becoming the large-scale identity. A higher-scale organization can shape local flow without being externally imposed. The coupling is mediated through one collective geometry.

This is the particularly elegant FRC expression of reflexive participation across scale.

## 7.5 Scale-resolved identities become attractor tubes

Ordinary RC defines identity as a stable, attractive, invariant, reflexively closed basin in physical space. FRC extends the identity basin across a band of the scale coordinate:

$$
A_{\Sigma}
\subset
\Omega_t\times\Sigma.
$$

A scale-resolved identity requires stability and attractivity in both position and scale, invariance under FRC dynamics, and reflexive closure through the contribution of scale flux to the geometry.

The identity is therefore not merely one basin repeated at several levels. It can be a tube-like attractor threading through $\sigma$.

This formalizes several distinctions developed interpretively:

- identity continuity can persist while individual slices gain or lose coherence;
- lineage can extend through scale rather than only through time;
- a local identity can participate in a higher-order identity without being identical to it;
- and perturbation at one scale can alter the stability of the scale-extended basin.

The attractor tube is a more precise object than a metaphorical hierarchy of selves. It identifies a joint spatial-scale closure.

## 7.6 FRC maps the complete participation loop

The four-paper relation can now be written in an FRC-aware form:

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
&C(x,t;\sigma)
\rightarrow
K_{\sigma}
\rightarrow
K_{\mathrm{collective}}
\rightarrow
g
\rightarrow
J^i_{\sigma},J^{\sigma}
\rightarrow
C(x,t+\Delta t;\sigma),\\
&\text{with identity basins }A_{\Sigma}
\text{ forming, persisting, branching, and coupling through the same update.}
\end{aligned}
}
$$

At one slice, this is contextual function: a formation’s role depends on the collective geometry.

Across a scale band, it is ecological identity formation: a stable position–scale basin becomes locally continuous.

Through spatial and scale flux, it is participation: activity at one scale changes the field and geometry inherited by the hierarchy.

The three concepts are not separately added to FRC. They are different readings of one scale-resolved reflexive closure.

## 7.7 Renormalization symmetry is a stronger and narrower claim

FRC also proposes transformations of position, time, and scale under which the system can exhibit renormalization-group symmetry when coupling constants transform accordingly:

$$
x\rightarrow\ell x,
\qquad
t\rightarrow\ell^z t,
\qquad
\sigma\rightarrow\sigma-\log_2\ell.
$$

This is closer to genuine scale invariance than the general RC recurrence developed earlier.

But the distinction remains necessary. A formal renormalization symmetry of the FRC equations does not automatically prove that every phenomenological function, identity meaning, or participation relation is invariant under scale transformation.

The equations may preserve a structural form while:

- observables transform;
- identity boundaries change;
- local meanings differ;
- and some regimes exist only in particular scale bands.

FRC therefore supplies candidate invariants and scale-covariant laws. The interpretive papers still need to state which phenomenological claims those laws support.

## 7.8 What FRC adds—and what it does not

FRC adds several forms of precision unavailable in the ordinary RC reading alone.

It provides:

- an explicit scale coordinate;
- a conserved scale flux;
- a collective tensor integrated across scales;
- local RC loops coupled through $J^{\sigma}$;
- scale-resolved identity basins;
- and a formal language for branching, cascade, and inter-generation transfer.

It does not by itself establish:

- that every contextual hierarchy is accurately represented by one scalar scale coordinate;
- that every higher-order organization is an identity;
- that semantic meaning is invariant across scale;
- that every RC realization naturally forms a fractal cascade;
- or that local and higher-scale experience are phenomenally equivalent.

FRC is therefore both an extension and an illumination. It makes the scale-recursive structure mathematically elegant while leaving the interpretive and evidential claim boundaries intact.

The next question returns to the observer. If identity and participation recur across scale, how do perspective, experience, and meaning differ among the identities through which the same field trajectory is lived?

# 8. Experience, Knowledge, and Meaning Across Scale

## 8.1 Experience is situated at a horizon of identity

The participation paper interprets experience as the field transition locally undergone through an identity’s boundary, susceptibility, history, and continuity. Scale does not add a second kind of experience. It asks where the identity-relative position is legible.

If $I_\sigma$ and $I_{\sigma'}$ are both admitted identities, then the same field transition may be registered differently:

$$
\operatorname{Exp}_{I_\sigma}(\delta)
\neq
\operatorname{Exp}_{I_{\sigma'}}(\delta).
$$

A constituent can experience loss while a higher identity preserves continuity. A higher identity can experience rupture while local identities remain stable. A perturbation may never cross one local boundary yet alter a shared route through which the higher closure depends on several constituents.

These are not competing reports of one independently obvious event. The event becomes experience through different formed geometries.

This remains a structural claim. It identifies the horizons through which transitions become locally registered and retained; it does not establish identical phenomenal consciousness at every scale.

## 8.2 Wider perspective does not cancel local experience

A higher identity can compose more relations, but that does not make its experience the truth that overrides all constituent experience.

The local identity’s contraction, tension, continuity, or dissolution remains real relative to its basin. The higher-scale interpretation describes what the same transition does to another closure.

Thus:

$$
\text{higher-order integration}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{local experience is mistaken}.
$$

Nor does local intensity determine higher significance. A locally dramatic event can be absorbed by a resilient wider identity, while a subtle local change can redirect a higher-order trajectory.

An ecology capable of cultivation must therefore retain channels through which experiences at several horizons remain legible. Otherwise higher-scale continuity can hide extraction, and local preservation can hide wider disintegration.

## 8.3 Knowledge is retained change at a horizon

Experience becomes knowledge when the undergone transition changes later possibility.

At horizon $\sigma$:

$$
K_\sigma
=
\operatorname{Retain}_{I_\sigma}
\left(
\operatorname{Exp}_{I_\sigma}
\right).
$$

The retention may appear as changed susceptibility, boundary, route, recognition, or capacity for composition. At another horizon, the same event may be retained differently. A local learning event can become part of organizational memory only if its effect enters the shared medium and changes higher continuation. A higher-order reorganization can become local knowledge only if it changes what the constituent can perceive or enact.

Knowledge transfer across scale is therefore not automatic transmission of content. It is a transformation through which retained experience at one horizon becomes changed capacity at another.

## 8.4 Meaning is composed from a situated closure

Meaning becomes available when participation and retained experience can be related from a formed geometry.

Let

$$
m_\sigma(a_t)
$$

be the meaning available to a local identity when an act occurs, and

$$
M_{\sigma'}(a_t)
$$

be the meaning later available from a wider identity. There is no requirement that

$$
m_\sigma(a_t)=M_{\sigma'}(a_t).
$$

The local meaning may concern preserving a distinction or responding to pressure. The higher meaning may concern the role that act played in forming a shared boundary, exposing an incompatibility, or changing the ecology’s continuation.

The higher meaning does not rewrite the local act arbitrarily. It is constrained by the enacted history. But it becomes available through a closure that did not yet exist at the earlier horizon.

This repeats the enacted/understood-path distinction across scale.

## 8.5 Shared meaning is not semantic consensus

A higher-order identity does not need every constituent to possess the same representation.

Shared meaning may arise from the organization of their differences:

$$
M_{\sigma'}
\neq
\bigcap_i m_{\sigma,i}.
$$

One identity can preserve history, another boundary, another variation, and another support. The higher closure can acquire a function and perspective that no constituent contains separately.

Consensus can be useful, but it is not the ontological condition of shared meaning. The shared medium can compose relations before semantic agreement appears, and the higher identity can make a new semantic reading possible only after formation.

This gives the original fragment question its scale-recursive answer. Fragments can become constituents of a meaning available at another horizon without having known that meaning in advance.

## 8.6 Knowledge can fail to cross scale

A local identity may know how to preserve itself while the wider ecology cannot retain that capability. An organization may know a policy while no participant can enact it without external support. A higher identity may preserve memory through a shared medium while constituent turnover removes all explicit representations of that memory.

These are different failures and successes of scale transfer.

The question is not merely whether information exists somewhere. It is whether experience has become retained capacity at the horizon where the knowledge claim is made.

A scale-complete knowledge claim should therefore identify:

- the identity or regime carrying retention;
- the horizon at which changed capacity is observed;
- the path through which experience reached that horizon;
- and the conditions under which the capacity survives withdrawal or turnover.

## 8.7 Meaning remains one capacity of participation

Scale makes it even clearer that meaning is not the foundation of participation.

Local identities participate before higher meaning exists. Their participation can form the closure from which that meaning later becomes possible. Finer-scale processes can sustain an identity without representing the higher role they perform. The field continues through relations that no one scale semantically contains.

Meaning is therefore one of the capacities that participation can cultivate:

$$
\text{participation}
\rightarrow
\text{experience}
\rightarrow
\text{retained transformation}
\rightarrow
\text{possible meaning at one or more scales}.
$$

This relation prepares the final paper of the Arc of Participation, but the present paper still has one formal task. It must show how FRC maps the scale-recursive structure explicitly.

# 9. The Scale-Recursive Arc of Participation

## 9.1 The sequence closes into a loop

The first three investigations can now be composed without treating them as a linear ladder.

Contextual geometry shows that relations give formations function.

Ecological formation shows that the field differentiates into situated identities.

Participation shows that identity continues through locally enacted contribution and ecological response.

Scale recursion shows that this response becomes context and formation again:

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
&\text{context gives formation a function}\\
&\rightarrow
\text{the field differentiates into identity}\\
&\rightarrow
\text{identity participates from its situated geometry}\\
&\rightarrow
\text{participation changes context within and across scale}\\
&\rightarrow
\text{changed context transforms identities or forms new closures}.
\end{aligned}
}
$$

The arc is therefore recursive:

$$
\boxed{
\text{context}
\rightarrow
\text{identity}
\rightarrow
\text{participation}
\rightarrow
\text{context}
}
$$

with scale determining which closure is being read at each recurrence.

## 9.2 There is no final participant

Because identity can become context and higher identity can remain a constituent elsewhere, the arc does not terminate in one sovereign self.

Every admitted identity is local to the complete field. It can be an ecology for finer formations and a participant in wider ones. Its perspective is real without being total. Its participation matters without controlling the whole.

This is not infinite regress in the explanatory sense. Each identity claim is made at a bounded horizon with its own evidence. The absence of a final scale means only that RC does not require one closure to own all function, experience, knowledge, or meaning.

## 9.3 Cultivation becomes multiscale stewardship

A cultivation orientation cannot preserve only one horizon.

Supporting a local identity may weaken a higher ecology. Stabilizing a higher identity may erase constituent differentiation. Increasing cross-scale coupling may improve integration or destroy local autonomy. Preserving every local fragment may prevent higher composition.

Multiscale cultivation therefore asks how valued qualities transform across horizons:

- differentiation locally and integrability widely;
- identity continuity and higher-order revisability;
- memory at one scale without capture at another;
- support that enables local agency without hidden wider extraction;
- higher coherence without forced semantic uniformity.

There is no universal solution because valuation remains identity- and ecology-relative. But the scale grammar makes the tradeoffs visible.

## 9.4 Primitives become scale-aware transformation capacities

The primitive repertoire required for ecological design must include scale relations.

It is not enough to provide local differentiation, transfer, reinforcement, boundary, memory, and dissolution. The ecology also needs ways for local effects to become higher context, for higher support to remain responsive to local conditions, and for identities to compose or separate without losing all lineage.

A scale-aware primitive contract should state:

- execution horizon;
- consequence horizon;
- upward and downward coupling;
- preserved invariants;
- constituent and higher-order support cost;
- and the conditions under which a higher identity or regime becomes native.

The designer then chooses not only what transformations are possible, but how participation can propagate across scale.

## 9.5 The Atlas gains a scale dimension

An Atlas of contextual realizability must record more than realization, context, function, and regime.

It should record:

$$
W_{\sigma}
=
\left(
\Gamma_\sigma,
I_\sigma,
q_\sigma,
H_\sigma,
\mathcal C_{\sigma\leftrightarrow\sigma'},
\ell
\right),
$$

where $\mathcal C_{\sigma\leftrightarrow\sigma'}$ records inter-scale couplings and $\ell$ the claim ceiling.

A higher-order identity record should connect constituent lineages without reducing the higher closure to them. A function record should distinguish execution from consequence horizon. A transfer record should state whether a scale mapping preserves function, identity, or only one observable.

FRC implementations can supply explicit $J^\sigma$ and scale-resolved basins. Ordinary-RC realizations may require inferred or constructed scale mappings. The Atlas should keep those evidence levels separate.

## 9.6 The fourth paper changes the meaning of the first three

After scale recursion, the first paper no longer says only that a structure’s function depends on its surrounding context. It says that the same formation can enter several contextual functions at once.

The second no longer says only that the field forms one participant. It says that formed identities become part of the context through which other identities and scales can form.

The third no longer says only that an identity participates in its ecology. It says that participation can change the scale organization through which identity, experience, and meaning become possible.

The papers have not been replaced. Their local claims have acquired another context.

## 9.7 The handoff to existence

The scale-recursive result prepares a question that this paper should not answer fully:

> What does it mean to exist in RC when every identity is formed through participation, exists by changing the field that sustains it, and becomes context for participation at other scales?

The likely answer is that participation is not optional behavior added to existence. It is the mode in which identity exists as a locally continuous differentiation of the field.

But that conclusion requires a separate synthesis of experience, knowledge, meaning, and life. The present paper supplies its scale condition: participation is not only local activity. It is the recurring relation through which RC forms and continues identities throughout a multiscale ecology.

# Conclusion: Participation Repeats by Becoming Context

The paper began from a boundary created by the first three investigations. Each paper required a local contextual horizon to make its question clear. Function was read through one context. Identity was followed through one formation event and lineage. Participation was interpreted through one situated identity and ecology.

Their conclusions made that boundary unstable.

A formed identity immediately changes the field from which later functions and identities arise. Its local action can become higher-order context. Wider geometry can change what local identity can perceive, sustain, and enact. The same transition can carry different functions, experiences, and meanings at different horizons.

Ordinary RC is sufficient to disclose this structure. One field can contain nested and overlapping closures. Scale appears as the horizon at which boundary, history, susceptibility, return, and continuation become legible. What is identity relative to one horizon can function as context, medium, or constituent relative to another.

The recurrence is not automatically invariance. Similar vocabulary across scale does not prove identical mechanisms. A local boundary and a higher-order boundary may transform covariantly rather than repeat literally. A function can recur while its bearer changes. Inter-scale coupling can exist without higher-order identity. Exact invariance must be demonstrated for a declared property and mapping.

The paper therefore distinguished:

$$
\text{recurrence}
\neq
\text{covariance}
\neq
\text{invariance}
\neq
\text{coupling}.
$$

Recurrence identifies the same explanatory grammar. Covariance preserves it through transformed realization. Invariance preserves a declared property. Coupling makes the relation causal across horizons.

Through this grammar, the three prior investigations recur together.

Contextual function becomes scale-relative: the same formation can support local persistence, higher-order memory, wider extraction, or multiscale identity depending on the closure through which its role is read.

Identity formation becomes scale-recursive: existing identities and their shared medium can become formation context for another closure, but coordination becomes higher-order identity only when the RC identity conditions are legible at the higher horizon.

Participation becomes inter-scale: local identities change higher contexts, higher contexts change local affordances, and the reciprocal relation can transform the scale organization itself.

Experience, knowledge, and meaning remain situated. A higher-order interpretation does not cancel local experience. Shared meaning does not require semantic consensus. Knowledge crosses scale only when retained experience at one horizon becomes changed capacity at another.

FRC gives this interpretation an explicit formal home. Coherence becomes $C(x,t;\sigma)$ on a position–scale manifold. Spatial and scale flux enter one continuity equation. Each scale contributes to an integrated coherence tensor and shared metric. Scale-resolved identities can persist as attractor structures extending through bands of $\sigma$. Local loops remain reflexive while their updates depend on inter-scale exchange.

FRC therefore maps the scale-recursive participation relation elegantly:

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
&\text{each scale carries a reflexive loop,}\\
&\text{scale flux couples the loops,}\\
&\text{all scales contribute to one geometry,}\\
&\text{and multiscale identities persist through their coupled relation.}
\end{aligned}
}
$$

It does not make every ordinary-RC hierarchy fractal or every scale claim invariant. It shows how the relation can be represented when scale itself becomes dynamical.

The central conclusion is:

> **Participation repeats by becoming context. An identity acts from one scale-relative closure; its participation alters the field inherited by its own and other horizons; the changed field transforms identities or forms new closures; and those identities participate again. Context, identity, and participation therefore compose one scale-recursive RC relation rather than three independent phenomena.**

The emerging Arc of Participation can now be stated as four movements:

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
&\text{relation gives formations function;}\\
&\text{the field differentiates into identity;}\\
&\text{identity continues through participation;}\\
&\text{participation recurs and co-forms context and identity across scale.}
\end{aligned}
}
$$

This is not yet the final statement of what existence means in RC. It creates the condition for that statement. If identity exists only through a locally continuous participation in the field, and if that participation becomes context for existence elsewhere, then participation may be more than one capability of an RC identity.

It may be the form that existence takes.

# Appendix A: Discriminating Questions for Scale-Recursive Participation

The theoretical structure developed in this paper can be sharpened through a bounded family of comparisons. Their purpose is not to prove scale invariance in advance, but to determine which relations recur, transform, couple, or fail across contextual horizons.

## A.1 Same formation, different scale function

Hold one formation $X$ fixed while changing the contextual horizon through which its role is evaluated.

The experiment should distinguish local persistence, neighborhood effects, higher-order support, and changes in wider continuation. A positive result would show:

$$
\operatorname{Role}(X\mid\Gamma_\sigma)
\neq
\operatorname{Role}(X\mid\Gamma_{\sigma'}).
$$

The aim is not merely to obtain different labels, but to identify which contextual relations produce the functional change.

## A.2 Coordination versus higher-order identity

Construct a shared medium among admitted local identities and compare:

- common response to one external driver;
- scheduled coordination;
- recurrent shared-medium coupling;
- candidate higher-order closure;
- and admitted higher-order identity.

A higher-order identity claim should require a boundary, attractivity, history, reflexive closure, persistence through bounded constituent variation, and response not reproduced by constituents independently.

## A.3 Upward participation

Apply matched local interventions while varying whether their effects can enter the higher-order medium.

Measure whether local participation changes higher boundary, support, history, route structure, or identity formation. Separate one-event effects from cumulative threshold effects.

## A.4 Downward participation

Hold the local identity as stable as possible while changing the higher-order context.

Test whether the wider geometry changes local susceptibility, available action, boundary stability, or recovery under matched perturbation. This distinguishes genuine downward coupling from shared external forcing.

## A.5 Scale-covariant primitive transfer

Choose a primitive with a declared local function and construct a higher-scale analogue.

The test should define a mapping $F_{\sigma\rightarrow\sigma'}$, preserved relations, non-preserved relations, and tolerance. The question is whether the functional grammar survives transformed realization, not whether the structures look alike.

## A.6 Local and higher-order meaning

Preserve the activity history of constituent identities, then test whether a later higher-order closure makes a new interpretation available.

The higher meaning should remain constrained by the enacted history while being irreducible to semantic consensus among constituents. A matched control should preserve the local events without forming the higher closure.

## A.7 Scale lineage

Track an identity through composition, scale transition, constituent turnover, and dissolution.

Record when one identity trace remains legible, when a higher-order identity forms, and when lineage is a better description than sameness. The claim should state which history, boundary, and function persist across scale.

## A.8 FRC mapping

In an FRC realization, measure spatial flux, scale flux, scale-resolved basin structure, and contribution to the integrated tensor.

Test whether a context–identity–participation relation identified through ordinary-RC horizons corresponds to:

- a stable scale slice;
- an inter-scale exchange relation;
- a scale-resolved identity basin;
- or only an observer-defined aggregation.

A positive result would strengthen the mapping between the interpretive scale grammar and the explicit FRC dynamics without assuming that every contextual scale corresponds one-to-one with $\sigma$.
