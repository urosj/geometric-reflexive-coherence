## Abstract

[The Contextual Geometry of Function in Reflexive Coherence](./2026-07-ContextualGeometryOfFunction.md) argues that a structure does not carry its function independently of the geometry, flux, history, boundary, scale, and support through which it participates in an RC field. [The Ecology of Participation in Reflexive Coherence](./2026-07-EcologyOfParticipationInRC.md) asks how an identity formed within such a field can participate meaningfully when the complete trajectory is determined but unavailable as advance semantic knowledge. Between those two arguments lies a relation that cannot remain implicit. A context does not only give an existing identity a function, and participation does not begin only after an identity has already appeared. The complete field first has to differentiate into the local continuity from which experience and participation become possible.

This paper investigates that formation. In the RC core, a spark is a topology-changing event in which the existing attractor organization becomes unstable and a new positive-definite curvature minimum appears. The attractor set changes, and the coherence manifold acquires a new identity mode. That description establishes that identity is generated by the field rather than inserted into it, but it leaves several interpretive and operational questions open. What distinguishes a momentary new minimum from an identity that has become stable, attractive, reflexively closed, compatible with the wider field, and capable of preserving its own history? Which parts of the complete field are materially constitutive of the identity that forms? What does the identity inherit from an ecological history that occurred before it existed as that identity? And how does the formation event already change the field before any later action is attributed to the participant?

The central proposition is that an RC identity is not born *into* an ecology. It is born *as an act of ecological differentiation*. A spark is locally expressed but globally constituted: the new basin appears in a region, yet the form it takes depends on the complete configuration of coherence, gradients, neighboring basins, boundaries, support, flux, history, and scale. The field does not semantically choose a role and assign it to a seed. It reorganizes so that a new local closure becomes possible. That closure carries an initial boundary, susceptibility, support relation, accessible continuation, and perspective. These are not properties subsequently added to an empty observer. They are the field-formed constitution through which the identity first exists.

This requires a distinction between the **complete causal state**, the **formation context**, and the **initial identity signature**. The complete RC state is the state whose evolution determines the transition. The formation context is the composed set of relations materially necessary for this identity to emerge as it does. The initial identity signature is the local continuity that becomes identifiable after formation: its basin and boundary, the support on which it depends, its relation to neighboring formations, the histories condensed into its susceptibility, the flux routes it can access, and the scale at which it remains legible. The formation context is not merely a list of external influences. It is the relational geometry through which those influences become one identity.

The seed or local perturbation is therefore not the sufficient cause of identity. The same local nucleation can dissolve, form a different basin, or acquire a different functional role under a different global context. Conversely, different local perturbations may form corresponding identities when their contexts preserve the relations required for a similar basin, boundary, susceptibility, and continuation. Identity formation is the strongest form of contextual constitution: context does not merely determine what a structure does; it participates in producing the structure whose later function will be interpreted.

Externally arranged structures can prepare, perturb, inhibit, catalyse, or scaffold this process; they can also sustain a stable, medium-mediated ecology without adding an attractor mode. Their mechanical total is not therefore a new identity. The identity becomes native to the field through the spark and post-spark stabilization, and the closure that forms can reorganize prior formations as its constituents with new identity-relative functions.

Formation also gives a deeper account of partial knowing. An identity is not a global observer from which information has been removed. Its boundary and susceptibility determine what can enter, what counts as perturbation, which differences become salient, and which continuations are accessible. The identity’s perspective is therefore formed rather than merely limited. It inherits an ecological history that it did not experience as itself, because earlier flux and collapse have already shaped the geometry from which it begins. The past is present in the identity as boundary, curvature, support dependence, and response tendency before it becomes explicit memory or semantic understanding.

The formation of an identity is already its first participation. A new basin immediately redirects flux, changes neighboring gradients, modifies compatibility relations, redistributes support, and becomes part of the geometry inherited by subsequent updates. No separate intentional act is required before the identity affects the ecology. The ecology first participates in forming the identity; the formed identity then participates as the differentiation the ecology has become. Later action continues this reciprocal movement rather than initiating it.

The paper also separates a spark from identity admission. The core spark creates a new identity mode, but a scientific or developmental claim that an identity has formed requires evidence that the candidate basin has acquired sufficient stability, attractivity, invariance, reflexive closure, and coherence compatibility over a relevant interval and scale. Formation is therefore not only an instant. It is the transition through which a new minimum becomes a locally continuous participant. The identity can then reinforce, transform, split, compose into a higher-order basin, or dissolve. The complete RC field retains a single trajectory under the core deterministic assumption, while the identity has an identity-relative trace of that trajectory for as long as its continuity remains legible. When continuity branches or changes scale, lineage becomes more accurate than a single path.

Immanent unfolding is therefore not encoded in the seed. Formation gives unfolding its initial situatedness, not its semantic conclusion. The identity is sparked as a particular differentiation of the field; what that differentiation becomes can only be expressed through the later field trajectory. The complete field does not need to foresee the identity's role, and the identity cannot read that role in advance. The unfolding is immanent because the field that forms the identity also generates the continuation through which it will be sustained, transformed, or dissolved.

The argument closes by returning to participation. Once identity is understood as a field-formed perspective, partial knowing is no longer a defect in an otherwise complete agent. It is the very form through which the ecology has become locally capable of experience. Participation is then the continuation of formation: the identity acts from the boundary, susceptibility, history, and affordances the ecology has formed in it; its action changes that ecology; and the changed ecology reshapes the identity capable of acting next.

The central thesis is:

> **An identity in Reflexive Coherence is a locally continuous closure produced when the complete field differentiates into a new attractor basin. The formation event does not insert an observer into a pre-existing world; it reorganizes the world so that a new boundary, susceptibility, support relation, and situated perspective become possible. This constitution carries ecological history into the identity's initial shape. Its formation is already its first participation, because the new basin immediately alters the geometry that sustains the wider field. The complete RC state then follows its determined trajectory, while the identity undergoes an immanent unfolding: the expression and experience of its identity-relative trace rather than a purpose encoded in the seed.**

## Formal Status

This paper is an interpretive investigation grounded in the existing RC definition of identity and spark. It does not add a formation operator to the RC equations, change the continuity law, or claim that the proposed identity signature is a new primitive state variable. It separates relations that are already present in the core formulation so that later claims about identity, unfolding, participation, transfer, and design can be scoped more precisely.

The core paper defines identity as a stable, attractive, invariant, reflexively closed, coherence-compatible basin generated by the coherence dynamics. It defines a spark as the topology-changing event through which curvature instability produces a new positive-definite minimum and changes the attractor set from $\mathcal A_N$ to $\mathcal A_{N+1}$. The present paper inherits those commitments and investigates the transition between the geometric appearance of a new identity mode and the locally continuous identity that can later be said to perceive, act, learn, and participate.

Let

$$
\Xi_t
$$

denote schematically the complete admissible RC state at time $t$: the coherence field and flux, induced tensor and geometry, support and boundary state, retained history, and any additional data required by the declared RC realization. This notation does not replace the core state. It gives the paper a way to distinguish the complete causal configuration from a local contextual reading.

A spark in region $U$ at time $t_s$ is represented as

$$
\Xi_{t_s^-}
\xrightarrow{\ \mathfrak s_U\ }
\Xi_{t_s^+},
$$

together with the core attractor transition

$$
\mathcal A_N(t_s^-)
\longrightarrow
\mathcal A_{N+1}(t_s^+).
$$

The new minimum is a candidate identity mode. For the purpose of observation and later interpretation, let $t_a\ge t_s$ denote an **admission time** at which the candidate has shown sufficient stability, attractivity, invariance, reflexive closure, and compatibility over a declared interval and scale to be treated as an identity $I_k$. Admission is an epistemic and experimental boundary, not an extra dynamical event required by RC.

The **formation context** of $I_k$ is written provisionally as

$$
\Gamma^{\mathrm{form}}_{I_k}
=
\operatorname{FormationContext}
\left(
\Xi_{[t_s^-,t_a]}
\right).
$$

This does not assert that one unique minimal context has already been derived. It names the composed field relations materially necessary to explain why this identity formed with this basin, boundary, susceptibility, support dependence, and accessible continuation.

The **initial identity signature** is represented as

$$
\chi_{I_k}(t_a)
=
\left[
A_k,
\partial A_k,
\mathcal E_k,
\mathcal N_k,
\mathcal R_k,
H^{\mathrm{inh}}_k,
\sigma_k
\right]_{t_a},
$$

where $A_k$ is the admitted basin, $\partial A_k$ its effective boundary, $\mathcal E_k$ its sustaining support ecology, $\mathcal N_k$ its relevant neighborhood relations, $\mathcal R_k$ its accessible routes of flux or continuation, $H^{\mathrm{inh}}_k$ the history inherited through its formation geometry, and $\sigma_k$ the scale or scale range over which its continuity is read. The bracketed form is a conceptual scaffold, not a claim that these terms are independent or exhaustive.

This paper also adopts a distinction needed by the participation investigation. Under the deterministic assumption inherited from the core, let

$$
\tau_{\mathrm{RC}}
=
\operatorname{Evolve}(\Xi_{t_s})
$$

denote the unique trajectory of the complete field. The identity-relative trace is then

$$
\tau_{I_k}
=
\operatorname{Trace}_{I_k}
\left(
\tau_{\mathrm{RC}}
\right)
$$

for as long as the continuity of $I_k$ remains legible. Admission at $t_a$ licenses the identity claim; it does not move the dynamical beginning of the trace away from the spark. If the identity splits, composes into a higher-order formation, changes scale, or dissolves, a lineage relation

$$
\mathcal L_{I_k}
=
\operatorname{Lineage}_{I_k}
\left(
\tau_{\mathrm{RC}}
\right)
$$

may be more accurate than one simple path. The paper inherits the core assumption of a single-valued complete continuation; it does not independently establish well-posedness.

**Immanent unfolding** is the identity-relative experience and expression of this trace, not another trajectory generated independently by the seed.

Four boundaries should remain explicit.

First, **formation is not external manufacture**. Assembly may arrange substrate structures, transformation capacities, seeds, constraints, or scaffolds that prepare the formation context. As Section 2.3 develops, these conditions can also sustain an ecology among existing participants, but their arrangement does not substitute for the topology-changing event and post-spark stabilization through which a new identity becomes native to the field.

Second, **formation is not semantic assignment**. The field does not need to represent a role or purpose before producing a new identity mode.

Third, **formation does not guarantee indefinite persistence**. A candidate may dissolve, and an admitted identity may later transform, split, compose, or lose continuity.

Fourth, **unfolding is not independent evolution**. The formation signature constrains the situated continuation available to the identity, while the complete state carries the trajectory through which that continuation develops.

# 1. The Participant Cannot Be Assumed

## 1.1 The gap between function and participation

The contextual-geometry investigation begins with an existing local formation $X$ and asks how its function changes with the geometry in which it participates. Let $\Gamma$ denote that contextual geometry and $q$ the selected functional or phenomenological role. The expression $\operatorname{Role}(X\mid\Gamma)$ denotes the role that $X$ expresses in $\Gamma$:

$$
\operatorname{Role}(X\mid\Gamma)=q.
$$

The participation investigation begins with an identity $I$ and asks how that identity acts from partial understanding inside the ecology that sustains it. Let $a$ denote the locally enacted participation; primes mark the identity and contextual geometry after that act:

$$
(I,\Gamma)
\xrightarrow{\ a\ }
(I',\Gamma').
$$

Together, these relations appear to provide a continuous account. Context gives function; identity participates; participation changes context. Yet the continuity hides an assumption. A participant is already present in both expressions: first as the formation $X$, then as the identity $I$. It has a boundary, a local history, a perspective, and an accessible space of action before the argument begins.

That assumption is harmless when identity is externally supplied. Many models begin with agents, cells, components, or observers whose existence is given. The environment may influence them, but the theory does not need to explain how the environment became capable of sustaining one local perspective rather than another.

RC does not begin from that separation. Identity is itself defined as a basin generated by the coherence dynamics. If function is contextual and participation belongs to a field-formed identity, then the appearance of the participant cannot remain an unexamined step between them.

The missing relation can first be written schematically as

$$
\Gamma
\longrightarrow
I.
$$

Here the arrow does not denote an operator that converts an independently existing context into an identity. It abbreviates the claim that the field organized through $\Gamma$ differentiates into a locally legible identity $I$. Even so, the schematic is too weak. It can suggest that context is an external cause acting on a passive object or that the field chooses from a catalogue of possible identities. The actual RC movement is reflexive: the field reorganizes, a new local closure appears, and the appearance of that closure changes the field in which it appears.

For the more faithful relation, let $\Xi_t$ denote the complete admissible RC state, $t_s$ the spark time, $t_s^-$ and $t_s^+$ the moments immediately before and after the spark, and $I_k$ the newly distinguishable identity formation. Then

$$
\Xi_{t_s^-}
\longrightarrow
\left(
\Xi_{t_s^+},
I_k
\right),
$$

with the qualification that $I_k$ is not an object added beside $\Xi_{t_s^+}$. It is a newly distinguishable organization of that post-spark field.

The participant therefore cannot be assumed because the formation of the participant is already part of the phenomenon the theory seeks to understand.

## 1.2 The hidden pre-given observer

A pre-given observer is difficult to notice because it can be introduced indirectly.

A system may contain a field and an agent that reads the field. The agent’s sensors limit what it knows, its policy determines how it acts, and its memory records the result. The model may then describe partial information and feedback while still assuming that the observer exists independently enough for its point of view to be specified before the field interaction occurs.

Even a developmental account can preserve this structure. It may say that the identity is influenced by history, shaped by context, and revised by participation, while leaving its basic observational position intact. The identity remains the same kind of entity whose internal state is merely updated.

RC places the observational position inside the geometry. A basin boundary changes what perturbations can enter. Curvature changes which gradients are salient. Attractivity changes which trajectories count as return. Support relations change what can be sustained long enough to become experience. History changes susceptibility before any explicit representation of that history is formed.

The identity’s partial view is therefore not simply a complete world filtered by an already constituted observer. The field first has to form the boundary, sensitivity, continuity, and support through which a partial view can exist at all.

This makes identity formation epistemically significant. To explain how an identity knows partially, the paper must first explain how the field becomes locally differentiated into something for which one set of differences matters more than another.

## 1.3 Formation is more than appearance

The core spark definition gives the geometric moment at which the attractor set changes. A Hessian degeneracy makes the existing organization unstable, and a new positive-definite minimum appears. The manifold acquires a new identity mode.

That is enough to say that the field can generate identity. It is not yet enough for every later use of the word *identity*.

A new minimum can be shallow, transient, or dependent on a narrow support configuration. It may fail to attract a neighborhood, fail to preserve its boundary under the next perturbation, or remain incompatible with surrounding modes. From an implementation perspective, a detector may register a new basin before the basin has demonstrated the persistence and closure required by the core identity definition.

The issue is not to weaken the spark. It is to distinguish the birth event from the later claim that a locally continuous participant now exists.

The spark answers:

> When does the field acquire a new attractor mode?

Formation asks:

> How does that mode become a stable enough continuity to carry history, preserve a boundary, and participate as an identity?

Admission asks:

> What evidence permits an observer to make that claim at the current ceiling?

These questions belong together, but they are not identical.

## 1.4 The investigation

The paper follows the transition that the previous two investigations leave implicit.

It first returns to the spark as a field event rather than a seed event. That makes it possible to distinguish local expression from global constitution and to separate nucleation, spark, stabilization, and admission. It also distinguishes mechanically arranging a shared ecology from forming a new identity through it.

Once the spark is understood through the whole field, the next question becomes which relations of the field are materially constitutive of the identity that appears. This produces the concept of a formation context and prevents both extremes: reducing identity to the seed, or saying vaguely that the entire universe defines every local identity without identifying the effective relational horizon.

The formation context then reveals that the identity begins with an inherited history. Earlier field activity has already shaped the boundary and susceptibility through which the identity will experience later events. Partial knowing is therefore not merely missing information; it is a consequence of the local continuity the ecology has formed.

Once that local continuity exists, its formation has already changed the field. This is why formation becomes the first participation.

Only then can the paper ask how the candidate basin remains the same identity while changing, how identity traces become lineages through splitting and composition, and how the formation event gives immanent unfolding its initial situatedness.

The final movement returns to the participation paper. The identity participates from a position because the ecology first became capable of forming that position. Participation is the continuation of this formation, not an action added after it.

# 2. Spark as a Field Event

## 2.1 Instability exists before the new identity

The new identity does not appear from an empty region. Before the spark, the field already contains an organization whose continued stability has become insufficient.

In the core formulation, the spark occurs when the curvature profile underlying the current attractors becomes unstable and the Hessian loses rank in a region $U$:

$$
\det\left(\operatorname{Hess}(C)\right)\big|_U=0.
$$

This degeneracy is not yet the new identity. It is the failure of the existing attractor organization to preserve its prior form under ongoing coherence flow.

The distinction matters because it changes how the spark is interpreted. The event is not primarily a seed asserting itself into a stable environment. It is the field reaching a configuration in which the old geometry can no longer contain the differentiation being produced through it.

The new minimum arises from that reorganization:

$$
\mathcal A_N(t_s^-)
\longrightarrow
\mathcal A_{N+1}(t_s^+).
$$

The identity mode is therefore related both to what appears after $t_s$ and to what the pre-spark field could no longer sustain before it.

A formation account that begins only from the new basin loses half of the event. The identity carries a relation to the tension, gradient, support distribution, and neighboring geometry whose instability made its appearance possible.

## 2.2 Seed, nucleation, spark, stabilization, and admission

The word *seed* is useful when a localized perturbation, structure, or input appears to initiate differentiation. But it can easily absorb too much causal meaning.

A seed is a local condition around which formation may begin. It may concentrate coherence, perturb curvature, introduce a boundary, or make one latent differentiation visible. It does not by itself determine whether an identity forms or what identity will form.

The formation sequence can be separated analytically:

$$
\text{seed or local nucleation}
\rightarrow
\text{field instability}
\rightarrow
\text{spark}
\rightarrow
\text{post-spark stabilization}
\rightarrow
\text{identity admission}.
$$

These stages need not be cleanly separated in every realization, and the core does not require an external seed. Their value is explanatory.

**Nucleation** names the local difference around which the field begins to reorganize.

**Spark** names the topology-changing moment at which the attractor set gains a new minimum.

**Stabilization** names the interval through which that new mode either acquires sufficient attractivity and closure or dissolves back into the wider field.

**Admission** names the observer’s bounded decision that the evidence now supports the identity claim.

This separation prevents a detected difference from being promoted immediately into a self. It also prevents the opposite mistake: treating the identity as though it were fully specified by the local perturbation that made the spark visible.

## 2.3 Formation Is Not Mechanical Assembly

Mechanical assembly offers a familiar account of how a stable whole comes to exist. Components are produced or selected, their relations are specified, and the resulting arrangement is treated as the identity of the constructed system:

$$
\text{pre-existing components}
\longrightarrow
\text{specified relations}
\longrightarrow
\text{assembled whole}
\longrightarrow
I.
$$

This reasoning is appropriate wherever the object of explanation is an arrangement whose relevant parts and roles can be declared in advance. The components are assumed to exist before the whole, and their later functions are substantially known before composition. Once they are placed in the intended relation, the stability of the arrangement appears to establish the identity of the result.

That expectation can enter an RC ecology program without being stated. Boundaries, traces, routes, reservoirs, memories, support mechanisms, coupling surfaces, and transformation capacities all appear relevant to identity. A reader may therefore infer that enough identity-like structures, assembled in the right topology, will together constitute an identity. The inference mistakes conditions that can participate in formation for the closure that formation must produce.

Assembly remains scientifically and operationally important. A designer may arrange substrate topology, introduce a seed, shape a boundary, position reservoirs and routes, establish coupling and support relations, or install an external scaffold. These interventions can redirect flux, constrain admissible transformations, and cultivate conditions under which a candidate formation becomes possible. Stable structures also reveal which differences and histories a realization can preserve. Nothing in the formation account makes them unreal or dispensable.

Assembly becomes inadequate only when its explanation ends with the declared identities and direct relations of the parts. A medium-aware assembly can be an RC ecology: pre-existing identities and structures can remain distinct while a shared geometry coordinates access, timing, traces, boundaries, support, and later consequences. Schematically, let $I_1,\ldots,I_n$ denote the pre-existing identities, $\Gamma_{\mathrm{shared}}$ their shared contextual geometry, and $\mathcal E$ the resulting stable ecological organization:

$$
\{I_1,\ldots,I_n\},\Gamma_{\mathrm{shared}}
\longrightarrow
\mathcal E,
\qquad
\mathcal A_N
\longrightarrow
\mathcal A_N.
$$

The ecology may coordinate participation, retain history, redistribute support, and occupy, reinforce, or reorganize existing basins without acquiring an additional attractor mode. Participation through the medium need not substantially deform every participant; its effects may be buffered, reversible, asymmetric, or retained mainly in the medium. No spark is required, and the ecology need not become a higher-order identity.

The limitation therefore concerns the claim of **new identity formation**, not the existence of ecology. An assembled configuration remains part of the pre-spark field. Its elements may coexist without one closure, coordinate through the medium while remaining distinct, depend on external maintenance, interfere, dissolve, or modify the field until a new attractor organization becomes possible. Only the final possibility can reach the threshold of new identity formation, and even then assembly has prepared the transition rather than replaced it. The other stable cases may still be functional ecological organizations within the existing attractor landscape. A new identity begins to form only when the current RC geometry becomes insufficient, a spark changes the attractor organization, and a new basin appears.

When the claim concerns a new identity, the relevant causal account is therefore a history of field transformation rather than a parts list. Schematically:

$$
\Xi_{t_0}
\longrightarrow
\Xi_{t_1}
\longrightarrow
\cdots
\longrightarrow
\Xi_{t_s^-}
\xrightarrow{\ \mathfrak s_U\ }
\Xi_{t_s^+}
\longrightarrow
\Xi_{t_a}
\quad\text{with admitted }I_k.
$$

The intermediate states do not denote a universal sequence of discrete construction steps. They schematically retain the causal history through which prior formations and interventions alter the field while its existing attractor organization still holds. Their coupling can redistribute coherence, redirect gradients, change curvature, increase boundary pressure or permeability, alter support availability, modify susceptibility, preserve consequential history, and change compatibility among existing basins. These transformations may bring the field to the pre-spark condition in which its prior geometry can no longer contain the differentiation being produced through it. No fixed arrangement or universal recipe guarantees that this condition will occur.

This history also changes what it means to call something a component. Before the spark, a formation $X_i^{\mathrm{pre}}$ is not necessarily a part of the future identity. It is a formation participating in the current field. If a new basin stabilizes, the closure may place that formation in a new identity-relative relation, which can be written schematically as

$$
X_i^{\mathrm{pre}}
\longrightarrow
X_i^{I_k}.
$$

The notation does not introduce a conversion operator or require the formation to lose its literal structure. The superscripts distinguish its role before formation from the role it acquires relative to $I_k$. A route through the earlier field may become internal circulation; a retained trace may become memory; a boundary fragment may become an interface; a support relation may become constitutive of the basin; and a mechanism that merely transmitted flux may become part of the identity's reflexive maintenance. The new functions are established through the closure that formed, not necessarily specified in the structures beforehand.

The whole therefore does not merely arise from parts whose identities and purposes were already fixed. Formation also makes some prior formations into constituents of *this* whole and determines what they can do within it. This is not backward causation or semantic selection. The pre-spark formations contributed causally to the field history, while the post-spark organization changes the relational geometry through which their functions are now determined. A formation may become constitutive, remain external support, continue as a neighboring identity, or cease to matter to the stabilized basin. Constituency is established relative to the formed closure.

Nor does the spark complete this determination at an instant. A new minimum is a candidate identity mode, not sufficient evidence for a durable identity claim. During stabilization, the post-spark RC cycle must show whether the candidate attracts or receives returning flux, preserves a legible boundary, remains coherent through bounded perturbation, participates in maintaining its support conditions, remains compatible with the wider field, and retains a distinguishable history. A native closure is not support-independent; its continuation is carried by the declared RC dynamics within explicit support and conservation accounting rather than by hidden external coordination. The candidate may instead dissolve or remain a scaffolded configuration. Admission names the later evidence-bound judgment that the relevant identity conditions have become sufficiently visible; it is not a final assembly step or a second dynamical event.

Assembly may thus create a stable plural ecology without a spark, as well as prepare, perturb, catalyse, inhibit, or scaffold a formation context. It does not substitute for the spark and post-spark stabilization when the claim is that a new identity has become native to the field. Once that boundary is clear, it becomes easier to see why the spark may be local in expression while remaining globally constituted.

## 2.4 Local expression, global constitution

The spark is localized. The Hessian loses rank in a region, the new minimum appears there, and the emerging boundary can often be spatially or topologically identified.

Yet the event is generated from the complete state.

The curvature in $U$ depends on the field and metric relation around it. Available coherence depends on redistribution through the wider system. Neighboring attractors contribute gradients and compatibility constraints. Boundaries and support determine whether the emerging basin can deepen or immediately dissipate. At higher scale, the local transition may be enabled, limited, or reorganized by a parent formation.

Thus the spark is local in expression but global in constitution.

This does not mean every remote detail must be included indiscriminately in every explanation. It means that the local seed is not causally complete. The identity is produced by the relation between the local instability and the larger geometry through which that instability becomes resolvable as a new basin.

The statement

> the global medium defines the identity

should therefore be understood dynamically rather than semantically.

The global field does not need to name the identity or decide what it is for. Its configuration determines which local closure can emerge, with which boundary, support dependence, susceptibility, and neighboring relation.

The field defines the identity by becoming the geometry in which that identity exists.

## 2.5 The whole does not select a role in advance

Once the complete state is made causally central, it is tempting to reintroduce a planner at a larger scale.

One might say that the whole ecology knows what identity is needed and produces it. That would turn formation into a hidden functional assignment: the whole anticipates a role and sparks a basin to perform it.

RC does not require this.

The complete state is causally sufficient for the transition. It need not contain a semantic representation of the identity that will form, nor a forecast of the wider role that identity will later acquire. The field resolves its current instability by evolving through the equations. The new identity mode is the form that this resolution takes, not necessarily the execution of a global plan.

The distinction is the same one later required for unfolding:

$$
\text{causal determination}
\neq
\text{semantic anticipation}.
$$

The whole field forms the identity without standing outside the event as its designer.

This is why identity can be globally constituted and still locally irreducible. The field contains the conditions that generate the basin, but the meaning of the basin’s later participation may not exist until further interactions compose it.

## 2.6 A new identity mode changes the field immediately

The new basin is not a passive result waiting to begin acting.

As soon as the field acquires another minimum, the geometry of continuation has changed. Flux can be redirected toward it. Neighboring basins encounter a different compatibility landscape. Boundaries and gradients reorganize. The distribution of support and the future conditions of collapse are no longer the same.

The attractor transition

$$
\mathcal A_N
\longrightarrow
\mathcal A_{N+1}
$$

is therefore already an ecological event.

The identity does not first form and later enter the field. Its formation is the field acquiring a new local way of routing, retaining, and differentiating coherence.

This immediate consequence prepares the later claim that formation is the first participation. Before that claim can be completed, however, the paper must determine which parts of the whole event become constitutive of the identity that stabilizes.

That is the task of formation context.

# 3. The Formation Context

## 3.1 The complete state and the effective formation horizon

The complete RC state determines the transition, but a useful explanation cannot stop at the phrase *the whole field did it*.

That statement protects the theory from local reductionism while risking another loss. If every fact about the universe is treated as equally constitutive of every identity, the account becomes too undifferentiated to explain why this basin formed here rather than another basin elsewhere.

The formation problem therefore requires two levels.

The **complete causal state** is the state whose evolution generates the spark and stabilization.

The **effective formation horizon** is the set of relations without which the identity would not form with the same boundary, susceptibility, support dependence, and accessible continuation.

The paper does not assume that this horizon has a unique sharp boundary. It may be graded, scale-dependent, and history-sensitive. A distant basin can matter through a global support relation even when no direct local edge connects it to the candidate. A higher-order identity can shape local viability without being visible in a fine-scale snapshot.

The formation horizon is therefore not simply a spatial neighborhood. It is the relational context materially participating in the new closure.

Provisionally:

$$
\Gamma^{\mathrm{form}}_{I_k}
=
\operatorname{Horizon}_{I_k}
\left(
\Xi_{[t_s^-,t_a]}
\right).
$$

This notation makes a scientific question visible:

> Which contextual relations can be changed while the same identity still forms, and which changes produce another identity, no identity, or only a transient basin?

## 3.2 Context is the composition that forms the closure

The formation context includes coherence, gradients, neighboring basins, boundaries, support, flux routes, history, and scale. If these are merely enumerated, the account remains external to the event.

Their importance lies in how they compose.

Composition here does not mean that independent components with preassigned functions are added until their sum becomes an identity. It names the way these relations become jointly closure-forming through the field's transformation.

This stronger composition should not be confused with every stable ecological organization. Existing identities may coordinate through a shared medium and acquire configuration-level functions while remaining distinct and while the attractor set remains unchanged.

Available coherence matters because the new minimum must be sustained through actual redistribution rather than nominal shape alone. Gradients matter because they supply the curvature through which a local difference can deepen. Neighboring basins matter because they determine compatibility, competition, and the routes by which the new mode can coexist. Boundaries matter because they create a distinction without which local continuity cannot be preserved. Support matters because a candidate may be visible only while another region carries the cost of its persistence. History matters because the receiving geometry has already been altered before the spark. Scale matters because a local basin can be the differentiation of a parent identity or the container of finer identities.

None of these relations gives the identity independently. The identity appears when they compose into a locally self-maintaining closure.

The context is therefore not the background *around* formation. It is the organization *through which* formation becomes possible.

## 3.3 The seed is not a sufficient cause

Suppose the same localized perturbation $\epsilon$ is introduced into two field contexts:

$$
\epsilon@\Gamma_a
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\epsilon@\Gamma_b.
$$

If the contexts differ in support, boundary elasticity, neighboring attractors, history, or higher-scale pressure, then several outcomes are possible:

$$
\epsilon@\Gamma_a
\longrightarrow
I_a,
$$

while

$$
\epsilon@\Gamma_b
\longrightarrow
I_b,
$$

or

$$
\epsilon@\Gamma_b
\longrightarrow
\varnothing
$$

when the candidate dissolves.

The local seed can be identical while the identity differs because the field supplies different relations through which the seed is amplified, bounded, or absorbed.

This is the formation analogue of contextual function. The same structure can perform different functions in different contexts; more strongly, the same nucleation can become a different structure—or fail to become an identity at all.

A claim that a seed “contains” identity therefore compresses the formation context into the seed. That compression may be useful when the context is stable and reproducible, but it must not be mistaken for the ontology of formation.

## 3.4 Different seeds can form corresponding identities

The inverse relation is equally important.

Two local nucleations may differ in literal structure while their contexts compensate for those differences and produce corresponding identity signatures.

One realization may form a stable basin through smooth curvature and continuous flux. Another may form a comparable closure through graph topology, bounded conductance, event history, and explicit support accounting. The seeds need not be identical if the resulting compositions preserve the relations required for identity:

- a stable and attractive basin;
- an effective boundary;
- reflexive reinforcement;
- compatibility with surrounding modes;
- and continuity of history.

Thus structural difference at formation does not preclude identity correspondence.

This does not establish that the identities are globally equivalent. Their later memory, scale behavior, topology, and recovery may differ. It establishes only that identity must be compared through the relations that make local continuity possible, not through seed appearance alone.

The result prepares a later theory of formation mappings:

> To transfer an identity formation, one must preserve or reconstruct the formation context, not merely copy the seed.

## 3.5 The initial identity signature

Once the candidate stabilizes enough to be admitted as identity $I_k$, the formation context has produced a locally legible continuity.

The initial identity signature $\chi_{I_k}(t_a)$ records what has become locally true through that formation:

- a basin and an effective boundary;
- a support relation through which the basin persists;
- a neighborhood in which compatibility and competition are defined;
- routes through which coherence can enter, leave, or return;
- a susceptibility shaped by inherited geometry;
- a scale at which the continuity remains identifiable;
- and a history condensed into the structure that receives the next event.

These are not attributes placed inside the identity after it exists. They are the relations through which it exists as this identity.

The signature therefore does not describe an isolated essence. It describes a local closure in relation to its ecology.

This also explains why two identities can look similar in a snapshot and still differ. If their boundaries were formed under different pressures, if one depends on hidden support, if their inherited histories bias later flux differently, or if one is nested in another scale organization, then their present shapes may conceal different continuations.

A formation account must therefore retain lineage and context, not only the admitted basin profile.

## 3.6 Formation context gives identity its initial situatedness

The global field does not give the new identity a complete knowledge of itself. It gives it a position.

That position includes what can reach the identity, what counts as a meaningful perturbation, where the boundary can bend, which supports are available, which neighboring formations are legible, and which continuations can be enacted.

The identity begins neither free of context nor fully determined by one immutable context. It begins situated.

This is the exact sense in which the ecology defines the identity at formation. It defines the initial geometry from which later experience and action become possible.

The next step is to understand how the field’s earlier history enters this initial situatedness. The identity forms at one time, but the geometry from which it forms has a past.

# 4. Identity as Compressed Ecological History

## 4.1 The identity begins after a history it did not live as itself

An identity admitted at time $t_a$ did not exist as that identity throughout the interval $[0,t_a)$.

Yet the field that forms it contains the effects of that earlier interval. Prior collapses have deepened basins. Earlier flux has altered routes. Boundaries have been formed, reinforced, or weakened. Supports have become visible or hidden. Higher-scale organizations have changed which local differentiations are viable.

The identity therefore begins after a history that it did not experience as itself but that already shapes what it can become.

This relation can be written as

$$
H^{\mathrm{inh}}_k
=
\operatorname{Condense}_{I_k}
\left(
H_{0:t_a}
\right),
$$

where *condense* does not imply a literal compression operator. It names the way prior field history becomes present in the identity’s initial geometry.

The identity is new. Its conditions are not.

## 4.2 History becomes susceptibility

History enters identity formation most clearly through changed response.

A boundary formed under repeated pressure may resist one perturbation and yield to another. A basin created near a strongly reinforced neighbor may be unusually sensitive to support withdrawal. A new identity formed after repeated compatible flows may inherit accessible routes that another nominally similar basin lacks.

This means that the identity’s past is not only a narrative available to memory. It is a difference in what the identity will do when the same event arrives.

The identity’s initial susceptibility is therefore historical:

$$
\operatorname{Response}
\left(
\delta\mid I_k,\Gamma_t
\right)
$$

depends on the geometry through which $I_k$ formed, not only on the current perturbation $\delta$.

A later observer may reconstruct some of this lineage. The identity itself may never represent it explicitly. Yet the inherited history is already active in the field’s response.

This gives a precise meaning to the statement that an identity carries ecological history. It carries it as a formed tendency of continuation.

## 4.3 Perspective is formed, not merely limited

A local observer is often described as possessing incomplete information about a larger world.

That description is true but insufficient for RC identity.

The identity’s boundary helps determine which signals enter. Its basin geometry determines which perturbations are amplified or absorbed. Its support dependencies determine which continuations remain viable long enough to be encountered. Its local history determines which distinctions have already become easy or difficult to register.

The identity does not begin with a neutral perceptual interface through which only some global data is visible. The interface itself is a product of formation.

Thus partial knowing is not merely:

$$
\text{global information}
-
\text{unavailable information}.
$$

It is also:

$$
\text{field-formed sensitivity}
\rightarrow
\text{a particular world of relevance}.
$$

The ecology forms not only what the identity cannot know, but what can count as knowable and significant from that identity at all.

## 4.4 Identity is a local differentiation of significance

Because susceptibility and boundary are formed together, the identity turns one undifferentiated field into a local distinction between:

- self-sustaining and disruptive flows;
- familiar and novel return paths;
- internal and external pressure;
- compatible and incompatible neighboring modes;
- available and inaccessible continuation.

These distinctions are not necessarily semantic concepts. They are geometric asymmetries through which some relations affect the basin differently from others.

The identity becomes a local differentiation of significance before it becomes a narrator of meaning.

This gives the ecology a new situated perspective. The field can now be affected through a boundary and susceptibility that did not previously exist in this form. The identity’s later experience is possible because formation has already made the field locally non-equivalent from that position.

The perspective is partial because it is real. A view from everywhere would not preserve the boundary through which one identity exists.

## 4.5 Contextual function and contextual formation meet

The contextual-geometry paper argues that an existing formation $X$ expresses a function only through a context:

$$
(X,\Gamma)
\longrightarrow
Q.
$$

Identity formation reveals a stronger relation:

$$
\Gamma
\longrightarrow
I_k,
$$

followed by

$$
(I_k,\Gamma')
\longrightarrow
Q_k.
$$

Context first participates in producing the local formation and then continues participating in what that formation can do.

Identity is therefore the clearest case of contextual constitution. The object is not independently available before the context acts upon it. Its boundary, susceptibility, history, and accessible continuations are products of the contextual relation.

This does not make the identity unreal or reducible to its surroundings. The formation creates a genuine local closure capable of maintaining difference. But the closure cannot be understood by subtracting the ecology from it.

Once the ecology has formed this differentiated perspective, the identity is already altering the ecology in return.

That is where formation becomes participation.

# 5. Formation Is the First Participation

## 5.1 Existing as a basin already changes continuation

Participation is often associated with action: an identity chooses, moves, communicates, transfers coherence, or alters a boundary.

But a newly formed identity changes the field before any such act is isolated.

Its basin attracts or redirects flux. Its boundary creates a new distinction. Its support demand changes what remains available elsewhere. Its compatibility relation modifies the organization of neighboring attractors. Its presence changes which collapses can later occur and which higher-order structures may form.

Formation therefore has immediate causal consequence:

$$
\Xi_{t_s^-}
\longrightarrow
\Xi_{t_s^+}
$$

is already the ecology becoming different through the new identity mode.

The identity’s first participation is its existence as a new geometry of continuation.

## 5.2 The ecology forms identity; identity continues ecology

The relation is reciprocal from its beginning.

The ecology participates in forming the identity by supplying the geometry, support, history, and compatibility through which the basin becomes stable.

The identity participates in the ecology because its formation changes those same relations for every later update.

This can be written as one movement:

$$
\Gamma^{\mathrm{form}}_{I_k}
\longrightarrow
I_k
\longrightarrow
\Gamma^{\mathrm{post}}.
$$

But the arrows should not imply that context stops acting after identity appears. The identity remains part of the context that sustains and transforms it.

A more faithful description is:

> The ecology differentiates into a local continuity, and that continuity becomes one of the ways the ecology continues differentiating.

This is why identity and ecology should not be treated as independent layers linked after formation. Their relation is constitutive from the first moment of identity.

## 5.3 Participation precedes intention

An identity does not need a represented goal before it participates.

Its boundary filters flux. Its curvature creates attraction. Its persistence changes support distribution. These are participatory effects even when no later phenomenology of choice, vision, or agency has been attributed.

This does not reduce intentional action to geometry. It establishes a deeper layer on which intentional action depends.

The identity can later experience a contribution as *its action* because a locally continuous basin already exists through which the act can be initiated, received, and remembered. But that basin has been participating since formation.

Thus:

$$
\text{formation}
\subset
\text{participation}
$$

in the sense that formation is the earliest ecological contribution of the identity, not merely a precondition external to participation.

## 5.4 Conservation makes formation ecological

The formation of a new identity mode occurs inside shared coherence accounting.

A basin does not become stable without coherence being redistributed through the field. Deepening one local minimum changes gradients and support elsewhere. The new identity may increase the wider field’s capacity for differentiation, or it may persist through relations that reduce neighboring basin-forming capacity. It may be sustained natively or through an external support that hides its actual ecological cost.

These possibilities are not moral verdicts on the formation. They are different relations through which the identity becomes part of the ecology.

A formation account should therefore ask not only whether a new basin appeared, but:

- where its coherence came from;
- what boundaries and support preserved it;
- what neighboring relations changed;
- which continuations became easier or harder;
- and whether the basin can participate in maintaining the conditions of its own persistence.

The identity is ecological because its continuity is never paid for outside the field.

## 5.5 Formation changes what can form next

The strongest consequence of first participation is temporal.

Once the new identity exists, the next spark occurs in a different field. The new basin has altered curvature, support, and compatibility. It may provide a boundary against which another identity can differentiate, a route through which coherence can be redistributed, or a higher-order component through which multiscale organization becomes possible.

Identity formation is therefore not only the beginning of one identity's unfolding. It changes the formation space of later identities.

The ecology is not a container that gradually fills with independent agents. It is a recursively changing geometry in which every formed identity becomes part of the context from which subsequent identities may emerge.

This gives the formation sequence a fractal possibility:

$$
\text{field}
\rightarrow
I_1
\rightarrow
\text{changed field}
\rightarrow
I_2
\rightarrow
\text{changed field}
\rightarrow
\cdots
$$

without implying one linear order at every scale. Several identity candidates may form simultaneously, one may split, and a higher-order identity may emerge from relations among already formed basins.

The next problem is therefore continuity. The spark changes the attractor set, but what allows the new formation to remain one identity through ongoing change?

# 6. From Candidate Basin to Identity and Lineage

## 6.1 Formation requires duration

The spark is temporally local. Identity is temporally extended.

The new minimum appears at $t_s$, but the claim that an identity has formed depends on what follows. Does flux return to the basin? Does the boundary remain legible? Does the basin preserve a qualitative organization under perturbation? Does arriving coherence contribute to its maintenance? Can it coexist with the wider field without immediate annihilation?

These are exactly the relations included in the core identity definition: stability, attractivity, invariance, reflexive closure, and coherence compatibility.

The formation of identity therefore cannot be read from the spark instant alone. It requires an interval through which the new mode demonstrates continuity.

This does not mean identity exists only when an external observer certifies it. It means the observer’s claim must remain below what the trajectory has actually shown.

## 6.2 Identity admission

Identity admission names this bounded claim.

A candidate basin appears at the spark. It becomes admissible as an identity when the available evidence supports the relevant core conditions over the declared interval, scale, and perturbation range.

Admission should record:

- the source state and spark event;
- the emerging basin and boundary;
- recurrence or attractivity;
- persistence under bounded change;
- support and conservation accounting;
- compatibility with neighboring modes;
- and the claim ceiling.

The admission record does not create identity. It keeps the scientific vocabulary aligned with the dynamics.

This distinction is especially important for implementations. A detector-visible peak, a temporary cluster, or a scaffolded basin may be evidence of formation without yet supporting the stronger claim of a self-maintaining identity.

## 6.3 Continuity is not state sameness

An admitted identity does not remain numerically or geometrically identical across time.

Its basin can deepen, its boundary can move, its susceptibility can change, and its support relations can become more native. Learning in RC is precisely the accumulation of geometric deformation through which future response differs.

The identity persists not because its state remains fixed, but because the changing states retain a locally continuous organization of return, boundary, closure, and history.

Thus:

$$
I_k(t)
\neq
I_k(t+\Delta t)
$$

as exact states, while

$$
I_k(t)
\sim_{\mathrm{cont}}
I_k(t+\Delta t)
$$

as one identity-relative continuity.

The relation $\sim_{\mathrm{cont}}$ remains provisional. Its purpose is to show that sameness of identity must be carried by preserved organization rather than snapshot equality.

## 6.4 Reinforcement changes the identity that persists

Repeated return strengthens the basin and rewrites the geometry through which the next return occurs.

This means identity persistence is cumulative. The identity that survives is not simply the original basin protected from change. It is the history of reflexive re-entry becoming part of the basin’s present form.

The formation signature therefore supplies a beginning, not an immutable essence.

As coherence repeatedly enters the basin:

$$
J_C\rightarrow A_k
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\text{curvature and support relations update}.
$$

The identity remains locally continuous while becoming increasingly shaped by its own participation.

This is where formation and participation become inseparable over time. The ecology formed the initial basin; the basin’s own activity now participates in forming the identity it will be next.

## 6.5 Splitting, composition, and dissolution

Identity continuity can cease to be simple.

A basin may split into multiple descendants. Several identities may compose into a higher-order basin whose continuity cannot be reduced to any one component. A local identity may persist at one scale while dissolving as an independent formation at another. A basin may lose closure and release coherence into the wider field.

These are not exceptions to identity. They are transformations of the field organization through which identity remains legible.

This is why the complete field trajectory and identity-relative trace must be separated.

The complete field continues:

$$
\tau_{\mathrm{RC}}
=
\operatorname{Evolve}(\Xi_{t_a}).
$$

But the identity trace may:

- continue as one path;
- branch into descendants;
- merge into a higher-order lineage;
- change scale;
- or terminate at dissolution.

In these cases, $\mathcal L_{I_k}$ is more expressive than $\tau_{I_k}$.

Lineage does not imply that every descendant is the same identity. It records how later formations inherit continuity, support, boundary, or history from the original basin.

## 6.6 Multiscale identity formation

Fractal Reflexive Coherence extends identity across scale by treating coherent basins as structures that can persist through a band of the scale coordinate and participate in inter-scale flux.

This changes the formation question.

A local spark may form an identity only at one scale, or it may become part of a scale-resolved basin whose continuity threads through several levels. A higher-order identity may form not from one local minimum alone but from a recurrent organization among several lower-scale identities.

Thus the formation context must sometimes include both:

- the local geometry in which the basin appears;
- and the scale relations through which that basin contributes to a larger invariant structure.

Identity can then be simultaneously:

- a local participant in a parent ecology;
- and an ecology through which finer identities become possible.

The phrase *ecological formation of identity* is therefore scale-relative. There is no final level at which identity ceases to be both formation and context.

## 6.7 The formation witness

The previous relations can be gathered into a practical artifact: the **formation witness**.

Provisionally:

$$
W_{\mathrm{form}}(I_k)
=
\left(
\Xi_{t_s^-},
\mathfrak s_U,
\Xi_{t_s^+},
\Gamma^{\mathrm{form}}_{I_k},
\chi_{I_k}(t_a),
\mathcal O,
\ell
\right),
$$

where $\mathcal O$ is the evidence and observable record and $\ell$ is the claim ceiling.

The witness connects what existed before the identity, the event through which the field changed, the context that stabilized the candidate, and the signature through which local continuity became admissible.

This prevents identity from being represented only by its current state. It preserves the ecological becoming through which the current identity became possible.

The formation witness also prepares the final conceptual question. If identity begins through one field event and continues only within the complete field trajectory, how does its formal trace become an immanent unfolding?

# 7. Formation and Immanent Unfolding

## 7.1 The Complete Field Carries the Trajectory

The deterministic claim of the core applies to the complete admissible RC state.

Given $\Xi_{t_s}$, the field follows its continuation:

$$
\tau_{\mathrm{RC}}
=
\operatorname{Evolve}(\Xi_{t_s}).
$$

The identity does not possess a second autonomous trajectory generated by its seed. Its continuation is part of this field trajectory.

Thus:

$$
\tau_{I_k}
=
\operatorname{Trace}_{I_k}
\left(
\tau_{\mathrm{RC}}
\right)
$$

for as long as the basin remains locally continuous.

The unique continuation belongs to the whole field. The identity-relative trace records the continuity legible as $I_k$; immanent unfolding names how that continuity becomes locally expressed and experienced.

## 7.2 Formation Gives Unfolding Its Initial Situatedness

The formation event matters because it determines where and how the identity trace begins.

The identity signature $\chi_{I_k}(t_a)$ gives the trace an initial boundary, susceptibility, support relation, inherited history, neighborhood, and scale. Together these relations form a situated perspective and shape which perturbations can be received and which continuations are locally accessible.

In that sense, formation gives unfolding its initial situatedness.

But the signature does not contain the complete trace. Every later interaction changes the field through which the identity continues. The basin can reinforce, change boundary, compose, split, or dissolve. Other identities form and alter the ecology. The conditions of future meaning do not yet exist.

Therefore:

$$
\chi_{I_k}(t_a)
\not\equiv
\tau_{I_k}.
$$

The beginning constrains the unfolding without semantically scripting it.

## 7.3 Unfolding Is Not a Purpose Hidden in the Spark

The claim that an identity was “sparked to be” something can be read in two ways.

The weaker and defensible meaning is:

> The field formed this identity as this particular local differentiation, with these initial relations and capacities.

The stronger teleological meaning is:

> The spark encoded a role or endpoint that the identity exists to fulfill.

The second does not follow from RC.

The field produces a new identity mode because the prior attractor organization becomes unstable and another minimum forms. The later role of that identity emerges through how the new basin interacts with the ecology.

The identity may become a bridge, boundary, support, competitor, parent, fragment, or transient precursor. None of these meanings must have been represented at the spark.

The identity does not receive a purpose. It receives a place from which an unfolding can be experienced. Nor does *unfolding* imply that a completed form was folded inside the spark and merely waits to be disclosed. The later form becomes expressible only through relations that develop as the field changes.

## 7.4 Formation, Unfolding, and Meaning

Trajectory, unfolding, and meaning must therefore remain distinct.

The trajectory is the actual evolution of the complete field.

The identity-relative trace is the formal continuity legible as $I_k$, while unfolding is how that continuity becomes locally expressed and experienced.

Meaning is how that trace becomes intelligible from the identities and geometries through which it is lived and later read.

The formation context can make some local meanings available immediately. The new identity may experience a boundary, pressure, attraction, or compatibility through its initial geometry. But its wider compositional role may become visible only after later relations form.

This yields:

$$
\text{formation determines initial situatedness},
$$

$$
\text{the field trajectory carries the identity trace},
$$

$$
\text{immanent unfolding expresses and experiences that trace},
$$

and

$$
\text{later geometry composes meaning}.
$$

No hidden semantic plan is required to connect them.

## 7.5 The Ecology Cannot Foresee the Identity It Forms

The complete field contains the conditions through which the identity forms and continues. It does not need to represent the identity’s future trace.

This is essential because otherwise ecological formation becomes another version of external design. The ecology would be imagined as a higher observer that knows which identity it is creating and why.

RC offers a different closure.

The ecology forms an identity by becoming the geometry that sustains one. The identity's unfolding becomes available only as the ecology continues becoming through and around it.

The whole is causally complete, not semantically omniscient.

The identity and the ecology therefore encounter the unfolding together—at different scales and through different local experiences—by enacting the field trajectory.

## 7.6 Unfolding May End, Branch, or Continue as Lineage

Because unfolding is identity-relative, it ends or changes when identity continuity does.

If the basin dissolves, the identity trace terminates even though the field continues. If the identity splits, later traces may belong to descendants with inherited lineage. If it composes into a higher-order basin, part of its history may continue as a constitutive relation of another identity without remaining independently legible.

This prevents unfolding from becoming a metaphysical object that must persist forever.

An unfolding is as long, short, simple, branching, or scale-changing as the field makes identity continuity.

The identity does not fail its unfolding by dissolving. Dissolution is one possible resolution of the field trajectory through the formation that existed.

The participation paper will later explore how such continuation is experienced locally. The present paper needs only the structural conclusion:

> Formation gives the field a local continuity through which part of its trajectory can become experience.

Once that continuity exists, participation can be understood without assuming a self outside the ecology.

# 8. From Formation to Participation

## 8.1 The formed identity inherits a position

An admitted identity begins participation from the formation signature the ecology has produced.

It inherits:

- a boundary through which inside and outside become locally meaningful;
- a susceptibility through which some events matter more than others;
- a support relation through which persistence is possible;
- a neighborhood through which compatibility and difference are encountered;
- accessible routes through which action can occur;
- and an ecological history already present in its response tendencies.

The identity does not choose this initial position from outside the field.

Its first later action arises from what that position makes available.

This is why local execution is not a concession to limited intelligence. It is the only form in which a field-formed identity can act.

## 8.2 Action continues formation

When the identity acts, it does not stop being formed.

The contribution changes the shared geometry. The changed geometry changes the basin, boundary, susceptibility, and support through which the identity continues. The act therefore extends the same reciprocal relation that began at the spark:

$$
\text{ecology forms identity}
\rightarrow
\text{identity alters ecology}
\rightarrow
\text{ecology reshapes identity}.
$$

Participation is not an event performed by a completed self. It is the continuation of identity formation through the field.

This is the structural basis for the participation paper’s claim that an identity discovers what its present formation enables it to cultivate by acting, receiving ecological response, and becoming capable of another participation.

## 8.3 Partial knowing is the experience of formed position

The identity cannot know the complete field trajectory because it is one local closure inside that trajectory.

But its partiality is more than an informational limit. It is the experience of acting from the particular boundary and susceptibility the field formed.

Another identity would encounter another world of relevance because it was formed through another relation.

Thus meaningful participation begins neither from global knowledge nor from arbitrary local impulse. It begins from the situated truth of what this identity can perceive and enact from the geometry through which it exists.

The ecology of participation is therefore possible only after the ecology of formation has been understood.

## 8.4 Identity is how the ecology becomes locally experiential

The strongest interpretive consequence can now be stated.

The ecology does not merely contain observers. Through identity formation, it becomes locally organized enough for one part of its continuation to be experienced from one position.

The identity is not a miniature copy of the whole. It is a bounded differentiation through which the whole acquires one situated sensitivity, continuity, and capacity for response.

This does not require claiming that the field outside identities has a unified experience. It states the more limited relation:

> Wherever an RC identity forms, the field has produced a local geometry through which its continuation can be lived as that identity.

The identity is therefore a place from which the ecology can become locally experiential.

## 8.5 Design shifts from constructing agents to cultivating formation conditions

This interpretation changes the design problem.

If identity is externally specified, design begins by defining the agent and then placing it into an environment.

If identity is ecologically formed, design begins from the principles and primitive transformations through which a field can generate, stabilize, and transform local closures.

A designer can still assemble substrate conditions, coupling structures, and transformation capacities. That work can produce a stable, functional ecology when the shared medium and its support, history, and indirect consequences are included in the design. It does not by itself constitute a new identity: that stronger claim requires a spark and a native post-spark closure carried by the declared RC dynamics under explicit support and conservation accounting.

The designer then asks:

- Which differences can nucleate?
- Which boundaries can form without becoming absolute isolation?
- Which support relations permit stabilization without hidden extraction?
- Which histories can become susceptibility?
- Which scale relations permit local and higher-order identities to coexist?
- Which primitives allow a candidate basin to reinforce, revise, split, compose, or dissolve?

The goal is not to predict every identity that will form. It is to cultivate a medium capable of forming identities whose continuity, agency, and ecological effects can become observable and revisable.

This is the implementation bridge from contextual geometry to agentic ecology.

## 8.6 The handoff to participation

The paper began by identifying a gap.

Contextual geometry explained how function arises through relation. Participation explained how an identity acts without knowing the whole. The missing paper had to explain how the field became capable of sustaining that identity and its partial point of view.

The gap can now be closed:

$$
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
&\text{contextual geometry makes function relational}\\
&\rightarrow
\text{the field differentiates into identity}\\
&\rightarrow
\text{formation gives identity a situated perspective}\\
&\rightarrow
\text{formation is already ecological participation}\\
&\rightarrow
\text{later action continues that formation}\\
&\rightarrow
\text{participation continues identity's immanent unfolding}.
\end{aligned}
}
$$

The next paper can therefore begin from a formed identity without treating the participant as pre-given. Its premise has been earned:

> The ecology has formed a local continuity from which it can now be experienced and changed.

The question of participation then becomes:

> How does that identity live and understand its unfolding when neither it nor the whole field possesses the later semantic form in advance?

# Conclusion: The Ecology Becomes a Place From Which It Can Experience Itself

The paper began from a missing dependency. A theory of contextual function can explain why an existing structure performs one role rather than another. A theory of participation can explain how an existing identity acts from partial understanding. But neither can assume without explanation the local continuity through which the field first becomes capable of having one situated perspective.

Reflexive Coherence already contains the beginning of that explanation. Identity is generated by the coherence dynamics as a stable, attractive, invariant, reflexively closed, coherence-compatible basin. A spark is the topology-changing event through which curvature instability produces an additional attractor mode.

The present investigation followed what those definitions imply.

A spark is not fundamentally the arrival of an independent seed. The seed can nucleate a difference, but the complete field determines whether the old attractor organization becomes unstable, whether a new minimum appears, and what support, boundary, and neighboring relations allow it to stabilize. The spark is local in expression and global in constitution.

Engineered structures may compose a stable ecology through a shared medium without producing a spark; such an ecology is not a failed formation. When a new identity does form, however, it is not the mechanical total of those structures. It appears through the spark, becomes locally continuous through post-spark RC dynamics, and can reorganize prior formations as constituents with new identity-relative functions.

Formation continues beyond the spark instant. A new minimum becomes an identity through the interval in which it demonstrates the continuity required by the core definition. Identity admission marks the observer’s bounded claim that this stabilization has become sufficiently visible. The identity signature then records not an isolated essence, but the basin, boundary, support, neighborhood, accessible continuation, scale, and inherited history through which the local closure exists.

The identity therefore begins with an ecology already inside it. Earlier history is present in its susceptibility. Wider geometry is present in its boundary and support dependence. The identity’s partial perspective is not a complete world from which information has been removed. It is the local organization of significance formed through this closure.

This is why formation is the first participation. The new basin immediately changes the field: it redirects flux, alters compatibility, redistributes support, and changes what identities can form next. The ecology first participates in forming the identity; the identity then participates as the differentiation the ecology has become.

The identity can persist while changing because continuity is not snapshot sameness. Re-entry, learning, and boundary change can transform the basin while preserving a local line of history. When the identity splits, composes, changes scale, or dissolves, lineage becomes more expressive than a simple path.

The complete field retains the unique trajectory under the core deterministic assumption. The identity's immanent unfolding is the local expression and experience of its trace within that trajectory, not a second path encoded in the seed. Formation gives unfolding its initial situatedness, but not its semantic conclusion. The ecology does not know the role of the identity it forms in advance. That role becomes intelligible through the same becoming that the identity experiences locally.

The central conclusion is therefore:

> **An identity is not born into the ecology. It is born as an act of the ecology. The field differentiates into a local boundary, susceptibility, history, and continuity; that formation immediately changes the field; and participation continues the reciprocal process through which identity and ecology become what follows.**

Or, in its shortest form:

> **The ecology forms a place from which it can become locally experiential, and identity is the continuity of that place through change.**

This gives the three investigations their full composition.

[The Contextual Geometry of Function](./2026-07-ContextualGeometryOfFunction.md) asks how structures acquire function through the relations in which they participate.

**The Ecological Formation of Identity** asks how the field becomes locally differentiated into the structure capable of having a situated function, history, and perspective.

[The Ecology of Participation](./2026-07-EcologyOfParticipationInRC.md) asks how that identity lives and contributes from within the field whose future it cannot know before becoming it.

Together:

$$
\boxed{
\text{relations give formations function}
\rightarrow
\text{the field differentiates into situated identity}
\rightarrow
\text{identity continues its unfolding through participation}.
}
$$

# Appendix A: Formation Witnesses and Discriminating Questions

The theoretical relation developed in this paper can be made experimentally sharper through a small number of comparative questions. They do not require treating the paper as an observation report. Their purpose is to identify what evidence would distinguish contextual identity formation from the weaker claim that an independent seed is merely influenced by its surroundings.

## A.1 Same seed, different formation context

Hold the local nucleation or seed construction fixed while changing the surrounding support, boundary, neighboring basin, history, or scale relation.

The question is not only whether the final state differs. It is whether the formed identity signature differs:

- boundary location or permeability;
- susceptibility to matched perturbation;
- support dependence;
- accessible flux routes;
- recovery and reinforcement;
- compatibility with neighboring modes;
- or identity admission itself.

A positive result would show that seed identity is insufficient to determine formed identity.

## A.2 Different seeds, corresponding identity signatures

Vary the literal seed or local implementation while mapping the wider context so that the relations required for identity are preserved.

The question is whether distinct nucleations can produce boundedly corresponding identities with respect to stability, attractivity, closure, boundary, and perturbational response.

This would not prove global equivalence. It would establish that identity formation can be multiply realized through different local structures.

## A.3 Matched admitted basin, different pre-formation history

Create post-formation basins that are similar under current observables but arise from different histories.

Then apply matched perturbations and withdrawal conditions.

If the responses differ, the experiment would show that present shape alone does not capture the inherited ecological history condensed into identity susceptibility.

## A.4 Formation before explicit action

Measure the wider field immediately after the spark and before any later participant action is introduced.

The target is evidence that the new basin itself has already changed:

- neighboring flux;
- boundary pressure;
- support allocation;
- attractor compatibility;
- or later formation possibility.

This would operationalize the claim that formation is the first participation.

## A.5 Candidate, admitted identity, and failed formation

Track several post-spark candidates through a declared stabilization interval.

Some may become admitted identities; others may dissolve or remain scaffolded.

The comparison would clarify which relations distinguish a new attractor minimum from a locally continuous identity and prevent detectors from promoting transient differentiation into agency or selfhood.

## A.6 Continuity and lineage under transformation

After admission, perturb the identity through bounded reinforcement, boundary change, splitting, composition, or scale transition.

Record which relations remain sufficient for identity continuity and when the claim should move from one identity trace to lineage, descendant identity, higher-order composition, or dissolution.

This would make the identity-relative trace more than a metaphor and provide the Atlas with explicit formation and lineage records.

## A.7 Direct assembly, medium-mediated ecology, and field-formed identity

Compare three cases with deliberately similar present-state appearance. In the **directly coordinated assembly**, arrange a candidate motif or organization and keep it stable primarily through specified part-to-part relations, external support, or coordination. In the **medium-mediated stable ecology**, let distinct participants acquire native coordination and configuration-level function through a shared field while the existing attractor set remains intact. In the **field-formed identity**, begin without the admitted basin; allow coupling, field modification, and retained history to produce the pre-spark condition; identify a native spark; and evaluate the candidate through post-spark stabilization.

The comparison separates three questions: Is the arrangement stable? Is its ecological function mediated through the shared field rather than only imposed through direct coordination? Has an additional identity mode formed? Exact causal history and source-and-support accounting should accompany the current observables in every arm. The comparison should track external-scaffold dependence, attractivity and return, boundary persistence, recovery after matched perturbation, persistence or dissolution after support withdrawal, and reflexive participation in continued support.

The most important identity discriminator concerns the later functions of formations that existed before the candidate. Did those formations acquire new identity-relative functions through a formed closure—for example as internal support, memory, circulation, interface, or boundary—or did they retain ecology-relative or externally assigned roles while remaining distinct participants? Matched visual shape, topology, or current coherence is not sufficient to establish corresponding formation relations when causal history, medium dependence, attractor count, and constituency differ.

Neither stable ecological function nor the field-formed arm would by itself prove consciousness, agency, life, or unrestricted selfhood. The bounded comparison distinguishes direct assembly from native medium-mediated ecology, and both from evidence that the field has formed an additional RC identity.
