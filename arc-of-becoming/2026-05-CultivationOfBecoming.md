# Cultivation of Becoming

## A Method for Experiencing and Developing Irreducible Change

**Date:** 2026-05-28

Copyright © 2026 Uroš Jovanovič, CC BY-SA 4.0.

## Abstract

The earlier *Language of Becoming* papers described the phenomenology of Reflexive Coherence: what it feels like when a coherent system stabilizes, fragments, collapses, opens, deepens, or enters abundance. Later methodological work on classification and bounded probes revealed a complementary discipline. In nonlinear, self-describing, computationally irreducible systems, the observer cannot assume that the important classes, rules, endpoints, or outcomes are known in advance. One must learn from what the system expresses.

This paper proposes **Cultivation of Becoming** as a practical synthesis of those threads. It is not a theory of passive acceptance, and it is not a method of control. It is a way of participating in irreducible change without prematurely closing it. The method proceeds through seven movements: orient, observe, classify, probe, withdraw, naturalize, and integrate. It begins with direction but not certainty; it lets experience disclose its class; it treats probes as questions rather than proof; it distinguishes supported expression from natural development; and it returns to the system with a better question.

Cultivation of Becoming generalizes beyond PDE-RC implementation. It applies wherever an embedded observer is trying to develop coherence inside a system whose future forms cannot be fully predeclared: research programs, organisms, relationships, creative work, therapeutic processes, social systems, and artificial or hybrid intelligence. Its central claim is that development in irreducible systems is not achieved by forcing a known endpoint, but by cultivating conditions under which richer properties can become natural.

This keeps Cultivation distinct from Naturalization. Naturalization asks the
narrow bridge question: when does supported capacity become native
availability? Cultivation asks the broader practice question: how does an
observer participate in the whole cycle without forcing closure or optimizing
proxies? Cultivation contains naturalization, but it is not reducible to it.

Later Paper-30 evidence adds a necessary correction. Cultivation is not simply repeated classification and probing. A classification must add reusable or generative leverage, and a probe must reveal a function the system could learn to supply. Otherwise cultivation can collapse into local proxy optimization: the observer keeps making the next scaffold more precise while losing contact with the broader coherence that was supposed to be developed.

## 1. From Description to Cultivation

The *Language of Becoming* papers gave a vocabulary for recognizing the inner weather of coherence. They described stability as flux within bounds, fragmentation as loss of integrated basin structure, leakage as failed containment, collapse as a transition into selected continuation, abundance as surplus capacity, and reinforcement as basin deepening rather than mere repair. They made it possible to speak about becoming not as a vague metaphor, but as lived geometry: a system maintaining itself, opening, closing, resisting, reshaping, and sometimes flourishing.

But description is not yet cultivation.

To describe becoming is to recognize its regimes. To cultivate becoming is to participate in those regimes without reducing them too quickly to success, failure, threat, or proof. A person, organism, research program, or artificial system does not live in a world where all relevant classes are known in advance. It lives inside change. Its next valid form may not yet be visible. Its most important property may not be the one the observer predicted. Its present disturbance may not be a mistake, but neither is it automatically wisdom. It has to be met, classified, tested, and integrated.

This distinction matters because many methods of inquiry and development begin with an endpoint. They ask what the system should become, measure how far it is from that endpoint, and intervene to close the gap. That approach is useful when the target is known and the path is stable. It is less useful when the target is itself being discovered. In irreducible systems, the problem is not only that we do not know the value of the next state. We may not know which properties of that state will matter.

The method that emerged through the later PDE-RC work began to shift this order. It stopped asking first whether the system matched the predicted class. It asked what the system actually expressed. A run that failed the intended endpoint could still reveal score competence, gate competence, loaded-overlap coupling, local-null superiority, body-lineage limits, or a new support-regime class. The failure was not erased, but it was no longer empty. It became a classified event in the system’s becoming.

The next layer came from bounded probes. Once a property had been classified, a small intervention could ask a more precise question: which boundary is missing? Did an event merely appear in telemetry, or did it affect the substrate? Did a substrate consequence become geometry? Was the geometry local or broad? Was it source-specific or control-competitive? Did it persist when the support was withdrawn? The probe did not prove the desired phenomenon. It revealed which layer had changed and which layer remained absent.

Cultivation of Becoming brings these layers together. It asks how an embedded observer should live, learn, design, and develop inside systems whose rules and outcomes are not fully known in advance. It is the practice of orienting without premature closure, observing without immediate judgment, classifying without overclaiming, probing without forcing proof, withdrawing support to test dependence, naturalizing capacities, and integrating the result into the next movement.

The word *cultivation* is important. It avoids the harshness of control and the passivity of mere observation. A cultivator participates. They arrange conditions, test boundaries, remove obstacles, protect fragile growth, and respond to what appears. But they do not fully determine the living process. They do not pull the plant upward to make it grow faster. They learn the soil, the season, the root, the weather, and the timing. They create conditions in which the organism can express more of itself.

Cultivation of Becoming is the same stance toward irreducible systems. It is not endpoint forcing. It is not resignation. It is disciplined participation in unfolding form.

## 2. Why Cultivation Is Needed

In a reducible system, knowledge can often be planned in advance. The observer knows the variables, writes the model, predicts the result, and tests the prediction. If the prediction fails, the model is adjusted. This remains valuable and should not be discarded. But irreducible systems create a deeper problem. Their future states are not only difficult to calculate; their future meanings may be difficult to preclassify.

This is especially true for self-describing systems. A self-describing system does not merely occupy states. It forms, loses, and reorganizes the very objects through which it can be understood. A person may enter an experience thinking the relevant category is success or failure, only to discover that the real property is fear of exposure, desire for recognition, depleted attention, or an unformed capacity. A research system may search for a target region and find instead an evolved pocket. A social system may attempt to repair a policy and discover that the relevant object is a support-regime family rather than the authored target. A creative process may begin with an intended work and discover that the work is actually about the conditions that make expression possible.

In such systems, prediction-first inquiry becomes brittle because it treats the class as fixed before the system has had a chance to disclose its own structure. When the result does not match, the observer says the run failed, the action failed, the person failed, or the design failed. Sometimes that is correct. But sometimes the system expressed a real adjacent property, a lower-rung property, or a new class that was invisible to the original vocabulary.

Cultivation is needed because development depends on preserving those appearances without prematurely promoting them. A non-promoting result can still be true. A probe-supported capacity can still be meaningful. A boundary failure can still be the most important thing the system showed. The observer’s task is not to rescue every result into importance, but to avoid discarding what the system actually expressed.

This is also why the phenomenology of becoming matters. Experience is not just noise around the real process. It is evidence of regime. Anxiety, spaciousness, compulsion, curiosity, joy, resistance, fatigue, and relief are not automatically trustworthy interpretations, but they are signals that the system is in some geometry of relation to perturbation. Scarcity feels different from abundance. Completion-drive feels different from cultivation-drive. A bounded probe feels different from a threat. A supported expression feels different from a naturalized capacity.

To cultivate becoming is to learn to read these signals without being ruled by them. Experience is not command. It is not proof. It is regime evidence.

The *Language of Becoming* companion on reinforcement makes this distinction especially clear. It separates completion-drive from cultivation-drive. Completion-drive wants to close, resolve, finish, and protect coherence under pressure. It is not always wrong; under genuine scarcity or danger it may be appropriate. But when it becomes the default, every experience becomes something to get through. Cultivation-drive is different. It treats perturbation as possible nutrition. It gives the system enough space to observe before responding, enough confidence to let inputs enter, and enough surplus capacity to explore without immediately collapsing into defense.

Cultivation of Becoming begins from this recognition: not every perturbation is a threat, not every openness is wisdom, not every closure is pathology, and not every intervention is development. The question is always: what regime is this system in, and what movement is now possible without lying about what has appeared?

## 3. The Seven Movements

The method of cultivation can be described as seven movements: orient, observe, classify, probe, withdraw, naturalize, and integrate. These are not rigid stages. In practice they loop, overlap, and return. But they name the rhythm by which an embedded observer can participate in change without pretending that the rules are already known.

**Orient.** The process begins with direction, not certainty. An observer always arrives with some desire, concern, hypothesis, fear, or intention. There is no view from nowhere. The mistake is not having an intention; the mistake is confusing intention with knowledge of what the system must become. Orientation gives the first vector. It says: this is what I care about; this is the region of experience or inquiry I am entering; this is the current frame I can responsibly declare. But it keeps the frame provisional.

In scientific work, orientation may be an intended property or a hypothesis. In life, it may be a wish, a problem, a longing, or a question. In creative work, it may be an image of what the work might become. In each case, orientation is needed because without it the observer drifts. But orientation must remain open enough to be corrected by encounter.

**Observe.** Observation is the moment of contact. The system unfolds, and the observer resists the temptation to compress the result immediately into success, failure, threat, proof, or error. This is difficult because completion-drive wants fast classification. It wants to know what this means, whether we won, whether we lost, whether to continue, whether to stop. Cultivation asks for a pause before closure. What appeared? What changed? What did not change? What was expected but absent? What was unexpected but stable enough to notice?

Observation is not passivity. It is disciplined receptivity. In formal systems it means reading the emitted surfaces before writing the interpretation. In lived experience it means letting the event disclose its texture before turning it into a story.

**Classify.** After observation comes naming. The observer asks what class of event or property appeared. This is where Classification of Becoming enters. If the result does not match the predicted endpoint, it is not automatically discarded. It may belong to a lower rung. It may expose a split between properties that were previously conflated. It may reveal that a local null, control, or adjacent placement expresses the property more clearly than the intended target. It may require a bounded new class.

Classification is not promotion. To name a class is not to raise the claim ceiling. A system may express a support-regime family without expressing identity. It may show probe-exposed capacity without natural recurrence. It may show event telemetry without substrate consequence. Good classification protects these distinctions.

Classification is also not automatically progress. A label may be faithful but too local to guide development. It may name only the latest obstacle in the latest neighborhood. Cultivation therefore distinguishes:

```text
observation tag:
  records what happened locally

becoming class:
  creates reusable or generative leverage
```

Only the second should organize a cultivation arc. The first should remain in the activity history unless it exposes a broader boundary.

**Probe.** Once a property is classified, the observer can ask a bounded question. A probe is not an attempt to force the endpoint. It is a small intervention designed to reveal one missing boundary. If the system expressed score without gate, the probe asks whether gate can appear while score is preserved. If a split event appears without field consequence, the probe asks whether coupling can turn event into consequence. If calm appears only under perfect support, the probe asks how much support is necessary before calm collapses.

The probe must be small enough to remain a question. If it supplies the whole phenomenon, it may still teach something, but its result has a lower claim ceiling. The system has shown capacity under support, not natural expression. This distinction is central.

A probe should also be tied to the right object. If the classified object is only a local tag, the probe may become a repair of the last miss. If the classified object is reusable or generative, the probe can ask a more important question: what boundary or support function made this property possible?

**Withdraw.** After the probe, support must be weakened or removed. This is the movement that prevents intervention from masquerading as development. If a property appears only because the observer held it in place, then the property is not yet naturalized. Withdrawal asks what remains when the explicit support is reduced. Does the system keep the property? Does it collapse? Does it become broad and control-competitive? Does it localize? Does it become endogenous? Does it reveal that the probe was constructive rather than diagnostic?

Withdrawal is often the most honest movement in cultivation. It separates what the system can express under special conditions from what it can express through its own organization.

**Naturalize.** Naturalization is the developmental movement. It asks what endogenous conditions would allow a probe-supported property to become naturally available. If a person can be calm only when everything is quiet, naturalization asks how calm becomes possible under ordinary perturbation. If a system expresses polarity only under an explicit probe, naturalization asks what regime would let polarity arise without that probe. If a creative state appears only under crisis, naturalization asks how insight can become available without collapse.

Naturalization is not the same as repetition. Repeating a supported state may deepen dependence on the support. Naturalization requires identifying what the support provided and cultivating the internal or environmental conditions that let the system generate that function for itself.

This is where cultivation must avoid the proxy-localization trap. If support provided a function, the goal is not to optimize the nearest measurable proxy until it passes. The goal is to cultivate the generator condition for that function. In short:

```text
Do not cultivate the proxy.
Cultivate the function.
```

**Integrate.** Finally, the observer updates the activity history. What was learned? What class was added or refined? What support was necessary? What disappeared when the support was withdrawn? What next question is now better than the old one? Integration turns the episode into usable history. Without it, the system may repeat encounters without development. With it, the next orientation begins from a richer map.

This seven-movement loop is the practical form of cultivation. It does not promise certainty. It gives the observer a way to stay in contact with change without either forcing it or dissolving into it.

The Paper-30 correction adds a value gate after classification and a function gate before naturalization:

```text
classification value gate:
  did the class become reusable or generative,
  or is it only a local observation tag?

function gate:
  did the probe reveal a support function the system could learn to supply,
  or did it only create a local proxy to optimize?
```

Without these gates, the seven movements can become a loop of increasingly specific interventions. The process may look disciplined, but it is no longer cultivation. It is local repair.

## 4. Experience as Regime Evidence

A method for cultivating becoming cannot ignore experience. But it also cannot treat experience as self-validating. The crucial distinction is that experience is evidence of regime, not automatic truth about the world.

Fear may indicate danger. It may also indicate an old completion basin, a boundary that has become too rigid, a previous collapse being reactivated, or a system encountering a genuinely new perturbation before it has developed capacity. Joy may indicate surplus coherence. It may also be an escape from contact, a temporary expansion produced by support that will disappear under withdrawal, or a fragile abundance that needs protection before it can be trusted. Resistance may indicate a wise boundary or a brittle one. Desire may indicate growth, craving, leakage, or a path toward naturalization. Relief may indicate healthy closure or premature compression.

To cultivate becoming is to learn to ask what experience is evidence *of*. This prevents two opposite mistakes. The first mistake is distrust: treating lived signals as noise, irrationality, or subjective distortion. The second mistake is fusion: treating every felt signal as direct command. Cultivation requires a third stance. Experience is a report from the system’s geometry. It must be read, but it must also be interpreted.

The Language of Becoming vocabulary is useful here because it distinguishes regimes. Deep coherence feels different from shallow coherence. Fragmentation feels different from leakage. Bifurcation feels different from collapse. Abundance feels different from manic expansion because abundance has spare capacity and boundary integrity, while manic expansion may be broad but fragile. Completion-drive feels different from cultivation-drive because completion compresses time and seeks closure, while cultivation creates time and permits perturbation to disclose itself.

This vocabulary does not solve interpretation automatically. It gives the observer better questions. Is this urgency appropriate to current conditions, or is the system treating all perturbation as threat? Is this openness supported by real capacity, or is it boundary leakage? Is this calm naturalized, or does it depend on hidden support? Is this resistance protecting a coherent basin, or preventing a necessary probe?

Experience becomes especially important during transition. When a system moves from completion toward cultivation, it often feels worse before it feels better. Openness can initially feel unsafe because the system’s old geometry associated openness with danger. A bounded probe can feel like a threat if the system has little history of successful integration. Withdrawal can feel like abandonment if support has been confused with natural capacity. These feelings are not errors, but neither are they final verdicts. They are regime evidence during restructuring.

This is where patience becomes methodological, not merely moral. A system cannot instantly trust a regime it has not yet inhabited. It has to build the basin through small, bounded, repeated experiences. Cultivation respects this. It does not demand that the system feel safe before safety has been grown. It creates conditions where safety can become natural.

## 5. Probes, Withdrawal, and Naturalization

The probe layer is what keeps cultivation from becoming only reflection. Once a property has appeared or failed to appear, the observer can ask a small question of the system. But probes must be understood correctly. A probe is not proof. A probe is a question-answer pair.

The question may be: if I touch here, does the system respond? If I supply this missing coupling, does event become consequence? If I support this boundary, does the property localize? If I shift the support, does the effect remain source-specific? If I weaken the support, does the expression survive? If a control receives the same probe, does it express the same property?

The answer is not simply pass or fail. The answer says which boundary was crossed. Perhaps nothing changed except telemetry. Perhaps telemetry became field consequence but not geometry. Perhaps geometry appeared but was broad and control-competitive. Perhaps the source showed susceptibility, but recurrence did not occur. Perhaps the property appeared under the probe but vanished when the probe was withdrawn. Each of these answers is valuable because each places the result on the lowest valid rung.

This is one of the central disciplines of cultivation: never confuse the rung reached with the rung desired. If a probe creates the property, the result is not natural expression. If a probe reveals susceptibility, the result is not yet recurrence. If a property survives withdrawal, the claim grows stronger. If it fails under withdrawal, the support dependence has been located.

Different probes have different authority. A constructive probe supplies missing structure and asks what the system can express under support. A diagnostic probe lightly perturbs the system to reveal a susceptibility that was already near expression. A boundary probe tests whether one rung can become the next: event to consequence, consequence to geometry, geometry to source-specificity, source-specificity to recurrence. A withdrawal probe removes or weakens support to locate dependence. A naturalization probe asks whether the property can appear without explicit support.

The most dangerous mistake is to treat a constructive probe as proof of natural capacity. A person may become calm because the environment is tightly controlled. That does not mean calm has been naturalized. A social group may cooperate because an external authority holds the boundary. That does not mean cooperation is endogenous. A simulation may express a property because an explicit perturbation supplies the missing structure. That does not mean the regime naturally generates the property.

The probe-to-naturalization gap is therefore a central developmental problem. It can be stated simply:

Probe-exposed capacity is not natural regime expression.

This gap is not a failure. It is a map. The probe tells us what the system can do when support is present. Naturalization asks what conditions would let the system generate that support for itself. Development lives in that gap.

In human terms, naturalization is the difference between being brave because someone else is holding the room and having the internal geometry to act bravely under ordinary uncertainty. It is the difference between insight appearing only under crisis and insight becoming available in spacious attention. It is the difference between needing perfect silence to feel grounded and being able to return to ground after perturbation. In research terms, it is the difference between a result produced by a special intervention and a property expressed by the system’s native regime.

Cultivation seeks naturalization without despising support. Support is often necessary. A fragile system needs scaffold. A new capacity often appears first under artificial conditions. The mistake is not support. The mistake is forgetting that support was present.

## 6. Cooperation and Shared Becoming

Cultivation is not only individual. The Language of Becoming companion on reinforcement emphasizes that abundance is not merely personal surplus; it opens toward cooperation. When systems have enough coherence to spare, they can treat perturbation from others as information or nutrition rather than threat. They can resonate, exchange, and deepen without immediately collapsing into competition or defense.

This matters because many forms of becoming are relational. A capacity may not naturalize inside one isolated system. It may require a field, a partner, a community, a shared rhythm, or an ecology. Cooperation is not simply agreement or negotiated benefit. It can be a geometric relation in which two or more systems deepen each other’s basins by contact.

But cooperation also requires the same disciplines as individual cultivation. The relation must not be overclaimed. Shared excitement is not yet shared coherence. Mutual dependence is not yet mutual cultivation. Enmeshment is not intimacy. A group that functions only under a strong external leader has not necessarily naturalized cooperation. A social system that appears abundant under hidden extraction is not abundant in the stronger sense.

The same seven movements apply. A group or relationship or research community must orient without pretending to know its final form. It must observe what it actually expresses. It must classify its present regime: cooperation, competition, completion, leakage, support-dependence, shared basin formation, or local-null equivalence. It must use probes as questions: can this boundary hold? can this role rotate? can this capacity survive without one person carrying it? can the group metabolize disagreement? Then it must withdraw supports carefully and ask what remains.

Cultivation in shared systems requires special attention to support and naturalization. A capacity may be real but not evenly distributed. One person may supply the calm, another the direction, another the permeability, another the integration. The group may function well because these supports are present, but the system has not naturalized the capacity until it can reproduce the relevant property through its own structure. This does not mean every member must become identical. Naturalization in a group may mean distributed reliability rather than uniform possession.

This gives a different view of cooperation. Cooperation is not only a decision to be nice or fair. It is the cultivation of shared conditions in which perturbations deepen rather than deplete, differences remain distinguishable without becoming threats, and capacities that first appear through one support can gradually become available to the field.

## 7. Twelve Principles for Cultivating Becoming

The method can be summarized as twelve principles. These principles are not commandments; they are reminders of how to remain in contact with irreducible change without forcing it into premature closure.

**1. Do not begin with closure.** Begin with orientation. Know what you care about, but do not pretend that your first endpoint is the final class of the event. Intention is useful; certainty too early is dangerous.

**2. Let the event disclose its class before judging it.** A result may not be success or failure yet. It may be an adjacent property, a lower-rung expression, a support-dependence, a control-competitive effect, or a new class that needs careful naming.

**3. Treat experience as evidence of regime.** Feelings are not commands and not noise. They are reports from the system’s geometry. Ask what regime they indicate before acting on them or dismissing them.

**4. Use probes as questions, not proof.** A bounded intervention asks what boundary is live. It does not prove that the target phenomenon naturally exists. Always ask what the probe supplied.

**5. Preserve what appears.** Do not discard non-promoting results if they express a stable or informative property. A failed endpoint can become a map of the next transition.

**6. Seek naturalization.** If a property appears under support, ask what conditions would allow it to appear without that explicit support. Development is the movement from supported possibility toward native availability.

**7. Track activity history.** Understanding comes from how the present became present. Record the sequence of orientations, observations, classifications, probes, withdrawals, demotions, and integrations.

**8. Prefer cultivation over force.** Forcing may produce the endpoint, but it may also produce dependence, distortion, or collapse. Cultivation creates conditions where the richer property can become less forced.

**9. Keep claim ceilings.** Naming a property does not promote it to identity, learning, truth, agency, or destiny. The lowest valid rung is the most honest one.

**10. Re-enter with a better question.** Integration is not the end. It is preparation for a more accurate encounter. Prediction returns after classification, as a local question shaped by what the system actually expressed.

**11. Promote only reusable classes.** A classification can be accurate and still remain local. Do not organize development around every label. Promote a class only when it travels beyond the immediate setup, creates a new question, or preserves a material distinction that future inquiry can reuse.

**12. Cultivate the function, not the proxy.** A proxy is useful only while it remains a sign of the system supplying a missing function. When the proxy becomes the target, cultivation has become local optimization. Return to the function the system must learn to generate.

## 8. Boundary and Conclusion

Cultivation of Becoming should not be mistaken for a universal formula. It does not make the unknown safe. It does not guarantee growth. It does not remove the need for prediction, discipline, boundary, or judgment. It also does not say that every event contains hidden wisdom. Some events are simply damaging. Some systems are too depleted for exploration. Some probes are too invasive. Some supports create dependence rather than development. Some closures are appropriate and necessary.

The method is useful precisely because it keeps these distinctions alive. It allows the observer to act without pretending to control the whole system. It allows openness without dissolving into vagueness. It allows support without confusing support for natural expression. It allows experience to matter without treating experience as unquestionable truth.

The earlier Language of Becoming papers described the regimes of coherence and the positive possibility of abundance. Classification of Becoming added an epistemic method for discovering what a system actually expressed when the expected class was not reached. Bounded Interrogative Probes added an action method for asking small questions and locating missing boundaries. Naturalization of Becoming added the developmental standard: a probed capacity is not native until the system can generate the relevant support function for itself. Cultivation of Becoming brings them together as a practice of development.

Its shortest formulation is this:

Classification names what has appeared.  
Probing asks what boundary is missing.  
Naturalization asks whether the system can generate that boundary for itself.  
Cultivation develops the conditions under which the richer property can become natural without reducing development to proxy optimization.

To cultivate becoming is to live and inquire as an embedded observer in a world that does not reveal all its rules in advance. It is to approach the unknown without reducing it immediately to fear, failure, or proof. It is to notice what appears, name it carefully, ask bounded questions, withdraw artificial support, and integrate the result into the next encounter.

It is not control of becoming.

It is participation in becoming with enough discipline that the participation can teach.
